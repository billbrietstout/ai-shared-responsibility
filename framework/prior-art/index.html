How established enterprise architectures support the five SRF layers

The SRF compares well with the architecture and security frameworks people already use. This page shows where each one lines up with L1 to L5, where the SRF goes further, and how each source was checked.

← Back to the framework

Introduction

The CoSAI Shared Responsibility Framework (SRF) v1.0 sorts an AI system into five layers: L1 AI Business & Usage; L2 AI Information; L3 AI Application; L4 AI Platform; L5 AI Model Provider.

The SRF calls these “Enterprise Architecture layers” that “provide solution dependencies.” It also says “Specific Security & Governance requirements cascade from the Business layer to the supporting layers” [1]. That is a claim about architecture, so it makes sense to compare it with the architecture and security frameworks people already use.

This page looks at seven: TOGAF and ArchiMate, OBASHI, the Zachman Framework, NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0, NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) 1.0, ISO/IEC 22989 and 42001, and the cloud shared-responsibility models from AWS and Microsoft. Each one supports part of the SRF. The first four layers line up with domains these frameworks have used for decades. The ownership model, one accountable party per component, is consistent with how OBASHI, NIST and the cloud providers assign responsibility. L5 AI Model Provider is where the SRF extends the older frameworks, which treat an outside model as one more supplier. Some AI frameworks list their parts in a different order, and the SRF complements them by naming who is accountable at each layer.

This is an independent analysis on an independent site. It is not affiliated with or endorsed by OASIS or CoSAI. Claims marked “secondary” rest on a summary, not the official text.

Business, data, application, technology

TOGAF, The Open Group’s enterprise architecture standard, names four core architecture domains: business, data, application and technology [6]. Those map to L1, L2, L3 and L4, so the SRF’s first four layers follow a structure enterprise architects already know. TOGAF’s text is behind a sign-in, so the TOGAF points here rest on search excerpts, a secondary source.

TOGAF also separates architecture building blocks, which are logical and vendor-neutral, from solution building blocks, which are the products that fill them [6, secondary]. That pair supports the SRF operating models. An “LLM gateway” is the component. Who owns the product behind it depends on whether the customer uses AI-SaaS, AI-PaaS, Agent-PaaS or IaaS. The component stays in the same layer, and only its owner changes.

ArchiMate, TOGAF’s modeling language, supports the SRF with typed links between components. ArchiMate 3.2 names relationship types such as composition, aggregation, assignment, realization, serving, access, flow, triggering and influence [5]. The Open Group released ArchiMate 4 in April 2026 [2]. Those type names appear in the ArchiMate 4 table of contents, but the v4 definitions are behind a sign-in, so the 3.2 wording is used here.

Typed links strengthen the SRF’s cascade idea. The SRF says requirements cascade down, and typed links also show how failures travel up and sideways. When an L5 model changes, it serves an L4 gateway, which serves an L3 agent. That agent realizes an L1 business process. The L1 owner is affected even though no business rule changed. With typed links, “cascade” has a defined meaning.

This analysis suggests two working rules:

  • L3 realizes L1 logic. When an agent at L3 runs a business process owned at L1, record that link with the logic version and the L1 owner’s approval. This is both a design pattern and a control.
  • A control lowers impact. It does not stop a cascade. A component with a working control is marked impacted instead of failed, and the cascade keeps going past it.

OBASHI brings Ownership as a view

OBASHI, a method for mapping business and IT assets, puts an Ownership layer above Business Process, Application, System, Hardware and Infrastructure [7, secondary]. That is consistent with the SRF principle of “exactly one accountable party per component” [1]. OBASHI’s lower three layers fit inside L4.

OBASHI’s layers should not become SRF layers, because they would add layers with no accountable persona. Instead, OBASHI’s ownership idea can be added as an overlay. The overlay shows the owner on every component, so a component with no owner, or with two, stands out. OBASHI’s Dataflow Analysis View also points to a data flow view across layers, which the SRF could add.

Zachman as a completeness check

The Zachman Framework is a 6-by-6 schema. Its columns are the questions What, How, When, Who, Where and Why. Its rows are stages that go from Identification to Instantiation. Zachman describes it as an ontology and says it is not a methodology [8].

Zachman’s columns make a good completeness check for the SRF. Ask each question of each SRF layer. In this analysis, the SRF answers Who well (personas) and Why at L1 (obligations). It answers What at L2 and How at L3. Where, such as data residency and jurisdiction, and When, such as change windows and model version timing, are places the SRF could extend its coverage. Zachman’s rows do not map to SRF layers, because rows are levels of abstraction and SRF layers are not. L1 is no more abstract than L4. Each layer has its own concrete components and owners.

Security and AI risk frameworks

NIST CSF 2.0 organizes outcomes into six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover [9]. These functions apply at every layer. Govern supports the SRF ownership model most directly. Its Roles, Responsibilities, and Authorities category (GV.RR) calls for cybersecurity roles and responsibilities “to foster accountability,” and GV.SC-02 calls for roles and responsibilities for “suppliers, customers, and partners” to be “established, communicated, and coordinated internally and externally” [9]. The two fit together. CSF says what outcomes to reach, and the SRF says who is accountable for reaching them at each layer.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 describes AI system dimensions: Application Context, Data and Input, AI Model, and Task and Output, with People & Planet at the center [10]. Application Context fits L1, and Data and Input fits L2. AI Model fits L5, with L4 serving it, and Task and Output fits L3. The AI RMF also supports the ownership model. GOVERN 2.1 asks that “roles and responsibilities and lines of communication related to mapping, measuring, and managing AI risks are documented and are clear,” and GOVERN 6 covers AI risks from “third-party software and data and other supply chain issues” [10]. That includes a model obtained from an outside AI Model Provider. The NIST AI RMF is being revised, so this mapping will need a recheck.

ISO/IEC 42001 is a management system standard, so it has no layers. SRF v1.0 says its accountability structure “can satisfy ISO 42001 Clause 5.3 requirements,” which cover roles, responsibilities and authorities [1, 11]. ISO/IEC 22989 defines AI stakeholder roles: AI provider, AI producer, AI customer, AI partner, AI subject and relevant authorities. SRF v1.0 maps each of its personas to one of these roles, so the SRF builds on ISO’s role vocabulary [1, 12].

Cloud shared-responsibility models

The SRF borrows its basic idea from cloud models. AWS describes “Security of the Cloud” for the provider and “Security in the Cloud” for the customer, with the dividing point moving by service type [13]. SRF v1.0 applies the same idea: responsibility is shared “at the boundary of what is being provided” [1].

Microsoft’s AI shared responsibility model is the closest match. It describes three layers, AI platform, AI application and AI usage, and responsibility shifts across SaaS, PaaS and IaaS [14]. Those line up with L4, L3 and L1. Microsoft places the model, training data and weights in the platform layer and has no separate data layer. The SRF extends this by giving data (L2) and the AI Model Provider (L5) their own layers and owners. Microsoft also lists its layers from the platform up, the reverse of the SRF’s order.

Microsoft’s AI agent shared responsibility model supports the SRF’s Agent-PaaS operating model [15]. It describes a “PaaS agent” built on a managed agent platform. Microsoft provides the runtime, model hosting and platform safety controls. The customer owns the agent’s instructions, tool selection and permissions, orchestration logic, memory design, identity and authorization. In this reading, that matches the idea behind Agent-PaaS: the agent platform has one owner, and the agent built on it has another.

Where the SRF extends or complements these frameworks

L5 AI Model Provider extends the older frameworks. TOGAF, ArchiMate, OBASHI and Zachman treat an outside model as one more supplier building block. The SRF makes it a layer because the AI Model Provider controls training data, model development and provenance, which a customer cannot inspect or fix. That is a choice the SRF makes for accountability reasons. NIST points the same way: CSF 2.0 (GV.SC) and the AI RMF (GOVERN 6) both treat supplier risk as something to govern [9, 10].

NIST AI RMF uses a different order, and the SRF complements it. Its dimensions go Application Context, Data and Input, AI Model, then Task and Output [10]. In SRF terms, that is L1, L2, L5, then L3. NIST orders things by how an AI system works: context, data in, model, output. The SRF orders them by dependency and accountability, so the crosswalk is not a straight line. The two work together: the AI RMF describes how the system works, and the SRF adds who is accountable at each step. NIST’s People & Planet dimension has no SRF layer or persona.

ISO/IEC 22989 roles complement SRF layers. Roles describe who someone is in a relationship. Layers describe where a component sits. The AI partner role shows up at L2 (data provider), at L4 (platform and infrastructure provider) and for tool providers. An L4 provider can also be an AI provider to its own customers. One party can hold components in several layers, and SRF v1.0 maps its personas to ISO roles, so the two work together [1, 12; ISO roles as reported in SRF v1.0]. The AI subject role, the people affected, has no SRF persona. L1 threat models should list affected people as stakeholders.

ArchiMate 4 and Zachman support reading SRF layers as areas of accountability, not a strict stack. ArchiMate 4 replaced “layers” with “domains.” Marc Lankhorst of Bizzdesign writes that reading the old “layer cake” as a strict hierarchy, with business served by applications served by infrastructure, was a misinterpretation [3, secondary]. Zachman says its framework implies nothing about whether you work top-down or bottom-up [8]. SRF layers are areas of accountability, and dependencies between components form a graph, not a ladder.

CSF 2.0 functions work on a different axis, which makes them easy to combine with the SRF. Its six functions are activities, not places in a system, so each function applies in every layer.
