Critical infrastructure sectors #sectors

The profile applies across the 16 CISA-defined CI sectors: Chemical; Commercial Facilities; Communications; Critical Manufacturing; Dams; Defense Industrial Base; Emergency Services; Energy; Financial Services; Food and Agriculture; Government Services and Facilities; Healthcare and Public Health; Information Technology; Nuclear Reactors, Materials, and Waste; Transportation Systems; Water and Wastewater Systems.

The Practices #practices-heading

Twelve practices, each with tasks and illustrative implementations. The order does not imply priority; sectors and organizations set their own must-haves. Each node carries its AI RMF mappings, references, and — where applicable — pointers to the machine-readable automation standards (OSCAL, SCAP, SBOM) that let the control be expressed and checked by tools.

Practice 1. Establish requirements for success #practice-1

Define performance, safety, and security benchmarks that establish the baseline for existing and proposed systems to ensure AI-driven systems align with the organization's mission and operational resilience goals while achieving desired improvements. This ensures that the adoption of AI delivers measurable operational value rather than purely technological optimization. These requirements provide a verifiable framework for system acceptance and ongoing oversight, supporting clear accountability and audit-readiness throughout the asset lifecycle. This practice focuses on ensuring that the adoption of AI provides measurable improvements. It is related to Practice 2: Define AI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service expectations, which ensures that any trade-offs still result in acceptable overall levels of robustness, resilience, and quality of service.

1.3.1  Define TEVV scope and requirements per AI system #impl-1-3-1

For AI systems deployed in safety-critical CI operational roles, define TEVV scope requirements that are proportionate to consequence severity of errors identified during risk assessments within Practice 3: Define risks, policies, and oversight for automated AI and agentic behavior and explicitly account for CI-specific failure modes, including model drift, adversarial manipulation, and degraded-mode operation. Establish pass/fail criteria for each lifecycle including pre-deployment, post-incident, and periodic drift or degradation checks. Test and Evaluation (T&E) planning documentation, of the kind already used for many traditional CI systems to specify what will be tested, under what conditions, and against what success criteria before testing begins, provides a useful model for defining these requirements up front, rather than determining them ad hoc during testing. Where an AI system cannot be shown to satisfy reliability requirements for autonomous operation, constrain it to a mode with increased human oversight appropriate to the consequence severity of the application, until TEVV evidence supports a higher level of autonomy. Ensure that this temporary oversight mode does not exceed the documented cognitive baseline of the available operators, as high algorithmic error rates rapidly saturate human triage capacity. This may require use to be limited to what can be supported by available human oversight capacity as discussed in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation. To prevent accidental operational outages caused by evaluation overhead, procurement and engineering policies should explicitly prohibit high-load active adversarial testing, automated input fuzzing, or dense red-teaming (testing a system by deliberately seeking to compromise it as an attacker would) vectors on live, production model paths. All destructive profiling or exploratory boundary testing should be restricted to isolated staging environments, offline hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) test rigs, or high-fidelity digital twins (detailed virtual replicas of physical equipment or processes that are kept up to date with real data).

1.3.2  Categorization of Testing Environments #impl-1-3-2

TEVV activities across the system lifecycle should be conducted within a validated environment tailored to the operational nature of the underlying infrastructure: - For critical applications where service delivery, safety constraints, or physical control loops cannot tolerate production path disruption or performance overhead, adhere to the production path testing prohibitions defined in Implementation 1.3.1: Define TEVV scope and requirements per AI.... Testing, including active verification, automated input fuzzing, and adversarial red-teaming should be restricted to non-production systems such as staging mirrors, hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) test rigs, simulators, or high-fidelity digital twins. - For critical applications that possess regular, planned downtime or can sustain temporary outages without safety impacts (e.g., administrative or transactional batch processors, data summarization tools, or off-peak fraud queues), it may be safe to test directly on the active production system strictly during authorized maintenance or scheduled outage windows. Where feasible, this may be preferable to testing on simulators or staging systems, whose behavior may unintentionally differ from the live system.

2.1  Establish AI usage thresholds and rate control mechanisms #task-2-1

Define and implement technical constraints on the volume, frequency, and scope of AI-driven actions, communications, and resource usage, particularly where there are physical and safety limitations or constrained resources. This task reduces the potential for rapid, automated system instability and resource bottlenecks by ensuring that AI interactions remain within predefined operational tolerances and do not overwhelm upstream or downstream legacy systems.

2.1.1  Implement baseline rate limiting #impl-2-1-1

Rate limit the various resources and actions that an AI system can take, including consuming AI generation resources (often measured in tokens), making configuration change requests, taking control actions, and requesting attention from humans and other systems. This reduces the likelihood that automated activity will saturate critical networks or cause instability in other systems, particularly legacy systems that may not be designed for the rapid changes in resource usage or control that can result from some AI systems. It may also highlight signs of adversarial action or interference. Ensure that when rate limits are hit, the system's overall behavior falls back to a safe state and that operators are notified via non-disruptive alerts where appropriate (Tasks 3.4: Monitor for anomalous AI behavior and 4.2: Ensure that personnel are able to gain and maintain situational awareness). This may be particularly important where AI is used to improve efficiency in normal times, resulting in either more systems utilizing the same allocated resources or the same systems being allocated fewer resources. Such rate limits act as a first line of defense against overload, particularly in unusual or unforeseen situations where the efficiency gains may not hold. To safeguard operator cognitive capacity and minimize alert fatigue, notification delivery metrics should follow the non-compulsory exception batching, aggregation, and soft-throttling strategies outlined in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation.

2.1.2  Develop process-informed guardrails that dynamically determine the appropriate rate at which AI systems should be utilizing resources or taking actions. For example: #impl-2-1-2

- An AI-based optimization system may use more tokens during quiet periods. - Rapid changes to power grid load distribution should be avoided when the grid is running close to capacity. These guardrails should be deterministic and based on the application's needs. Such guardrails give the AI system flexibility to use additional resources or take additional actions when the application requires it, such as during times of rapid change or heightened activity, while maintaining tight control during quieter times.

2.5  Verify and Ensure Policy Robustness Against Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance #task-2-5

Ensure that learning systems that optimize their behavior, such as reinforcement learning systems, do not learn to rely on undocumented or out-of-specification behavior of their target system. 2.5.1  Train Across the Full Operating Range #impl-2-5-1

AI systems that are trained in a very narrow part of the operating range can become dependent on parameters remaining within the range observed in training, even if operational conditions can legitimately deviate from this. To counter this, when training optimization models, particularly via simulations or digital twins, intentionally randomize and cycle environmental parameters to the absolute limits allowed (e.g., maximum and minimum rated latencies, tolerances, thermal thresholds, new vs worn equipment and sensors, different weather conditions, etc.). When training on physical hardware, ensure that training and testing is performed on a representative set of functionally equivalent equipment that the system is likely to encounter in deployment, including equipment at different levels of wear and subject to different environmental conditions. For AI systems that can take symbolic background knowledge, ensure that the formal specifications of component systems are appropriately encoded into the AI system so that the learning process avoids relying on capabilities outside of those specifications. This prevents the AI from building a policy that breaks when, for instance, a component operates at a different but legally compliant point within its spec sheet.

3.2.2  Define deterministic, operational constraints that the AI system should remain within #impl-3-2-2

Define the boundary that the AI system should remain within for safe operation. These might be in- and out-of-range values, semantic and behavioral constraints, rate-of-change violations, and deterministic logical constraints that should be respected regardless of the AI system's confidence level. These deterministic constraints should be defined and enforced independent of the AI system's internal logic (e.g., at the physical process boundary). These constraints should reflect the most restrictive combination of the organization's, equipment's, application's, and the AI system's safe operating limits (including the limits of the AI system's training). Constraints should apply uniformly to all automated command sources at the physical process boundary, including AI recommendations, automated agents, remote operator sessions, and any other upstream system. Authentication and physical safety are separate concerns: confirming who or what sent a command (addressed in Practice 5: Implement identity and access management (IdAM) for AI agents...) does not guarantee that the command itself is safe to execute. An authenticated source can still issue a damaging command through error, misconfiguration, or compromise. Absent an explicit, authorized override, such safety wrappers should intercept physically unsafe commands regardless of their source. Where such constraints must be lifted, e.g., to facilitate an experienced human engineer remotely dealing with a novel situation, such constraints should be explicitly overridden in accordance with emergency safety protocols. The same principle of independently-enforced constraints applies beyond physical process control: for AI systems that generate or filter information rather than direct commands, the "constraint" may be one of required completeness or fidelity, independently enforced regardless of the AI system's own judgment about what is relevant. For example: - An attacker gaining access to a plant human-machine interface (HMI) could issue setpoint commands indistinguishable from legitimate AI recommendations. - A valve controlled by an AI system might have constraints that the AI system may not have properly learned, such as not being opened and closed in quick succession, and not being opened at the same time as another valve. - A condition report summarization tool should be constrained to include information about every asset, even if the AI system judges a given condition not to be of concern. - An AI system routing high-volume transactions or processing patient records should be bounded by deterministic validation rules that block anomalous account or resource allocations (e.g., assigning an atomic resource to two entities), prevent unauthorized data aggregation, or flag out-of-bounds risk parameters prior to downstream operational execution.

3.3.2  Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds #impl-3-3-2

Establish quantitative monitoring and thresholds against which the AI system is continuously or periodically evaluated for how far the real-world operating environment, data inputs, task, and system context can shift from the historic conditions on which the system was trained before mitigations are put in place, defining the specific thresholds that determine when the periodic evaluations in Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations or the continuous monitoring in Task 3.4: Monitor for anomalous AI behavior should trigger a fail-safe response. Drift thresholds should be reviewed and recalibrated at a defined interval or whenever a significant change in the operational environment occurs. This implementation mitigates hazard risk associated with silent AI performance degradation. 3.5  Define and control risks to adjacent and related systems #task-3-5

Define and control risks to other systems that may be affected by a failure in this system, including: - Systems that are intentionally interdependent with this system, where errors travel along functional, data, or operational dependencies ("cascading failures"). - Systems that may be unrelated except for shared adjacency in a physical, logical, network, resource, supply chain, or other sense ("splash damage" or "blast radius").

3.5.1  Map direct functional and algorithmic dependencies #impl-3-5-1

Trace and document downstream logical, algorithmic, and operational paths of AI outputs to identify how errors, whether in control outputs, direct executable code, or human-mediated summaries, could trigger a chain reaction through connected assets. These dependencies often extend beyond traditional IT/OT/ICS boundaries; for example, an AI agent defending an IT network might erroneously disable authentication traffic, physically locking personnel out of a facility. To fully capture these risks, organizations should conduct a high-value asset (HVA) assessment that extends outward to map functional dependencies on upstream suppliers and downstream customers, ensuring automated errors do not propagate along the extended supply chain.

3.5.2  Identify indirect shared infrastructure and collateral damage exposure #impl-3-5-2

Audit the operational environment to identify functionally independent systems that are vulnerable to collateral damage from an AI failure. Evaluation criteria should include cross-cutting environmental and infrastructure commonalities, such as: - Physical Proximity: Independent physical assets co-located in the same facility or enclosure, or that share the same power, cooling, or other physical resources. - Network Topography: Systems sharing the same network switches, backbones, or uplink paths. - Logical Substrates: Unrelated applications sharing the same hypervisor, underlying cloud tenant, base operating system image, or database instance. This mapping should also consider whether a single event or action could affect multiple identified safeguards at once, rather than assuming redundant controls fail independently. This asset assessment should, where possible, reach outward past the facility's immediate perimeter to evaluate shared municipal substrates, such as the regional electrical grid, water networks, or telecommunication backbones, where a localized AI failure or resource-saturation event could cause regional collateral damage. Where direct technical visibility or telemetry past the facility's perimeter is unavailable, organizations should utilize qualified engineering approximations and best-available sector intelligence to estimate these external impacts. These estimations must be explicitly documented as bounded technical assumptions to ensure transparency regarding data limitations, preventing the assessment from stalling due to incomplete external information. The task of mapping internal dependencies is common to Implementation 7.6.1: Internal Dependency Mapping (Orphan System Prevention). Maintaining this map can reduce the effort needed when this system is later decommissioned.

3.6.2  Scheduled "Manual Control" Drills #impl-3-6-2

The failure could include a loss of availability or a combination of other failures as identified in 3.1: Identify risk tradeoffs. These drills should be designed to achieve two primary outcomes: - Technical Validation: Verify that fallback deterministic control systems, analog backups, and safety interlocks successfully assume the operational load without data loss or safety breaches. - Human Readiness: Force human operators to actively manage operations via legacy or manual methods, preserving the critical muscle memory, tactical domain skills, and situational awareness required to safely navigate a real-world, unannounced AI outage. 4.4.1  Recognizing novel AI-driven alert floods in unfamiliar domains #impl-4-4-1

Domains with no prior experience managing alarm floods may not recognize the pattern when AI introduces it, or know that established mitigation techniques exist. For example, a fraud-detection system entering a confused state can flood a review queue with false positives; a summarization tool can generate excessive content when it should stay silent; a hospital's AI-assisted clinical decision support can generate alert volumes comparable to those long documented in traditional medical device alarm fatigue, but without the institutional awareness built up around that older problem. Measures to reduce this risk range from training personnel to recognize alert-flood patterns as they emerge in the tool they're using and developing procedures for manual prioritization, to automated triage and consolidation of output (e.g., rate-limiting generated content, grouping duplicate flags).

4.4.2  Adapting legacy alarm management for AI's non-deterministic failure modes #impl-4-4-2

Domains with mature, trusted alarm-management practices may find those practices don't transfer cleanly to some AI systems. Traditional mitigations often assume alarms correspond to discrete, mechanical fault states (e.g., a stuck valve or tripped breaker). 4.4.3  Managing correlated multi-system attention surges #impl-4-4-3

A single external event can trigger a simultaneous surge of attention requests across many AI-controlled systems at once, overwhelming a limited pool of human operators even if each individual system's alert volume is otherwise manageable. In cases where the adoption of AI has coincided with a reduction in staffing levels, this can result in a lack of on-call or appropriately trained operators to address sudden issues. For example, a widespread power failure or loss of traffic signals can cause an entire autonomous vehicle fleet to simultaneously request remote operator attention, exceeding the capacity of the remote operations center. Measures to address this risk include: - Identifying which AI-driven systems share a human supervision pool, and estimating what external events could cause them to request attention simultaneously. - Design and resource supervision capacity and escalation procedures explicitly for correlated worst-case surges, not just average or independent per-system load. - Developing automated alert triaging mechanisms so that limited operator attention is not spent sorting through large quantities of alerts or addressing low priority issues.

4.4.4  Applying consolidation and low-cognitive-load design techniques #impl-4-4-4

Once alarm flood risks are identified, apply techniques that reduce the rate and cognitive cost of notifications without hiding genuinely urgent, isolated signals. Examples of measures to consider include: - Implementing basic consolidation, such as grouping repeated identical alerts into a single notification. - Applying user-interface design choices that reduce distraction (often referred to as calm-technology or mixed-initiative interfaces) such as exception batching, trend dashboards, dynamic deadbands, time-delay hysteresis, matched to the specific application, supervision model, and the range of personnel familiarity likely to encounter these notifications, from regular operators to occasional external responders. Note that there is a trade-off between many of these measures. For example, aggressive batching reduces fatigue but risks delaying a genuinely urgent, isolated signal.

4.5  Define operational regimes for human-on-the-loop and human-out-of-the-loop operation #task-4-5

Identify operational contexts where real-time, manual human-in-the-loop (HITL) oversight is physically or technically infeasible due to tight system time constants (such as stability anomalies operating in milliseconds, real-time industrial control loops, or machine-speed cyber defense events). Human oversight degrades into a purely nominal or superficial check if the required cognitive review and validation latency exceeds the system's physical action time, particularly in situations where fatigue or stress are factors. For these regimes, formally define the boundaries where human-on-the-loop (supervisory oversight with delayed intervention capability) or human-out-of-the-loop (autonomous execution with retrospective post-hoc review) should substitute for active human control, and specify the deterministic automated protections required to support them. This task prevents dangerous operational dependencies on human reaction times during rapid, cascading physical anomalies. It ensures that when humans cannot act as an immediate safety-net, automated boundaries step in to preserve system stability.

5.1  Require identities and authentication for AI entities #task-5-1

Inventory AI entities and define authentication requirements that uniquely identify each AI entity, including those that are ephemeral, and ensure those identities are distinct across system boundaries, operational roles, and multi-agent deployment. In this task, an AI entity refers to an invocation of an AI agent or tool that performs atomic tasks, at a suitable level of granularity, and whose behavior should be meaningfully traced and disambiguated from the behavior of other invocations of the same or similar AI agents and tools. If the AI entity is compromised or otherwise found to be behaving in an undesired manner, the level of granularity should be sufficient to identify and revoke access while minimizing the effect on other AI entities. The appropriate level of granularity will vary depending on variables that include: - Available resources (finance, time, expertise). - Expected threat level. - Expected impact. - Other mitigating controls. - Scale. - Vendor co-operation. - Application complexity. Organizations should implement this task flexibly, to allow for changes in granularity as these variables change. Where this is not possible, such as with legacy or compute-constrained OT devices, alternative controls may be necessary and enforced at an alternative point in the system.

5.1.1  Audit for and replace shared credentials, service accounts, secrets, passwords, and API keys #impl-5-1-1

Technically enforce distinct, cryptographically verifiable non-human identities (NHIs - credentials designed to be issued to software/AI agents rather than people) for every autonomous sub-agent, micro-service hook, or transient tool invocation, particularly in AI systems built from multiple independent parts that each act and access resources on their own, including agentic AI systems. Where technically feasible, prohibit the reuse of a single, shared enterprise API key or master service account across multiple independent agent nodes. For compute-constrained or legacy assets where unique cryptographic identities cannot be natively generated, enforce compensating controls at an alternative point in the system. Relying on shared accounts or group credentials heavily degrades situational awareness and complicates post-incident forensics because it obscures the true root cause of an action. In a complex or chained multi-agent system, being able to trace actions back to a unique, non-shared programmatic identity is essential for granular attribution and containment. Appropriately implementing granular NHIs ensures that if an individual model node experiences behavioral drift, runtime errors, or prompt compromise, its specific logical tokens can be isolated and revoked by the Security Operations Center (SOC) without invalidating the wider multi-tiered asset application architecture.

5.3  Enforce principles of least agency and least privilege for AI entities #task-5-3

Least agency requires that the system's capability to initiate independent actions or invoke external operational tools is minimized to only what is strictly necessary for its defined function. This is distinct from least privilege which restricts what it is allowed to do should access be attempted. This helps to ensure that systems are restricted from being granted permanent access to control systems, configuration interfaces, or operational tools beyond the active operational need, and that they avoid accessing these unnecessarily even when access exists. Require that the access permissions defined under Task 5.2: Determine and enforce specific access requirements are minimized to only what is strictly necessary for the AI entity's defined function. Controls based on those applied to human users are a suitable point to build from, with additional controls put in place to account for AI agents and systems that may: - Have limited needs and whose access should be further compartmentalized. - Be influenced by multiple people beyond those notionally responsible. - Interact with other AI agents and systems, resulting in potential emergent behavior. - Operate at speeds beyond those expected of humans. - May not be able to provide meaningful explanations for their actions.

8.1  Identify scenarios requiring deterministic root cause analysis and incident reconstruction #task-8-1

Define the specific applications, operational thresholds, and safety-critical triggers that mandate a comprehensive, deterministic reconstruction of an AI-driven failure, for both post-event investigation as well as to provide situational awareness for immediate-term incident response. This task ensures that applications requiring forensic root cause analysis are performed by AI systems that support such investigations. It also ensures that forensic resources are prioritized for high-impact events and that the organization meets the evidentiary standards required for regulatory and safety oversight.

8.1.1  Evaluate AI applications #impl-8-1-1

Evaluate AI applications to identify the extent of root cause analysis required to satisfy risk management and regulatory requirements. This evaluation should explicitly screen for process paths where deterministic forensic reconstruction is a safety or operational requirement. Where a proposed AI system cannot satisfy these specific analytical demands, organizations should either substitute systems inherently capable of true root cause analysis, demonstrably close the gap via validated compensating controls, or implement deterministic boundaries to ensure execution hands over to manual or legacy systems capable of the required analysis.

8.1.2  Identify AI systems that may not satisfy root cause analysis requirements #impl-8-1-2

Identify specific AI system classes that carry inherent forensic gaps that cannot be fully closed by techniques like runtime telemetry. These include large language models with non-deterministic sampling, agentic systems with ephemeral context, and remote-hosted inference services without preserved telemetry. In addition, some architectures and systems rely on inherent operational randomness that may not be able to be meaningfully replayed. Post-hoc explainability tools such as those based on SHAP and LIME should not be treated as forensic proof of how or why an incident occurred. Such techniques test a system with altered inputs and infer, after the fact, one hypothesis for what likely drove the system's output, without consideration for the system's actual decision making process. Where such systems are identified, technical managers should cross-reference with Task 8.2: Identify additional controls that may be required in scenarios where deterministic root cause analysis is not possible to implement mandatory safety margins or alternative validation frameworks.

12.1  Establish Governance and Accountability for AI-Generated Execution Artifacts #task-12-1

Define the policy framework and responsibility structures for the creation, classification, and authorization of AI-generated logic. This task ensures that every machine-authored artifact---from code to procedural summaries---is assigned a risk tier and a clear human owner responsible for its final approval and operational deployment.

12.1.1  Artifact Risk Classification #impl-12-1-1

Categorize AI-generated logic and outputs (artifacts) based on their potential for harm if incorrect, using both proximity to execution and influence on human decisions as evaluative criteria. To ensure appropriate validation velocity, organizations should explicitly distinguish between 'Direct-Execution Logic' (which directly commands physical or digital assets like Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) code or system configurations, requiring automated validation gates) and 'Human-Mediated Logic' (which influences human choices like procedural summaries or diagnostic reports, relying on structured expert review workflows). Artifacts such as PLC code, Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) configurations or automated control parameters may carry the highest execution risk as they have close proximity to execution. For example: - AI-generated valve control logic is likely to be classified as a High Risk, Direct Execution artifact. - Artifacts such as procedure summaries, condition reports or operational recommendations carry risk in proportion to the consequence of human actions they may influence. - AI-generated summary of a maintenance procedure should be classified as High Risk Human Mediated artifacts due to safety consequences if incorrect. - A draft internal email summary of this same procedure that is subject to ongoing human brainstorming and refinement might be classified as Low Risk.

Validation leads should rely strictly on this hard configuration data for audit paths, rather than relying on post-hoc explanation tools which can mistake downstream symptoms for the root cause of an artifact error.
