Section 2: The Profile

This profile is organized to help critical infrastructure stakeholders translate trustworthy SI objectives into actionable risk management activities. It does so through a structured set of practices, tasks, and illustrative implementations grounded in critical infrastructure use cases, case studies, and operational scenarios expanded upon in Appendix B: Scenario-to-Practice Mapping and Appendix C: Use Cases and Operational Scenarios, and informed by operational realities and existing risk management resources.

Each practice presents an application-relevant description of the risks that it addresses along with examples of tasks, implementations, and references that facilitate the translation of these high level practices into actionable controls for a given critical infrastructure environment. These are designed to be generally relatable across infrastructure sectors and include context to help organizations navigate the inherent trade-offs between different risk management considerations.

  • Practice: A high-level, governance-focused description of a risk management objective, providing a shared starting point across roles. Each practice includes a unique identifier and a clear incentive for adoption, emphasizing organizational mission and resilience rather than purely technical goals. Practices are designed to be conceptually distinct and ready for delegation to appropriate internal departments. Delegation works best when the owner also has the visibility, time, resources, access, and authority to act on the Practice.
  • Tasks: Discrete, assignable steps describing how the objective of a practice can be approached. These tasks identify specific actions that can be executed independently and provide a clear bridge between governance policy and operational implementation. Tasks often draw from across the AI RMF Functions, Categories, and Subcategories to emphasize a holistic approach to managing risk.
  • Implementations: Illustrative examples of tools, processes, or methods used to fulfill a task, giving concrete steps. These suggestions offer a spectrum of options, from steps that organizations with limited resources can take early to more advanced strategies.

The levels also work upward. Those carrying out an Implementation can read the Task and Practice above it to see what the work serves, what it is not expected to cover, and what to report back, so that technical teams and leadership are working from the same structure.

Each Practice, Task, and Implementation also carries up to five labeled fields. Readers in different roles may use them differently, and a reader may use only some of them.

  • Search Keywords: Terms that should lead a reader to this item, including words other sectors use for the same thing, and suggestions for terms that the reader might search for in other resources for more information. Search and navigation aids rather than a taxonomy, and not exhaustive.
  • Trustworthiness: Up to three headings; a heading appears only where there is something to put under it. Main Trustworthiness Contributions lists characteristics from the Trustworthiness Characteristics table (Section 1.3) that this item may contribute to, and the limits of those contributions. Each entry names one limit of the item’s contribution and one place where that limit is taken up, as examples: an item may have other limits, and other items may also address the same point. Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider lists characteristics that this item may work against as a side effect, and gives an example of where that side effect may be addressed, usually by another measure elsewhere. That pointer assumes the other measure can be put in place and resourced. Where it cannot, the side effect is no longer handled elsewhere, and the choice comes back to this item: accept the side effect, scale back this item, or find another way to address it. It has then become a tension to balance here. Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance lists characteristics that may pull against each other within this item, including a characteristic against itself, together with the conditions that bear on the choice between them.
  • Decisions for the Organization: Choices that this item leaves to the organization, posed as questions. They are prompts for the people responsible, not a complete list, and the answers will depend on the organization’s context and on any regulatory or contractual obligations that apply. Lists of triggers, conditions, and examples in this profile are illustrative, not complete. An organization is not expected to foresee every change. The lists help it recognize the common ones, and a named role decides whether others warrant action, and to what depth (see How Far to Go).
  • Related AI RMF Subcategories: The subcategories of the NIST AI RMF (1.0) that this item relates to. Listing one means this item can contribute toward that outcome. It does not mean that following this item satisfies the subcategory: AI RMF subcategories are organization-wide outcomes, and any single item here addresses part of one at most. The lists are not exhaustive. Each entry names one limit of the item’s contribution and one place where that limit is taken up, as examples: an item may have other limits, and other items may also address the same point.
  • References: Pointers to material that may help in implementing this item. Each citation under an item names the part that applies and why it is relevant there. These descriptions are also intended to help a reader, or an SI assistant, locate the current version or successor of a reference, or related material, when the cited document has been revised or superseded. Each description opens with a label denoting the kind of document: Regulation, Standard, Guidance, Research, Commentary, Incident Report, or Working Document, followed by information about the publisher and the reference itself. Working Document marks a source that presents itself as unfinished, such as a draft, a request for comments, or a concept paper. These are cited deliberately: the work is relevant and under way, and citing it records where practice is heading and that this profile is being developed alongside it. Each such description gives the source’s status as of this draft. These references are expected to change as the work matures, and will be updated or replaced in later drafts. The labels describe the document, not its worth: a seminal research paper may be as relevant as a standard, and the description says why each source is cited. References are neither requirements nor endorsements, and are not exhaustive.

The 13 core practices are summarized below. Many of the practices detailed herein are not novel inventions, but rather time-tested principles drawn from classic systems security engineering (such as NIST SP 800-160), process safety, and operational resilience. As the ease of fielding SI capabilities outpaces traditional engineering workflows, this Guidebook highlights these foundational disciplines, adapting established practices to SI systems.

Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success

Define performance, safety, and security baselines to help ensure that SI systems, if adopted, deliver measurable operational value aligned with organizational mission goals.

Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations

Establish multi-layered safeguards to maintain essential service delivery and functional safety when SI performance deviates or degrades.

Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior

Set deterministic boundary logic, risk thresholds, and independent safety wrappers for high-risk autonomous SI actions.

Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness

Maintain “break-glass” intervention procedures, out-of-band communication channels, and real-time situational awareness displays so human operators can safely override SI actions.

Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools

Implement distinct non-human identities (NHIs), cryptographically verifiable authentication, and least-privilege permissions to bound an SI agent’s operational scope and blast radius.

Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management

Apply pre-acquisition procurement oversight and supply-chain transparency (using SIBOMs and hazard reachability disclosures) to evaluate external vendor dependencies.

Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance

Maintain post-acquisition operational governance over internal SI deployments, dataset provenance, prompt versioning, and safety wrapper configurations.

Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures

Formulate forensic response protocols, triage pathways, and root-cause analysis capabilities tailored to SI system failures that can be complex, opaque, or non-deterministic.

Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust

Deliver role-specific training to combat automation complacency, build accurate mental models of SI limitations, and preserve critical manual control skills.

Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities

Capture decision-level runtime telemetry, prompt history, and guardrail interactions in logs protected from alteration where they serve as evidence, for forensic auditability.

Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning

Pre-allocate backup operational resources and document “manual mode” fallback playbooks to help maintain essential service delivery during extended SI system outages.

Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle

Subject machine-authored code, configurations, and procedures to multi-layered deterministic testing and subject matter expert reviews prior to operational execution and across their lifecycle.

Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority

Withdraw an SI system’s authority completely at end of life, preserve the knowledge and evidence that would otherwise be destroyed with it, and record what happens to everything that depended on it.

To the Reviewer:
The following text describes the intended complete state of the document. Many of its aspects are currently incomplete (e.g., not all tasks have complete mappings and the glossary is still a work-in-progress).

The Practices, Tasks, and Implementations that follow are not intended to be exhaustive, nor are they intended to be a checklist. Rather, they are intended to inform active professional discretion, illustrating specific examples of controls that are generally achievable and impactful in managing risk, and how to contextualize them in a critical infrastructure setting. Organizations should consider related additional risk management controls that may apply to their situation. Much more information on each practice can be found in the references and associated guidance and commentary. Each task includes mappings to AI RMF sub-categories. Organizations may cross-reference these with other SI risk management resources, including other AI RMF profiles, for additional guidance.

The order in which the practices, tasks, and implementations appear in this document represents a convenient ordering for understandability, rather than importance, sequence, or priority. Prioritization is specific to each organization’s situation and should be based on factors that include their regulatory and other obligations, operating environment, localized risk tolerances, legacy dependencies, and available resources. Appendix B: Scenario-to-Practice Mapping is intended to provide real-world context that can help to illuminate these factors. The practices, tasks, and implementations act together, and are linked in networks of localized dependencies, conceptual similarities, convenient actionable sequences, relationships connected by trustworthiness concepts and AI RMF control subcategories, and alternatives depending on specific situations and resourcing. These are shown by cross-references between entries. Due to the complexity of these networks, readers may find it useful to navigate them with an LLM-based assistant provided with the Markdown content. A YAML knowledge graph is also provided that the LLM-based assistant may find helpful in reducing incidences of truncation, omission, and hallucination.

Many of the terms used in this section are defined in Glossary and Terminology Crosswalk, particularly where they have different definitions across sectors and/or between the SI and cybersecurity communities. In many cases, terms are context-specific and may have slightly different meanings between sectors.

Unless this Guidebook defines a term, this Guidebook uses it in its general sense. Where a sector, regulation or standard gives a term a precise meaning, such as validation, verification, incident or safety function, this Guidebook does not invoke that meaning, and following this Guidebook does not by itself establish conformance with a requirement stated in those terms.

To the Reviewer:
We welcome suggestions for terms that should be in the glossary! We particularly welcome suggestions that might be confused between sectors, or that have specific compliance/regulatory meanings that we should clarify.

Organizations adopting this profile may find it useful to define terms in the context of their own environments and use cases, and to crosswalk them to similar terms and concepts used by the organizations and supply chain entities with whom they interact.
