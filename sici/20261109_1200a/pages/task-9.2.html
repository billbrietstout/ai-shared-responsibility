Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias

Training on how people work with SI systems helps personnel catch a convincing but wrong output in time, and avoid overriding one that is correct. Provide operational personnel with the skills to recognize and mitigate “automation bias”, the tendency to over-trust automated outputs, and other psychological factors that lead to miscalibrated over or under trust. Automation bias can show as an error of omission, in which personnel miss a problem that the SI system did not flag, or as an error of commission, in which they follow an incorrect recommendation despite information that contradicts it. Training should address both. This Task helps reduce the risk of human error during AI-driven anomalies and helps ensure that manual overrides remain an effective and timely safety-net for critical infrastructure functions. Training programs should explicitly cultivate accurate mental models of AI behavior across all operational roles, from grid dispatchers and plant operators to clinicians, fraud analysts, and network engineers.

Personnel should be trained not only on how the AI functions during ideal conditions, but on its known operational limitations, edge-case failure modes, and boundary behaviors. Hands-on simulation drills (Implementation 9.2.2: Implement Realistic Incident Simulation Training) should periodically expose personnel to ambiguous, out-of-distribution, or low-confidence AI outputs, building tactical muscle memory for evaluating uncertainty, performing spot-check investigations, and executing timely overrides under realistic time pressure. Training should also address the effect of explanation and rationale features on reliance, so that personnel do not treat a plausible rationale as evidence that a recommendation is correct (see Implementation 4.6.1: Design Rationale Displays that Give Personnel Something to Check). Refresh this training when an update to the SI system, including one made by its provider, or a change in its operating context, could alter its limitations or failure modes, as assessed under Task 7.5: Conduct Impact Assessments and Risk Management for SI System and Model Updates. Failure behavior that personnel learned to recognize before such a change may no longer apply. Where personnel in simulation consistently miss the same kind of SI system error, report it to those responsible for the interface or the SI system’s design as possible evidence of a design problem, not only of a training need.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes overrides that remain an effective and timely safety net: personnel trained on the AI system’s known limitations, edge-case failure modes and the procedure for safely overriding it, and drilled in simulation on ambiguous, out-of-distribution and low-confidence outputs under realistic time pressure. This Task does not cover the override mechanisms and authorization tiers, which Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures covers.
  • Bias Managed: Contributes management of automation bias, a human-cognitive bias with a known direction, toward accepting outputs that repeated correct performance has made seem authoritative, and known aggravating conditions such as cognitive fatigue from extended shifts or isolated duty stations, in decisions made with the AI system. This Task does not cover interface design against passive monitoring, which Implementation 3.6.3: Cognitive Engagement by Design covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Training content on the SI system’s known limitations, edge-case failure modes, and override procedures shows which failures personnel are prepared for, and by implication which they are not. An attacker could use it to craft a manipulation that falls outside what personnel are drilled to recognize. Limiting that detail to the roles that need it can reduce the exposure. Protecting the material is otherwise a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Safe: Where personnel defer to the AI system by habit, training that stresses failure modes and rehearses overrides moves their trust toward what the system warrants. Where the system is usually right and the rehearsed failures are rare, the same emphasis can push personnel toward the under-trust this Task’s own text names, overriding correct outputs in situations where an unwarranted override is itself hazardous. Options include training against evidence personnel can check, such as the rationale displays in Implementation 4.6.1: Design Rationale Displays that Give Personnel Something to Check, so that acceptance and override both rest on a check. They also include drills under Implementation 9.2.2: Implement Realistic Incident Simulation Training that present unusual outputs which turn out to be correct.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 3.4 (on defining operator proficiency): This Task covers training and simulation drills that build operator proficiency with the AI system’s known limitations, failure modes and boundary behaviors, and with timely override; it does not cover assessing whether that proficiency has been achieved or retained. For that, see for example Task 9.3: Establish Audit-Ready Systems for Verifying Workforce Readiness.
  • MEASURE 3.1 (on tracking known and emerging risks): This Task covers personnel who can recognize AI degradation and near-misses, and who know which reporting channel is open to them even where organizational disincentives discourage its use; it does not cover establishing those channels or their protections. For that, see for example Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which updates or changes in operating context trigger a refresh of this training?
  • How much weight does training give to failure modes, given the risk of under-trust? (see the Safe versus Safe tension in Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias)
  • Who receives reports that personnel in simulation consistently miss the same kind of SI error, and who decides whether it is a design problem or a training need?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2.7, Human-AI Configuration, describes both unwarranted aversion to generative AI and over-reliance on it, defines automation bias as excessive deference to automated systems, and notes that it can exacerbate other risks such as confabulation. These are the miscalibrated over- and under-trust this Task trains personnel to recognize.
