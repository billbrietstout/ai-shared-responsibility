Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures

Develop and implement protocols for responding to and investigating SI-related failures in operational environments, addressing the challenges posed by SI system behaviors that can be complex, opaque, or non-deterministic.

This Practice helps an organization investigate and respond to incidents involving SI systems with the clarity that regulators, safety investigators, and incident response teams need, and contributes to keeping such incidents from compromising accountability or the reliable operation of the organization’s systems. Organizations with established incident response, investigation, and reporting arrangements can build on them for SI systems. For example, cybersecurity-focused playbooks for incident response should be expanded to cover the broader SI Risk Surface discussed in Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.

Those investigating and responding to an SI-related incident within the organization may need to judge whether an anomaly is more likely an active exploit or a non-adversarial risk across the Trustworthiness Characteristics, such as drift, an unannounced model update, stale or contaminated retrieved context, dynamic multi-agent feedback loops, or plausible hallucinations in generated logic (Implementation 8.1.6: Incident Triage Across the Dual Attack/Risk Surface). That judgment often has to be made, and acted on, before the cause is known for sure. Containing the situation and understanding it can pull against each other: investigating can delay restoring service, while acting without understanding can let the situation worsen or destroy the evidence needed to stop it recurring. Some steps, such as isolating the SI system, preserving its state, or falling back to an alternative operating mode, can serve both (see Task 3.4 and Practice 11). Isolating the system or falling back is available only where the organization has prepared for it in advance, and each brings its own considerations, such as losing whatever the SI system was contributing, including protective functions (see Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity).

An apparent operator error may also have been shaped by an SI system’s output, so investigations should consider an SI system whose outputs personnel relied on as a possible contributing factor, and not only as a cause or an attack vector. This Practice focuses on governance and resourcing to satisfy transparency and regulatory requirements. It dovetails with Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations, which focuses on technical and functional requirements, and Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning, which focuses on maintaining operations. It also draws on, among others, the records kept under Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities and the inventory of SI deployments in Task 7.1: Maintain an Internal Registry of SI Deployments.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Accountable: Contributes identification of where an SI system’s failures must be reconstructable to the standard that regulators and safety investigators expect, and what to do where a system cannot support that, together with named people who own an incident from first response through to return to service. This Practice does not cover capturing the records an investigation draws on, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.
  • Transparent: Contributes disclosure in both directions: notification of SI incidents to regulators and the public, sharing of de-identified failure modes with peer operators, and reporting routes through which personnel can raise near-misses without bearing the cost of doing so. This Practice does not cover obtaining disclosures from vendors in the first place, which Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management covers.
  • Safe: Contributes a decision on where to use an SI system whose failures cannot be traced to a cause (Task 8.2: Identify Additional Controls that May Be Required in Scenarios Where Deterministic Root Cause Analysis Is Not Possible). Where regulation requires the cause to be found, that requirement governs. Otherwise, the organization records whether other controls close the gap, and sets margins beyond which control passes to manual oversight or to a fallback whose own failures can be traced and that shares as few of the SI system’s sources of error as practical, and with those it does share, such as common sensors, identified. This Practice does not cover enforcing those boundaries while the system runs, which Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Disclosing an incident to regulators and the public, and sharing failure modes with peer operators, describes how the organization’s SI systems failed and which control did not hold. An adversary could use that account to repeat an attack here, or to target peers running the same SI system before they have closed the gap. Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning strips model internals and safety-sensitive data from shared records, and the same considerations can inform what a public disclosure includes, within any legal or regulatory obligation to disclose.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Practice covers SI-aware processes for analyzing, responding to and communicating incidents, including to regulators, the public and peer operators; it does not cover sustaining and restoring essential functions during and after an incident. For that, see for example Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning.
  • GOVERN 4.3 (on establishing SI incident response and information-sharing processes): This Practice covers incident identification across the attack and risk surface, protected near-miss reporting, and de-identified sharing with peer operators; it does not cover the SI testing GOVERN 4.3 also names. For that, see for example Practice 1 and Practice 2.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who decides, during an incident, whether to contain first or investigate first, and do they have the information, authority, and support to act on that decision?
  • Where do the people, time, and budget for investigating SI incidents come from, and what happens when they are not available?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-61r3, Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management, April 2025, from NIST. Section 2.2 sets out incident response roles beyond the response team, including leadership, legal, public affairs and asset owners, with incident handlers on staff, on contract or available when needed. Subcategory RS.AN-03 asks that analysis establish the sequence of events and the underlying or systemic root causes. Section 1 limits the guide to adverse cybersecurity events, which is the boundary this Practice extends to the non-adversarial part of the SI Risk Surface.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 6.4.3 asks that cyber and non-cyber incidents affecting OT be identified and classified, with accidental actions by authorized personnel as a class alongside targeted and untargeted attacks, and suggests engaging external organizations where OT forensic capability is lacking. Section 6.2.4.5 names the choice between returning to normal operations quickly and preserving forensic data as a priority to determine, which bears on the tension in Task 8.3.
  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Appendix A.1.8, Incident Disclosure, quotes a definition of an “AI incident” whose listed harms include disruption of the management and operation of critical infrastructure, notes that formal channels to report and document SI incidents do not currently exist, and holds that logging, recording and analyzing incidents ease sharing with other “AI Actors.” It bears on this Practice’s regulatory notification and de-identified sharing with peer operators.
  • Guidance AI Incident Response Framework, Workstream 2: Preparing Defenders for a Changing Cybersecurity Landscape, version 1.0, 27 October 2025, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 1.3.1 gives an SI incident taxonomy that places performance failures such as model drift beside security vulnerabilities and misuse. Section 3.3.3.1 notes that a model will not in general give the same output from the same input, so raw outputs need preserving to reconstruct an incident. It covers only what is SI-specific, deferring the rest to NIST SP 800-61r3.
  • Guidance DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles, June 2009, from the U.S. Department of Energy. The Preface treats human error not as a cause of failure on its own but as the effect or symptom of deeper trouble in the system, systematically connected to people’s tools, tasks and operating environment. Written for DOE facilities, it bears on treating an SI system whose outputs personnel relied on as part of that system when investigating an apparent operator error.
