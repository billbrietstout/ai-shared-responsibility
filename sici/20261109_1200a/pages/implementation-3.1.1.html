Implementation 3.1.1: Identify SI System Decision Points and Potential Outcomes of SI System Errors

Mapping where SI output feeds a decision, and whether anything independent of the SI system would catch an error there, shows where existing safeguards can be relied on and where new ones are needed. Determine decision points that may be affected by errors in AI-generated outputs (e.g. control action, predictions, recommendations, summaries, code, procedures, or system configurations) and how they can influence operations, including downstream effects. For each decision point, also record what currently detects or stops an error before it takes effect, such as an operator check, an interlock or a downstream validation, and whether that safeguard depends on the SI system.

In addition to unintentional errors, consider decision points where a capable adversary could deliberately manipulate AI-generated outputs to achieve a specific harmful outcome, distinct from the AI simply malfunctioning on its own. For cyber-physical and industrial systems, mapping should explicitly encompass decision points bridging enterprise IT and Operational Technology (OT) environments, establishing as much visibility across the IT/OT boundary as practical to detect telemetry that is spoofed or faulty, command paths that are unauthorized or unintended, or unverified configuration transfers. Error modes to evaluate include false positives/negatives, increased measurement bias, reduced precision, instruction misinterpretation, or increased output latency. Further error modes include an output that is missing when it is needed, outputs issued out of sequence, and actions outside the authority given to the SI system. Errors that resemble normal output, and errors whose consequences appear only later, such as a maintenance recommendation that defers work on a failing component, can be harder to detect and trace than errors that fail visibly. Hazard analysis methods the organization already applies to its processes, such as process hazard analysis (PHA) or failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), can help structure this enumeration.

Downstream operational effects to identify include:

  • An AI-based machine vision system for detecting the presence and extent of defects can affect the actions of automated systems as well as humans, such as if the vision system overestimates or underestimates defects.
  • Conventional productivity tools, such as report summarizers, applied to summarizing technical condition reports, affect the decisions made by humans who rely on the summaries, particularly if they fail to recognize application-specific terminology (Implementation 2.4.1: Benchmarking on Application-Specific Terminology) and erroneously omit mentions of signs of imminent failure.
  • SI systems that filter, rank or prioritize information, such as alarm suppression, work-order ranking, or an agent’s choice of which data to retrieve or which tool to call, affect decisions by determining what people and downstream systems see, even when they make no recommendation themselves.
  • General-purpose AI assistants used to generate configuration files, automation scripts, or diagnostic guidance for OT/ICS systems can introduce the same type of error as an AI system directly connected to the control loop, even though the assistant itself has no OT connection. This second-order path, in which an engineer reviews assistant-generated output before it is deployed as a configuration or action, should be identified and assessed as a separate decision point, since the presence of human review does not by itself establish that the output was adequately verified. See Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle for further discussion.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which decision points count as significant, and so get the intercept points of Implementation 3.2.1: Identify SI Output Intercept Points?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 200-1: AI Use Taxonomy: A Human-Centered Approach, from NIST. Its Table 1, Taxonomy of Human-AI Activities lists the kinds of activity an AI system performs for a person, including content synthesis, detection, monitoring, prediction and decision making. A published counterpart to the list of output types above, and a fuller checklist when enumerating what a deployment actually does.
  • Guidance MITRE ATLAS, maintained by MITRE, a not-for-profit organization in the United States that operates federally funded research and development centers. A matrix of tactics and techniques observed in attacks on SI-enabled systems, structured after the same organization’s ATT\&CK matrix for enterprise systems. Supplies named techniques for the second half of the enumeration asked for here, the decision points at which an adversary shapes an output deliberately rather than the system erring on its own.
  • Commentary Dragos 2026 OT Cybersecurity Year in Review, from Dragos, a commercial industrial control system cybersecurity company in the United States, drawing on its own threat intelligence and field engagements. Estimates that fewer than 10 percent of OT networks worldwide have network visibility and monitoring in place, and reports poor segmentation between IT and OT networks as the most common architectural weakness in its 2025 service reports, present in 81 percent of them. Evidence for the condition under which the IT/OT boundary decision points named here go unobserved.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 4.1.1, Framing OT Risk, suggests incorporating cyber attack and IT failure scenarios into process hazard analysis (PHA) or failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), and considering what design features exist in the OT system to prevent or mitigate an impact. Section 6.1.3 adds that a PHA, FMEA or analysis of past events can help in understanding the potential impact of a cyber incident. Supports folding SI error modes, and the safeguards that catch them, into those methods here.
