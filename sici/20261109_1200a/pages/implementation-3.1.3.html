Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses

Thresholds agreed in advance give an organization a defined point at which to respond to SI behavior, rather than deciding case by case while the behavior unfolds. Establish threshold criteria that bound acceptable AI behavior in terms of impact severity, likelihood, and recovery feasibility. When establishing risk thresholds, avoid relying too heavily on purely technical, point-in-time metrics, whether traditional vulnerability scoring systems (such as the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS)) or raw algorithmic outputs and performance benchmarks (such as model confidence scores, error rates, or statistical accuracy). Instead, utilize consequence-driven frameworks, drawing on the operational prioritization logic of models like Stakeholder-Specific Vulnerability Categorization (SSVC), or on layer of protection analysis (LOPA) in sectors that already use it, to evaluate risks based on real-world impacts to public health, safety, and mission continuity. This does not preclude runtime-evaluable metrics, such as model confidence or deviation from a reference estimate, from serving as monitor triggers that indicate when a consequence-based threshold may be approached. Associate these risk thresholds with a defined organizational response such as enforced action, system isolation, authority throttling, reduction to a read-only or advisory mode in which further actions need human approval, or operator escalation. Each of these responses is available only if prepared in advance, and each brings its own considerations. An advisory mode, for example, moves the workload to people who need the time and information to approve each action (see Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation). Review these risk thresholds and control responses as the AI system, task, and operational environment change. Records of how often each threshold is reached, and of how often people reverse the SI system’s actions or decline its proposals, can inform that review. Frequent reversals can indicate a threshold set too permissively. An approval step at which proposals are almost never declined may be working as intended, or may indicate that approvers lack the time, information or authority to examine them. Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers discusses how to determine who holds authority to accept a risk against these thresholds, and to restrict or suspend operation when they are exceeded.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which consequence-driven scheme sets the thresholds, such as SSVC, or LOPA where the sector already uses it?
  • What response is tied to each threshold, from operator escalation through a read-only or advisory mode to isolation?
  • Who may accept a risk against a threshold? (Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers)

References:

  • Guidance NIST Special Publication 800-30 Revision 1, Guide for Conducting Risk Assessments, from NIST. Sections 3.1 to 3.3 set out preparing for, conducting and communicating a risk assessment, with the conducting step broken into numbered tasks. Appendix G gives assessment scales for the likelihood of a threat event occurring and of its resulting in adverse impacts, Appendix H scales and a template for impact, and Appendix I scales for risk determination. These are the published scales behind the impact severity and likelihood limbs of a threshold set here, applied once per decision point.
