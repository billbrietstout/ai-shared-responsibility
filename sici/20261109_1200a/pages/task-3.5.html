Task 3.5: Define and Control Risks to Adjacent and Related Systems

Because a failure in an SI system can affect other systems through its outputs, its access, and the infrastructure and resources it shares, sometimes faster than people can intervene, planning for those effects helps an organization limit how far a failure spreads. Define and control risks to other systems that may be affected by a failure in this system, including:

  • Systems that are intentionally interdependent with this system, where errors travel along functional, data, or operational dependencies (“cascading failures”).
  • Systems that may be unrelated except for shared adjacency in a physical, logical, network, resource, supply chain, or other sense (“splash damage” or “blast radius”).
  • Systems and parties outside the organization that are affected through shared capacity, such as demand on grid or water capacity, or through disruption to neighboring communities, where the effect can return to the organization as direct risk or as indirect risk, such as regulatory or community opposition.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes containment of consequence beyond the AI system’s own function: execution confined to isolated segments, and limits on shared resources such as compute, bandwidth, power and exclusive sensor access, so a failure reaches a bounded set of assets. This Task does not cover bounding what the AI system is permitted to reach in the first place, which Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities covers.
  • Resilient: Contributes the common-cause analysis that redundancy rests on: whether systems and safeguards that are functionally independent share a facility, a power or cooling supply, a network path, a hypervisor or a municipal substrate. This Task does not cover establishing the redundancy itself, which Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The dependency and shared-infrastructure maps built under this Task show which systems a failure would reach, and which safeguards share a facility, power supply, network path, or service. An attacker could use them to choose a single point whose failure disables several systems or safeguards at once. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Reliable: Where an AI system’s demand for a shared resource is steady, a hard limit protects the systems alongside it while the SI-enabled function continues to perform as required. Where demand rises during the same upset that raises demand elsewhere, the limit removes capacity from the function at the point it is most needed. One way to balance this is the graded arrangement in Implementation 3.5.4: Define and Implement Resource Limiting, where a soft limit and a notification precede the hard limit; others include reserving capacity for the highest-consequence function, or defining the state the function enters when it is throttled.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 5.1 (on identifying risk likelihood and impacts): This Task identifies impacts on systems that share physical space, network paths, logical substrates or scarce resources with the AI system, and on systems downstream of it; it does not cover impacts at the AI system’s own decision points. For those, see for example Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs.
  • MAP 3.3 (on documenting application scope of the AI system): This Task bounds the scope an AI system can reach, by confining its execution to isolated segments and capping the shared resources it may consume; it does not cover defining the intended scope from which those bounds should be derived. For that, see for example Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success.
  • GOVERN 4.2 (on documenting impact): This Task requires that impacts estimated beyond the facility perimeter be recorded as bounded engineering assumptions where telemetry is unavailable, so that the limits of the assessment stay visible to a later reader; it does not cover communicating those impacts to the operators of the affected systems.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which external parties, such as utilities or neighboring operators, are told about dependencies or exposure the analysis finds, and by whom?
  • Who resources analysis of shared infrastructure that the organization does not own or see into?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Table D-2 gives the Segmentation technique the purpose of containing adversary activities and non-adversarial stresses, such as fires and floods, to the enclave, segment or facility in which they arise. It gives the Realignment technique the purpose of minimizing connections between mission-critical and non-critical services, so that a failure of non-critical services is less likely to affect mission-critical ones. These are the containment aims this Task applies to the failure of an AI system. Section C.3 counts alterations in behavior that affect external systems, which can cascade beyond the system of interest, among the effects cyber resiliency engineering considers.
  • Incident Report Incident Review: Considering Simultaneous Voltage-Sensitive Load Reductions, January 2025, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). On July 10, 2024, a transmission line fault led to the customer-initiated, simultaneous loss of about 1,500 MW of voltage-sensitive load, mostly data centers transferring to backup power. Much of it came from a protection scheme that counts voltage disturbances and moves the facility off the grid until it is manually reconnected. NERC notes that grid planning has historically considered large generation losses rather than load losses of this size. This bears on the third category above: the combined response of many facilities reaching the regional grid that they and others depend on.
