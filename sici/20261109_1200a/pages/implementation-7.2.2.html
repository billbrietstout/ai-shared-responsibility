Implementation 7.2.2: Verify Data Provenance

A provenance trail helps the organization show where critical data came from and, where the trail is protected, detect later changes to the data or the trail. Tools such as cryptographic hashing and data lineage tools can be used to maintain an audit trail for tracing critical data from source to final SI system output. Whether a trail is protected from later change, and how strongly, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Weigh the consequence of the data, how likely the trail is to be needed as evidence, and the risk that someone changes the data or the trail without detection. Some guidance, such as AI Data Security, describes a cryptographically signed, immutable, append-only ledger of data changes as one way to make such changes detectable. Implementation 10.2.2: Protect Logs from Alteration Where They Serve as Evidence describes the options, including how to reconcile them with deletion obligations.

For critical datasets, the trail can record where the data came from and the path it follows through the SI system. Original versions can be cryptographically signed, with each later revision signed by the person who made it. A hash confirms only that data matches what the source published, and cannot reveal changes made before publication. Data whose origin cannot be established, or whose hash does not match, can be labeled as unverified and limited to lower-consequence uses until it is verified. For retrieval, documents can be checked against the organization’s controlled document management system so that only current, approved versions are indexed.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which datasets count as critical for the provenance trail, and which uses are allowed for unverified data?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control SR-4, Provenance, documents, monitors and maintains valid provenance, defined as the chronology of origin, development, ownership, location and changes, for systems, components and associated data, and its discussion covers monitoring for unauthorized changes to provenance records and their non-repudiation, which is the audit trail this Implementation builds for data used by AI systems.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Table D-4 describes Provenance Tracking as identifying and tracking the provenance of data, software or hardware elements, and Integrity Checks as validating the integrity or quality of information, including through cryptographic hashes. Section 3.1.5.2 adds that checksums designed to catch accidental change are not sufficient where modification may be deliberate, and that cryptographically signed hashes are needed to support both integrity checking and provenance tracking.
  • Guidance AI Data Security: Best Practices for Securing Data Used to Train & Operate AI Systems, Version 1.0, May 2025, from the National Security Agency’s Artificial Intelligence Security Center with CISA, the FBI and partner agencies. Best practice 1 asks that provenance tracking trace data origins and log the path data follows through an SI system, using a cryptographically signed provenance database that keeps an immutable, append-only ledger of data changes. Best practice 3 asks that original versions of data be signed and that each later revision be signed by the person who made it. These are the mechanisms behind the audit trail this Implementation asks for.
  • Guidance Artificial intelligence and machine learning: Supply chain risks and mitigations, 2025, from the Australian Signals Directorate, co-sealed by national cyber security agencies of Canada, Japan, the Republic of Korea, New Zealand, Singapore, the United Kingdom and the United States (the National Security Agency). The AI data section, under data verification and provenance, notes that checksums, hashes and digital signatures verify integrity only against the source and do not include modifications made to the data before publishing, and that more robust lineage and provenance methods are being developed. This is the limit of the hashing this Implementation relies on.
