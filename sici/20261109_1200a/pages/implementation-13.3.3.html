Implementation 13.3.3: Determine and Record What Happens to Everything that Depended on the System

Deciding what happens to each thing that relied on a system helps an organization retire it without leaving others to fail unnoticed. Identify the processes, systems and people that relied on the AI system’s output, and record for each whether it will be migrated to an alternative, formally notified and wound down, or left without provision. The list of dependents can be built from observed calls and access logs as well as from design and integration records, over a period long enough to include infrequent uses such as monthly reporting, seasonal operations or rarely triggered emergency functions. Abandonment may be a legitimate decision, but it should be a recorded one. Where the SI system has authorized or initiated actions that are still pending in downstream systems, record how those actions will be completed, reversed or handed over, and the point from which new actions are no longer authorized, since withdrawing authority for new actions does not by itself resolve commitments already made. Human operators and downstream automation may have adapted to the retired system’s particular behavior, so the effect of removal is not limited to the function it formally provided. Where the dependents are internal systems consuming the AI system’s outputs, the mapping and the deprecation timeline are in Implementation 7.6.1: Internal Dependency Mapping (Orphan System Prevention); what this Implementation adds is that a decision is recorded for each dependent, including where that decision is to make no provision. Dependents can also be outside the organization. Where known external parties rely on the SI system’s outputs for consequential decisions, the record can include whether and how they will be notified, the effective date, and any alternative or transition arrangement, subject to applicable legal and contractual obligations.

After retirement, a dependent that was missed can fail without an obvious error, for example a dashboard that continues to display the last values it received as though they were current, or a controller that holds the last setpoint it was sent. Monitoring dependents for failed calls, missing inputs and stale values for a period after retirement can help reveal dependents the record missed, so that each receives a recorded decision. Where a dependent is a control system that received setpoints or recommendations from the SI system, its behavior on loss of that input should be defined before retirement, as set out in Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • For each dependent, is it migrated, notified and wound down, or left without provision?
  • Which external parties are notified, with what notice and transition arrangement, within any legal and contractual obligations?
  • For how long are dependents monitored after retirement for failed calls, missing inputs and stale values?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Suggested action GV-1.7-002, under GOVERN 1.7, lists factors to consider when decommissioning a generative AI system, including data retention, data leakage after decommissioning, dependencies between upstream, downstream and other systems, and users’ emotional entanglement. Suggested action MG-2.4-001, under MANAGE 2.4, asks for communication plans for deactivation that cover reasons, workarounds, user access removal and alternative processes. Together they cover the dependents and the notification this Implementation asks to be decided and recorded.
