Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System

Before an AI system is torn down, capture the evidence that will not survive it, and address what was relying on it. This includes volatile state that exists only while the system is running, the operational function the system was performing for others, and any protective function it was performing that would otherwise disappear without a corresponding record. What is preserved, and for how long, can be set from records retention schedules, sector record-keeping obligations, and any requirement to preserve records, and the record can state what was preserved, where, for how long and in whose custody. Where the facility has a management of change process, retiring an SI function in a process that it covers can be taken through that process. Where a function returns to manual operation or to a fallback mode, such as the one in Implementation 11.2.2: Develop a Validated “Safe Operating Mode” that Depends Minimally on SI Infrastructure, that mode should be in place, and the personnel who will run it confirmed proficient under Implementation 3.6.1: Baseline Human Competency Retention, before authority is withdrawn under Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes the capture of state that exists only while the system runs, before the teardown that destroys it, so that an incident-driven retirement does not also destroy the evidence of what prompted it. This Task does not cover the durable logging that survives teardown on its own, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.
  • Whichever characteristic the retired protective function served: Contributes the treatment of a retired detection, monitoring, screening or filtering capability as a change to controls as well as an asset disposal, so that a protective function, whether it guarded against intrusion, equipment faults, unsafe outputs or bad input data, is handed over rather than silently discontinued. It draws on, but does not itself maintain, the account of system behavior kept under Implementation 13.1.2: Maintain a Current Account of System Behavior, Not Only Its Specification.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Preserving volatile state and learned material before teardown retains content that the retirement was in part intended to remove, and volatile state may contain material never intended to persist at all. Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance governs how that material is then handled, and the tension is set out at Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority.
  • Secure: The dependents list, captured state, and any record of a coverage gap left by a retired protective capability show what relied on the system and where detection is weaker for a time. An attacker could use them to time an attack for that window or to target a dependent that was left without provision. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accountable versus Safe: Where the retirement is planned and the capability is not protective, preserving evidence and handing over the function can both be done at an unhurried pace. Where the retirement follows an incident, the same actions compete: capturing volatile state takes time during which a system suspected of causing harm is still running. One way to balance this is to separate isolation from teardown, cutting the system’s authority and reach first under Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result while leaving it running long enough to capture state; others include pre-authorizing the capture so it does not require a decision in the moment.
  • Safe versus Safe: Where a successor runs alongside the retiring system in shadow mode, the overlap avoids a gap in the capability without the two competing for control. Where both can act on the same equipment or process, the same overlap can produce conflicting actions, with each system acting as though it held the authority. One way to balance this is to bound the overlap and to give authority over each item of equipment to one system at a time, transferring it at a recorded point; others include keeping the successor in shadow mode until the transfer, using the comparison described for re-commissioning in Implementation 11.4.2: Implement “Shadow Mode” Validation and Independent Safety Protection Checks for SI Re-Commissioning.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 4.2 (on documenting impact): This Task covers the identification of parties that depended on a retired AI system and a recorded decision for each, including where that decision is to make no provision; it does not cover the technical mapping of internal systems consuming its outputs, or the deprecation timeline given to them. For that, see for example Implementation 7.6.1: Internal Dependency Mapping (Orphan System Prevention).
  • GOVERN 1.7 (on establishing decommissioning processes): This Task covers the requirement that phasing out does not decrease trustworthiness by removing a protective function without handover, and that evidence is captured before teardown makes it unrecoverable; it does not cover the withdrawal of authority. For that, see for example Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What is preserved, for how long and in whose custody, given retention schedules, preservation requirements, and any obligation to purge? (see the Privacy-Enhanced and Secure versus Transparent tension in Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority)
  • Is the retirement of an SI function taken through the facility’s management of change process?
  • How long may a successor overlap the retiring system, and when does authority over each item of equipment pass to it? (see the Safe versus Safe tension in Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System)
  • Who runs the manual or fallback mode after retirement, and what training, time, and authority do they need to do it?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-86, Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response, August 2006, from NIST. Section 3.1.3, Incident Response Considerations, has the containment decision made with the incident response team, on the basis of containment policy and the assessed risk, so that the chosen strategy mitigates risk while maintaining the integrity of potential evidence whenever possible. It also asks organizations to consider in advance the impact of containment strategies on operations. This bears on the Accountable versus Safe tension in this Task.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, September 2020, from NIST. Control SI-12, Information Management and Retention, has information within and output from a system retained in accordance with applicable laws, regulations, policies and operational requirements. Its discussion notes that these requirements can extend beyond system disposal and suggests coordinating with records management personnel. This bears on setting what this Task preserves from retention obligations rather than from what happens to be available.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. In the OT overlay, Appendix F.7.5, the OT Discussion for control CM-3, Configuration Change Control, asks that configuration change control procedures align with the organization’s management of change practices. This bears on taking the retirement of an SI function in a covered process through an existing management of change process.
