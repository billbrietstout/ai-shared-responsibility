Implementation 4.5.1: Define Engineering Time-Constant Thresholds

Audit automated processes to identify paths where the window between an AI anomaly and an irreversible consequence is shorter than what human intervention can be relied upon to achieve. For these paths, document that real-time human intervention is not a primary risk mitigation control. Where the process already has a hazard analysis, this window corresponds to what process safety practice calls the process safety time, with the SI anomaly as the initiating failure, and the existing analysis may provide a starting figure. An SI system can also shorten the window, for example by acting faster than the process normally changes, or by issuing the same action to many assets at once, so that the consequence arrives at all of them before an operator has dealt with the first. What can be relied upon depends on the time available and on what the operator has to do with it. The time an operator needs should be established for the specific role, interface and staffing level, for example through the exercises in Implementation 4.1.3: Regularly Practice and Evaluate Intervention Processes, and should include the time to gain situational awareness of the system’s state (Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness), not only the time to act. Where the time is measured in minutes, an operator may be able to detect, diagnose, and act. Where it is measured in seconds, a memorized response to an unambiguous trigger is the most dependable option. For example, FAA guidance on standard operating procedures (see FAA AC 120-71B) advises avoiding such responses wherever possible and limiting unavoidable ones to fewer than three items containing no conditional decision steps. Judging whether an AI-selected action is appropriate is likely to be a conditional decision that does not meet that bound. Time is not the only limit. An operator can rarely be expected to verify in real time that a complex AI system is following its rules, and can judge only whether its actions, perhaps with an accompanying explanation, appear acceptable (see Bainbridge 1983). Where the system was adopted because it handles cases human judgment handles poorly, that judgment is what the oversight arrangement relies on. This even applies to an attentive operator, and is compounded where automatic control masks a developing problem by compensating for it up until the point where human intervention becomes necessary. See Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs for automated controls, as well as Implementation 4.2.3, Task 4.6, Task 4.4, and Implementation 11.2.3 for further details.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What operator response time is assumed for each role, and from what evidence?

References:

  • Research Lisanne Bainbridge, “Ironies of Automation”, Automatica volume 19 number 6, 1983, pages 775 to 779. Automatica is the journal of the International Federation of Automatic Control, an international body whose members are national control engineering organizations. A seminal paper on the human factors of automated process control. Its subject is the process-control computers of 1983, and the mechanisms it identifies are relevant to AI systems. Section 1.1.3 sets out why an operator cannot verify in real time that an automated system is following its rules.
  • Guidance FAA AC 120-71B, Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers, 10 January 2017, from the United States Federal Aviation Administration, the civil aviation authority of the United States. The AC states that it is not mandatory and does not constitute a regulation. Paragraph 4.2.5 gives the conditions under which a response may be relied on from memory: avoided wherever possible, clearly identified, emphasized in training, fewer than three items, and containing no conditional decision steps.
  • Research Endsley, M.R. and Kiris, E.O., “The Out-of-the-Loop Performance Problem and Level of Control in Automation”, Human Factors 37(2), 1995, from the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society. In its experiment, decisions after an expert system broke down took longer for participants who had worked under higher levels of automation, and they were less aware of what had been happening. This bears on counting the time to regain situational awareness, not only the time to act, in operator response time.
