Implementation 11.4.1: Define a Checklist for Validating System Safety Before Re-Engaging SI Control

A signed checklist gives an organization a clear record of why it judged an SI system ready to resume control. Create a specific, itemized checklist to be completed and signed off before returning a process from manual mode back to AI control. The checklist confirms that the root cause of the original outage has been addressed or, where it could not be identified, that the compensating controls and safety margins established under Task 8.2: Identify Additional Controls that May Be Required in Scenarios Where Deterministic Root Cause Analysis Is Not Possible support the scope of the return, and that the system behaves as expected against known baselines. Candidate items include confirming that recovered data, such as configuration and model files, is complete and current to the last known-good backup, and that system functionality has been tested. Where a malicious actor may have been present, the checklist can also ask whether those backups can be trusted and how the system will be shown to be safe after recovery. The checklist should also record whether the system has undergone significant change that needs reassessment or reauthorization before the SI system resumes control.

Where an organization already uses pre-startup safety reviews or management-of-change records, as is common in the process industries, the checklist can follow that format, with a named signatory and the evidence recorded for each item. Further candidate items include confirming that the override paths under Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures work, briefing operators on what changed during the outage, and setting the conditions that would return the process to manual operation during the first period after re-engagement. Some frameworks and obligations, such as SP 800-34, assign the determination of significant change to specific roles. Who signs the checklist is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. A signatory with authority under Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers and close knowledge of the system can judge the evidence well, while one whose performance is measured on the SI system’s availability carries pressure to return it to service. Where few qualified people are available, for example in a small organization or at a remote site, the only person able to judge the evidence may also be the one measured on availability; where that is so, record it and how the conflict is offset, such as by independent review of the evidence (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks). Record the signatory and the reason for the choice.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who signs the checklist, and how is any conflict with availability targets compensated?
  • Does the checklist follow an existing pre-startup safety review or management-of-change format?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5, Release 5.2.0, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement SI-2(7), Root Cause Analysis, added in Release 5.2.0, calls for root cause analysis of issues or failures, actions to address the root cause, and monitoring of those actions for effectiveness. Its discussion lists what the analysis includes: a timeline, missed warning signs, key decisions, gaps, mitigations and verification of effectiveness, which are candidate items for the re-engagement checklist here.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-34r1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems, May 2010, from NIST. Their Section 4.4, Reconstitution Phase, lists validation steps before a recovered system returns to normal operations: concurrent processing, validation data testing that recovered data is complete and current to the last available backup, and validation functionality testing. It also states that the ISCP Coordinator, in coordination with the Information System Owner, ISSO and SAISO and with the concurrence of the Authorizing Official, must determine whether the system has undergone significant change and will require reassessment and reauthorization. This bears on the items this checklist confirms.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-184, Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery, December 2016, from NIST. Section 2.3.4, Root Cause and Containment Strategy Determination, notes that discovering the full root cause of a cyber event is not always achievable, and sets two objectives an investigation should meet before recovery starts in earnest: basic knowledge of the adversary’s objective, and high confidence in the mechanisms used to persist access or confirmation of non-persistent intent. The search for the final root cause may then continue in parallel with recovery. Written for cyber events, it is an established model for the sufficiency test this Implementation applies when a root cause could not be identified.
  • Guidance Principles of operational technology cyber security, 2024, from the Australian Signals Directorate’s Australian Cyber Security Centre, co-sealed by CISA, NSA, FBI and agencies from seven other countries. Principle 1, Safety is paramount, asks whether backups can be trusted if a malicious actor has been on the network for a period of time, and whether there is a way to validate that a critical OT system is safe after recovery, which bears on the backup and safety items in this checklist.
