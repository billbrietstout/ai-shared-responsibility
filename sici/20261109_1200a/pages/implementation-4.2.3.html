Implementation 4.2.3: Human-Centered Interfaces for SI Uncertainty and Actionable Triggers

Design Human-System or Human-Machine Interfaces (HSIs or HMIs), such as operational dashboards, clinical decision-support tools, fraud triage queues, network management displays, or field interfaces, to communicate AI system state and confidence bounds while avoiding cognitive overload. Examples of features that should be considered include:

  • Calibrated indications of uncertainty: Present recommendations alongside visual confidence bands, data freshness indicators, and out-of-distribution warnings (e.g., flagging unhistorical conditions in finance, novel patient demographics in healthcare, or uncharacteristic network traffic in telecommunications) so personnel can quickly judge how far to rely on the output. Confidence bands are presented alongside, not instead of, the discrete triage indicators below; the NERC white paper asks that systems show their confidence intervals, while noting that those intervals usually assume the data matches the training distribution (see NERC 2024).
  • Mode and authority: Show persistently which mode the SI system is in and what it is currently permitted to do, such as acting autonomously, advising only, or paused, and make changes of mode conspicuous.
  • Recent and pending actions: Where the SI system acts rather than only advises, show what it has recently done and, where it has a defined plan or queue, what it intends to do next, so that personnel can see the cumulative effect and intervene before a pending action takes effect.
  • Actionable Triage Indicators: Provide standardized visual cues that explicitly signal the required human response:
  • Proceed (high confidence, within baseline bounds),
  • Investigate (moderate confidence, novel inputs/telemetry),
  • Escalate (low confidence, cross-system anomaly), or
  • Override Immediately (boundary or policy constraint violation).

A Proceed cue indicates that the output falls within the conditions the AI system was validated for, not that it is correct.

  • Information needed to act: Alongside a recommendation, show what acting on it is expected to change, which systems or dependencies it affects, any prerequisites, how urgent it is, the risk of following or deferring it, and how to reverse it, so that a recommendation personnel find credible is also one they can act on.

Displayed confidence metrics and data freshness warnings should be derived from the AI system’s decision making process (Implementation 4.6.2: Derive Confidence and Rationale for Learned Components from Their Decision Processes). Where the interface also displays a rationale for a recommendation, see Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output. For TEVV protocols for performance baselining, see Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which outputs show continuous confidence figures and which show discrete triage cues? (see the Explainable & Interpretable versus Safe tension in Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness)

References:

  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In Chapter 3, Contract Between Humans and AI/ML asks that users understand how information, including confidence intervals, will be conveyed to them and how they will be alerted to time-sensitive information. Lack of Generalizability and Recognition of Acceptable Input Ranges ask that systems warn users when inputs do not match the patterns they were trained on, defer to a human outside that range, and show their confidence intervals, noting that those intervals themselves usually assume the data matches the training distribution.
  • Guidance FAA AC 120-71B, Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers, 10 January 2017, from the United States Federal Aviation Administration, the civil aviation authority of the United States. Paragraph 6.9.1 reports that safety data show autoflight mode confusion to be a potential vulnerability in flight operations. Paragraph 6.9.2 describes the flight mode annunciator as the source for depicting “who is doing what”. Both bear on the mode and authority indication in this Implementation.
