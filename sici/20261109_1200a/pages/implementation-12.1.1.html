Implementation 12.1.1: Artifact Risk Classification

Classifying generated artifacts by consequence helps an organization spend its review effort where an error would matter most. Categorize AI-generated logic and outputs (artifacts) based primarily on consequence severity (see Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses). Proximity to execution and influence on human decisions should be used to determine the validation procedures. To ensure appropriate validation velocity, organizations should explicitly distinguish between ‘Direct-Execution Logic’ (which directly commands physical or digital assets like Programmable Logic Controller (PLC) code or system configurations, requiring automated validation gates) and ‘Human-Mediated Logic’ (which influences human choices like procedural summaries or diagnostic reports, relying on structured expert review workflows). Artifacts such as PLC code, Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) configurations or automated control parameters may carry the highest execution risk as they have close proximity to execution. Artifacts that implement or could defeat a safety or protection function, such as safety instrumented logic, protective relay settings or an interlock bypass procedure, can be placed in a class of their own above these. Where such a function already carries a safety integrity level or an equivalent assignment, a generated artifact for it can be held to the rigor that assignment already requires. Where an artifact’s class is uncertain, the higher candidate class can be assigned until the question is resolved. The class should reflect what the artifact can reach and how it may come to be used, not only its intended use. For example, an analysis script that holds write access to a control network, or that is later scheduled to run against live systems, belongs in a higher class than its original purpose suggests. Revisit the class when an artifact’s use or deployment changes, such as when a draft summary is adopted as the procedure itself.

For example:

  • AI-generated valve control logic is likely to be classified as a High Risk, Direct Execution artifact.
  • Artifacts such as procedure summaries, condition reports or operational recommendations carry risk in proportion to the consequence of human actions they may influence.
  • An SI-generated summary of a maintenance procedure is likely to be classified as a High Risk Human Mediated artifact because of the safety consequences if it is incorrect. A draft internal email summary of this same procedure that is subject to ongoing human brainstorming and refinement might be classified as Low Risk.
  • An SI system that sets a priority, severity, category or routing value in an operational record produces Direct-Execution Logic wherever automated workflows act on that field without human review, such as a service-level clock, a dispatch rule or an escalation path. The consequence follows from what the downstream automation does with the value, not from the apparent triviality of the artifact, and the automation reading the field may predate the SI system (see Implementation 3.5.1: Map Direct Functional and Algorithmic Dependencies and, for verification, Implementation 3.2.5: Artifact Verification).
  • An SI-drafted public emergency alert or mass notification is Human Mediated, since a person approves its release, but it cannot be recalled once sent and acts directly on the public. It is likely to be classified as a High Risk Human Mediated artifact that warrants validation as rigorous as that applied to Direct Execution artifacts.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which risk classes are used, and do artifacts that implement or could defeat a safety or protection function get a class of their own?
  • What triggers a review of an artifact’s class, such as a change in its use or deployment?

References:

  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, Version 2026, from the OWASP GenAI Security Project, a volunteer community project of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. LLM07:2026, Misinformation, defines the risk as incorrect, incomplete or misleading output that appears credible enough to influence a human decision, an automated workflow or an agent action, and lists misleading summaries and critical omissions among its examples. Its distinction between output that informs a decision and output that drives an action parallels this Implementation’s distinction between Human-Mediated and Direct-Execution Logic.
