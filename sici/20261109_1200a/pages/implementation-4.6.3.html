Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components

Where the AI system, or part of it, reasons symbolically, for example by applying encoded rules, constraints, or a knowledge base, or is a self-interpretable model such as a decision tree, the displayed rationale can be a trace of the steps it actually applied rather than a hypothesis constructed after the fact (see Sheh 2021). For a planner or optimizer, such as a tool that proposes a switching sequence or a generation schedule, the trace can include the constraints that bound the solution and the alternatives it rejected. In a hybrid system, such a trace is an accurate explanation only of the symbolic part: facts supplied by learned components, such as a classification of a vibration signature as a bearing fault, enter the trace as premises and should be displayed with their own uncertainty (Implementation 4.6.2: Derive Confidence and Rationale for Learned Components from Their Decision Processes). For an agent that orchestrates tools, the recorded sequence of tool calls, their arguments and their returns is a trace of what it did, though not of why; the narrative it gives alongside is a presentation layer, as in Implementation 4.6.2: Derive Confidence and Rationale for Learned Components from Their Decision Processes. Such a trace shows how the AI system reached its output, not that its rules are correct. The trace should be produced by the same execution that produced the output, rather than reconstructed afterward, and recorded with it (Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking). Whether the displayed trace matches the logic actually executed can be checked in acceptance testing and again after changes to the rule base or software. Where a trace is too long to use under time pressure, it can be condensed by a deterministic approximation with known error, and the display should indicate that it has been condensed. For when such a design might be weighed against a learned model, see Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output.

References:

  • Research Sheh, R., “Explainable Artificial Intelligence Requirements for Safe, Intelligent Robots”, Proceedings of the 2021 IEEE International Conference on Intelligence and Safety for Robotics (ISR), pages 382 to 387. A position paper on specifying explainability requirements for safety-critical robots. It distinguishes introspective explanations, drawn from the process that made the decision, from post-hoc explanations, and argues that local post-hoc methods such as LIME and SHAP hold only near the case being explained, within bounds that are hard to determine. It identifies decision trees, inductive logic programming, and models built on domain knowledge as able to give introspective explanations, argues that the trade-off against accuracy should be made deliberately, and notes that each stage of a system, from sensing to decision making, can have different explainability requirements. The paper does not address how explanations should be presented to operators.
