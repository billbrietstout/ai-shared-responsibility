Task 1.4: Establish the SI System’s Domain Knowledge and Linguistic Alignment Requirements

For AI systems that interact in natural language (including chatbots, summarization tools, schematic/diagram parsers, and user interfaces that generate unique textual or graphical human output), identify and document domain-specific knowledge, industry terminology, operational ontologies, graphical conventions, and community context that the AI system needs to understand in order to perform its intended function accurately and safely.

Especially for multimodal and language models trained on large, general datasets, success criteria should include a verified understanding of sector-specific jargon, graphical symbols, units of measure and sign conventions, operational nuances, and community variations to language, particularly where colloquial variations exist or where domain-specific conventions can be expected to diverge from those of the broader community. For example, an open circuit breaker interrupts current, while an open valve permits flow.

Success criteria can also cover how the AI system behaves at the edge of its domain knowledge, such as whether it flags a term or question that it does not recognize rather than resolving it to a familiar meaning. Where outputs carry exact identifiers, such as equipment tags or procedure numbers, the criteria can require that they be reproduced exactly rather than paraphrased.

For high-consequence applications, including otherwise benign AI systems, such as chatbots, or parsers of scanned technical diagrams that provide outputs that are used for high-consequence decisions, verification of understanding should be commensurate with the consequence. This may exclude post-hoc statistical explanations that are not guaranteed to reflect the AI system’s understanding (see Implementation 4.6.2 and Implementation 8.1.2) and/or necessitate additional verification of the AI system’s outputs. This helps ensure that the system’s outputs, whether in optimization, predictive maintenance, digital twins, report summarization, or AI-generated code, procedures, and configurations, are aligned with the precise technical, conceptual, safety, and execution logic requirements of the industry and the community it serves.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Valid: Contributes the sector knowledge requirement: the terminology, operational ontologies, graphical conventions and community context an AI system needs to hold for this industry, written as success criteria, verified in proportion to consequence, and with post-hoc statistical explanation ruled out as evidence of understanding. This Task does not cover testing a delivered system against those criteria. For that, see Task 2.4: Perform Linguistic and Domain-Context Robustness Testing.
  • Accurate: Contributes contextual accuracy as a stated benchmark rather than general fluency: whether sector terms, symbols and priorities are read as the sector uses them, so that a plausible summary which misreads a critical alert fails a criterion (Implementation 1.4.2: Administrative & Support). This Task does not cover verifying the artifacts the system generates once they exist. For that, see Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle.
  • Fair: Contributes comprehension across community and colloquial language variation as an explicit success criterion, so that uneven understanding across the populations an operator serves fails a stated requirement instead of passing unremarked. This Task does not cover evaluating unevenness in the decisions that follow or in the outcomes they produce.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accurate versus Explainable & Interpretable: Where a domain requirement can be written as a checkable distinction, such as separating a forced outage from planned maintenance (Implementation 1.4.1: Maintenance & Operations), demonstrated understanding and measured accuracy move together. Where the requirement is tacit institutional knowledge (Implementation 1.4.4: Requirements for SI-Generated Code, Configurations, Procedures, and Other Logic), a system can score well on the part that was written down, while the evidence that it holds the rest is exactly what this Task declines to take from post-hoc statistical explanation. One way to balance this is to raise verification of the outputs themselves in proportion to consequence, which this Task provides for. Others include writing the tacit knowledge down before the requirement is set, or limiting the system’s role to the part of the domain that can be stated. For where a stated part could be implemented symbolically, see Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 2.2 (on defining AI system utility and oversight needs): This Task documents the sector terminology, operational ontologies and graphical conventions that a system must hold, so that its knowledge limits can be stated for one industry rather than in general; it does not cover defining the oversight mode applied where those limits are reached. For that, see for example Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation.
  • MEASURE 2.9 (on measuring interpretability and explainability): This Task requires understanding to be verified in proportion to consequence, and treats post-hoc statistical explanations as insufficient evidence that the system understood the domain; it does not cover whether the confidence and rationale shown to an operator at run time reflect how the output was produced. For that, see for example Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output.
  • MEASURE 2.11 (on measuring fairness, accuracy, and bias): This Task makes comprehension of community and colloquial language variation an explicit success criterion, so that uneven understanding across communities fails a stated requirement instead of passing unremarked; it does not cover evaluating bias in the system’s decisions or in the outcomes they produce.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which domain distinctions are written as checkable requirements, and which tacit knowledge needs expert review instead? (see the Accurate versus Explainable & Interpretable tension in Task 1.4: Establish the SI System’s Domain Knowledge and Linguistic Alignment Requirements)
  • Whose time is set aside for eliciting tacit knowledge and agreeing reference interpretations, and how is it balanced against their operational duties?
