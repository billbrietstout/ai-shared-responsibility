Implementation 11.5.2: Execute High-Fidelity Simulation Drills Involving Total SI Infrastructure Loss and Recovery

A tabletop exercise shows what people would decide; a full simulation shows whether they can actually carry it out, at the pace a real outage demands. Beyond tabletop exercises, periodically run a full simulation of total AI infrastructure loss using digital twins or dedicated test environments. Include at least one scenario where the underlying cause is not immediately apparent, to confirm personnel can maintain continuity while the nature of the failure, accidental or deliberate, is still being determined. Such a scenario can also test how the organization balances preserving forensic evidence against restoring service. Carry the drill through recovery and reconstitution to a known state, not only the switch to manual operation. Where a full exercise would disrupt an operational system, planned and unplanned maintenance windows can offer a chance to exercise parts of it. Hold these drills after the tabletop exercises in Implementation 11.5.1: Conduct “Fail-to-Manual” Tabletop Exercises for Key Personnel, so that personnel are already trained in their roles. A simulation that leaves out behavior the real system would show, such as a protection action, can leave gaps in what personnel learn from it.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How does the drill balance preserving forensic evidence against restoring service?
  • Which maintenance windows are used to exercise parts of operational systems?
  • Which form of exercise suits each part, such as a high-fidelity simulation, a tabletop working from retained production records or past incidents, or a checkpoint of the production system, and what does each leave untested? (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks)

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-34r1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems, May 2010, from NIST. Section 3.5.3 distinguishes discussion-based tabletop exercises from functional exercises, in which personnel perform their duties in a simulated operational environment, up to full-scale exercises. Section 3.5.4 asks that a full-scale functional exercise include a full recovery and reconstitution to a known state, and notes that maintenance activities can offer opportunities to exercise operational systems such as ICS, which bears on the scope and timing of these drills.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-184, Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery, December 2016, from NIST. Section 2.3.3 describes a coordinated response that balances effective forensic investigation against business service restoration, and asks organizations to define the conditions under which the recovery plan is invoked and who has the authority to invoke it, which bears on the unknown-cause scenario in this drill.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-84, Guide to Test, Training, and Exercise Programs for IT Plans and Capabilities, September 2006, from NIST. Section 5 covers functional exercises, run in real or near-real time in a simulated operational environment. Section 5.1 advises that staff training and tabletop exercises take place before a functional exercise, and Section 5.2.2 allows the scope to focus on one phase of the plan, such as activation, operation or reconstitution, which bears on how these drills are sequenced and scoped.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In Chapter 3, Training with High-Fidelity Simulations holds that simulations must mimic the real-world systems and scenarios operators will face as closely as possible. Its example is a simulator that models overcurrent relay trips but ignores under- and over-frequency relay action. Such a gap could leave holes in operator training, which bears on the fidelity of the digital twins used in these drills.
