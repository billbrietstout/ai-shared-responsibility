Task 8.1: Identify Scenarios Requiring Deterministic Root Cause Analysis and Incident Reconstruction

Knowing in advance which failures must be reconstructable helps an organization choose SI systems that support those investigations and focus forensic effort where it matters most. Define the specific applications, operational thresholds, and safety-critical triggers that mandate a comprehensive, deterministic reconstruction of an AI-driven failure, for both post-event investigation as well as to provide situational awareness for immediate-term incident response. Applications where return to service after an incident depends on showing that its cause has been corrected, as in the checklist of Implementation 11.4.1: Define a Checklist for Validating System Safety Before Re-Engaging SI Control, are among these. Existing obligations to find a cause, for example incident investigation under process safety management rules or the identification of the causes of protection system misoperations under electric reliability standards, can also indicate where these triggers lie. Incident response criteria should explicitly account for both the AI Attack Surface and the AI Risk Surface (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface), helping ensure triage procedures can differentiate between adversarial cyber exploits and non-adversarial failures.

This Task helps the organization use, for applications that require forensic root cause analysis, AI systems that support such investigations. It also helps prioritize forensic resources for high-impact events and supports meeting the evidentiary standards that regulatory and safety oversight require. Here, deterministic reconstruction means being able to replay what the AI system received and produced, including its inputs, versions and settings, in a forensic investigation setting. This is distinct from explaining why it produced that output. The reconstruction needed can differ by scenario, for example full replay, a record of the inputs and retrieved documents that an output drew on, the full sequence of an agent’s actions and tool calls, or only what the SI system recommended and what the operator did. Reconstruction can be needed after the SI system has been updated or retired, so the model version, prompts, configuration, and other state and information that influenced the output at the time need keeping (Implementation 10.1.5: Define What Each Operational Record Is For; see also Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority).

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Accountable: Contributes identification, for each AI application, of where a failure must be reconstructable to the evidentiary standard of regulatory and safety oversight, with systems that cannot support this substituted, compensated or bounded by handover (Implementation 8.1.1: Evaluate SI Applications), and with post-hoc attribution such as SHAP and LIME excluded as forensic proof (Implementation 8.1.2: Identify SI Systems that May Not Satisfy Root Cause Analysis Requirements). This Task does not cover capturing the inputs, prompts and metadata reconstruction draws on, which Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies covers.
  • Secure: Contributes triage pathways that isolate whether an anomaly originated on the attack surface or in the non-adversarial part of the risk surface (Implementation 8.1.6: Incident Triage Across the Dual Attack/Risk Surface), so that an intrusion is not dismissed as drift. This Task does not cover detecting the anomaly at runtime, which Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior covers.
  • Reliable: Contributes the other half of the same triage, so that drift, sensor schema variance or a multi-agent cascade is not misclassified as an intrusion and left uncorrected while response effort goes elsewhere. This Task does not cover setting the drift thresholds that define when degradation requires action, which Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Telemetry that spans agent boundaries, capturing inter-agent communications and context window handoffs (Implementation 8.1.5: Multi-Agent Cascade Reconstruction Capabilities), can carry personal and sensitive data that passed through those agents. Implementation 10.1.3: Forensic Integrity and Privacy-Preserving Log Partitioning sets out the masking and the logically separated storage that keep such records out of routine operational view.
  • Secure: The list of SI systems whose failures cannot be reconstructed (Implementation 8.1.2: Identify SI Systems that May Not Satisfy Root Cause Analysis Requirements), and the triage pathways that decide whether an anomaly is treated as drift or as an attack (Implementation 8.1.6: Incident Triage Across the Dual Attack/Risk Surface), show where an attack would be hardest to trace and how to make one look like a non-adversarial failure. An attacker could use them to choose a target and to disguise an intrusion as drift. Protecting this material is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accountable versus Accurate: Where an SI system’s version and settings are already fixed, replaying it costs little. Where replay depends on holding back a newer version, or on disabling sampling in a generative component, it can keep a known defect in service or change output quality. Options include fixing versions and settings to the degree of reconstruction each scenario needs under this Task, and relying on records made at the time of the output (Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies) where full replay is not needed.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Task covers the criteria and triage pathways that let AI-driven failures be tracked and responded to, and the drills that keep reconstruction usable during a live incident; it does not cover communicating incidents outside the organization. For that, see for example Task 8.4: Define External Reporting and Regulatory Disclosure Protocols Following SI Incidents.
  • MEASURE 2.9 (on measuring interpretability and explainability): This Task informs how AI output is interpreted after an incident, by excluding post-hoc attribution such as SHAP and LIME as forensic proof of cause; it does not cover setting how outputs are explained to personnel during operation. For that, see for example Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which applications and scenarios call for deterministic reconstruction, and how much reconstruction does each need?
  • How far are versions and settings held fixed for replay? (see the Accountable versus Accurate tension in Task 8.1: Identify Scenarios Requiring Deterministic Root Cause Analysis and Incident Reconstruction)
  • Who decides that an incident meets a reconstruction trigger, and do they have the authority to hold back restoration while evidence is preserved?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-86, Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response, August 2006, from NIST. Section 2.4.3, Supporting Forensics in the Information System Life Cycle, notes that many incidents can be handled more efficiently and effectively if forensic considerations have been incorporated into the information system life cycle. Its examples include enabling auditing, forwarding audit records to secure centralized log servers and keeping configuration baselines. Written for conventional IT systems, it bears on choosing, before deployment, SI systems that support the investigations this Task identifies.
