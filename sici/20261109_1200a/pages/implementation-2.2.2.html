Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers

An independent watchdog helps an organization detect, at machine speed, that an SI system has stopped performing as required, and fail over without waiting for a person to notice. Watchdog controllers, also referred to as “enforcement applications,” are based on independent, deterministic architectures that monitor AI output and automatically perform fail-over, consistent with Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior. Deploy independent watchdog controllers to continuously monitor AI system health, execution latency, setpoint stability, and output integrity. Output integrity checks can include plausibility against the state and capacity of the physical process, for example flagging a recommended flow above what the installed pumps can deliver. They can also include agreement with an independent estimate, such as a simple physical model or a conventional controller running in parallel, with sustained divergence between the two flagged. Checks on the inputs that the AI system relies on, for freshness, range and expected source, can help catch a fault before it reaches the output.

To help keep watchdog mechanisms from introducing unmanaged complexity, secondary attack surfaces, or weaponized failover vectors, organizations should enforce:

  • Determinism (Non-AI Assurance): Watchdog logic should be strictly non-AI, lightweight, and deterministic. Example implementations may include dedicated hardware timers, separate Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) or microcontrollers, with minimal, formally verified software logic that shares as little as practical with the primary AI application stack, and where anything is shared, records what it is (see How Far to Go). Note that a notionally deterministic system can still exhibit non-deterministic behavior due to input/sensor noise, errors, wear, etc.
  • Independence and Tamper Resistance: Watchdog controllers should operate out-of-band on hardened, privileged infrastructure with read-only telemetry access, helping ensure that an upstream malfunction or mistaken change, as well as an upstream compromise (of the AI system or otherwise), prompt injection, or network intrusion, cannot modify watchdog setpoints, disable execution checks, or corrupt trip logic. Note that the watchdog itself may be part of the attack surface as discussed in Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.
  • Mitigation of Destructive/Forced Failover: Watchdog trigger thresholds should still incorporate state-aware interlocks and rate-of-change validation to help prevent corrupted inputs or malicious actors from forcing an unintended failover during sensitive operational windows (e.g., during active process transitions or critical control maneuvers). Thresholds can be tuned by running the watchdog in a monitoring-only mode alongside the AI system before it is given authority to trigger fail-over, with its false and missed trips reviewed periodically after that. A watchdog that trips too readily is at risk of being disabled or bypassed, as alarms that cause nuisance have been in process plants.

How the watchdog responds when it loses contact with the AI system, such as a missed heartbeat or stale telemetry, is a choice to make deliberately for each system and to record with its reason. Failing toward the fail-safe state is the usual default, but where an abrupt move to it during an active process transition is itself the hazard, loss of contact can instead pass through the same state-aware interlocks, which may delay reaching the fallback. The record of tradeoffs described in Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs is one place to keep that choice.

Triggers should initiate safe, deterministic fallback sequences (Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior) rather than uncoordinated abrupt shutdowns. Ensure the watchdog itself is included within Implementation 3.4.3: Monitor Validation Control Integrity. Notify operators of any watchdog-triggered fail-over per Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness, so that a failure the watchdog catches does not go unnoticed by operators.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How does each watchdog respond when it loses contact with the SI system, and where is that choice recorded?
  • How are trip thresholds tuned between missed trips and false trips?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 2.3.7, Safety Systems, describes a safety instrumented system as often independent from all other control systems, so that a failure of the basic process control system will not impact its functionality, and historically as physically and logically separated from the rest of the control system. That is the independence this Implementation asks of a watchdog relative to the AI application stack.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control AC-25, Reference Monitor, is written for access control enforcement, but it specifies a mechanism that is tamperproof, always invoked, and small enough to be subject to analysis and testing, which are the properties this Implementation asks of watchdog logic. Control enhancement SI-13(5), Failover Capability, provides real-time or near real-time automatic switchover to an alternate system upon failure of the primary system.
  • Research Bransby, M. L., and Jenkinson, J., The management of alarm systems, Contract Research Report 166/1998, funded by the Health and Safety Executive, Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. Volume 1, Section 2.2, Incidents reported, describes a refinery near miss in which operators were overwhelmed by alarms and the alarm hooter had been disconnected because of the nuisance it caused. This bears on tuning watchdog trip thresholds so that the watchdog is not bypassed.
