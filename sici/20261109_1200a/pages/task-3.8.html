Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface

To show what exposure an SI deployment adds and where controls are needed, catalog the AI Risk Surface introduced by AI deployment across all lifecycle stages. It includes both the adversarial exploitation vectors (the AI Attack Surface) and the non-adversarial, systemic vulnerability vectors. Evaluate how SI systems, whose outputs can vary or fail in ways that are hard to predict, expand operational risk beyond traditional cybersecurity boundaries to encompass all SICI Trustworthiness Characteristics described in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table. Who carries out this evaluation is for the organization to decide. It calls for technical knowledge of the SI system, and also for a view across the Trustworthiness Characteristics, which can be wider than any one technical function covers. Record who performed it. For example, an agent that reads incoming vendor email and can raise work orders can be steered by instructions placed in that email, a path that perimeter defenses see only as ordinary email.

To enable the evaluation of the reachable risk surface of third-party AI components, vendors should provide the disclosures, described in Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures, to formally declare which upstream model limitations are operationally contained before deployment.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure: Contributes the catalog of AI-specific adversarial exposure: the interfaces at which a crafted input can reach inference, from direct and indirect prompt injection to training data poisoning, model extraction and tool invocation. This Task does not cover placing controls at the interfaces it finds. For those, see Task 3.2 and Practice 5.
  • Safe: Contributes the half of the picture where harm arises with no attacker present: feedback loops or distribution shifts driving controllers out of limits, plausible hallucinations in generated logic, and opaque pathways that slow a response. This Task does not cover weighing the consequence of each exposure, which Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The catalog states where an AI system can be reached and which hazards a deployment has left reachable, which an adversary who obtains it could use to choose where to attack. How the catalog is classified and handled is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations, under its existing information classification and handling practice. Wide access helps those who have to act on it, and every additional reader is another route by which it can reach an adversary. Record the handling decided on.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Safe: Where a hazard has been shown unreachable in this deployment and the control that makes it so is stable, treating it as contained lets attention go to the deviations that can occur. Where autonomy is widened, one agent is chained to another, or the operating environment moves, the same hazard can become reachable while the record still shows it contained. One way to balance this is the periodic coupling analysis in Implementation 3.8.3: Dynamic Risk Surface Coupling Analysis, which treats a widened risk surface as a trigger to re-evaluate the guardrails a containment claim rested on; others include time-bounding a containment claim, or re-confirming it whenever guardrail scope changes.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 4.1 (on understanding AI component-level risks): This Task catalogs the adversarial vectors that can reach AI inference logic, including direct and indirect prompt injection, data poisoning, model extraction and malicious tool invocation; it does not cover obtaining the vendor statements that show which upstream model limitations remain reachable in deployment. For those, see for example Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures.
  • MEASURE 2.7 (on measuring security and resiliency): This Task covers the attack and risk surface that a security and resilience evaluation is scoped against, which is the identification MEASURE 2.7 refers back to the MAP function for; it does not cover carrying out that evaluation. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).
  • GOVERN 1.2 (on defining trustworthy AI policies): This Task turns each Trustworthiness Characteristic into a category of operational exposure to be mapped, so that safety, explainability, privacy, fairness and accuracy are examined as vulnerability surfaces rather than as aspirations; it does not cover defining the characteristic set or the terms used for it.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who carries out the risk-surface evaluation?
  • How is the catalog classified and handled?
  • Where the evaluation needs expertise the organization does not hold, where does it come from, and which characteristics are covered less thoroughly as a result?

References:

  • Guidance Mitigating Artificial Intelligence (AI) Risk: Safety and Security Guidelines for Critical Infrastructure Owners and Operators, April 2024, from the U.S. Department of Homeland Security. Its cross-sector analysis sets out three categories of system-level AI risk: attacks using AI, attacks targeting AI systems, and failures in AI design and implementation. Appendix A lists subcategories of each, including brittleness, inscrutability, overreliance by human operators and statistical bias among design and implementation failures. The second and third categories correspond to the attack surface and the non-adversarial risk surface this Task catalogs.
