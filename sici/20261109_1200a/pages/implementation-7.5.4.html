Implementation 7.5.4: Audit Interoperability for Hardware or Data Substrate Substitution

Treating a like-for-like replacement as a change helps the organization check whether a replacement component, data feed, or computing platform that meets its specification changes how an SI system behaves. Establish a change-management trigger within the internal supply chain for when physical operational components or software data feeds (e.g., valves, pumps, switches, or sensors, or API schemas, electronic health record modules, or transaction journals) are replaced with equivalents from a different vendor or manufacturer. A change in the manufacturer or model recorded for the asset in the asset inventory can serve as this trigger. A substitution of this kind is a form of drift that comes from the supply chain rather than from the process the SI system observes (see Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds). The same trigger applies to the SI system’s own substrate. A change of processor or accelerator, inference runtime or driver, or numerical precision, such as quantization to 8-bit integers, can change its behavior even when the model has not been retrained. For a replaced sensor, historian or protocol gateway, the checks can include units and scaling, sampling rate, timestamp handling, tag mapping, and the encoding of missing or bad-quality values. A difference in any of these can produce inputs that appear valid but were never tested (see also the runtime input checks in Implementation 7.2.4: Select and Prepare the Inputs Each SI System Receives).

Even if the replacement part complies perfectly with existing engineering specifications, the internal supply chain process should mandate a regression test of any governing AI systems to help ensure the vendor swap does not invalidate undocumented physical dependencies embedded within the AI system’s learned model as discussed in Task 2.5: Verify that Learned Behavior Is Robust to Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance. If mandating full regression testing for every replacement-in-kind substitution is not feasible given the volume of routine component swaps in normal maintenance, organizations can consider the following guidance for action:

  • If the substituted component has no plausible link to the AI system, such as an unrelated office HVAC sensor, standard engineering sign-off is sufficient.
  • If the component is one the AI system depends on or influences but outside any safety-critical path, such as a sensor on a non-critical secondary line, flag it for engineering review under Task 2.5: Verify that Learned Behavior Is Robust to Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance rather than a full regression test.
  • If the component sits within a safety-critical control path, such as a valve actuator on a shutdown system or a sensor feeding a chemical batch temperature loop, require full regression testing before deployment.

Where it is unclear which of these cases a component falls in, full regression testing remains available as the more conservative choice.

Regression testing after a substitution can also confirm that latency and throughput still meet control-loop or operator-response requirements, and that guardrails, logging and monitoring still function. Record the hardware, runtime, numerical precision and data interfaces with the model version in the configuration baseline, so that the tested configuration can be identified and a later discrepancy traced to the substitution.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who decides which tier a substituted component falls in?

References:

  • Guidance Adapting Zero Trust Principles to Operational Technology, 29 April 2026, from CISA, the Department of War and the Department of Energy with partners. The Configuration and Change Management section asks that all changes follow a formalized Management of Change process, typically with a multi-disciplinary review board covering engineering peer review, safety review, and size, weight, power and material review, followed by formal authorization and documentation. A substitution trigger under this Implementation can route through that existing process.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Appendix F.7.5, the OT discussion of CM-3, Configuration Change Control, states that configuration change control procedures should align with the organization’s management of change practices. This bears on placing the substitution trigger within existing change management rather than beside it.
  • Guidance Foundations for OT Cybersecurity: Asset Inventory Guidance for Owners and Operators, 13 August 2025, from CISA with the EPA, NSA, FBI and partner agencies in Australia, Canada, Germany, the Netherlands and New Zealand. It lists manufacturer and model among the high-priority attributes to collect for each asset, and under Continuous Improvement asks that change management processes track OT asset modifications, additions and decommissioning. A change in the recorded manufacturer or model is a point at which the substitution trigger in this Implementation can fire.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, Version 2026, August 4, 2026, from the OWASP GenAI Security Project, a volunteer community project. LLM04:2026, Supply Chain, notes under compromised conversion, merge and quantization workflows that model weights can be crafted so that the full-precision model evaluates benignly while the quantized artifact behaves as an attacker chooses, so full-precision assurances do not transfer to the deployed quantized artifact. This bears on treating a change of numerical precision as a substitution under this Implementation.
