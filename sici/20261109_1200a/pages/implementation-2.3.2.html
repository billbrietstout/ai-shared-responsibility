Implementation 2.3.2: Targeted Failure Mode Mapping

Map the highest risk AI-specific behaviors in response to both malicious attack (e.g., attacks listed in MITRE ATLAS and NIST AI 100-2e2025) as well as unintentional reasons (e.g., model drift). Existing hazard analysis methods, such as process hazard analysis (PHA) and failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA), can be extended to cover the SI system’s outputs. Familiar failure modes, such as an output that is missing, wrong, late, or repeated too often, apply to them, alongside those particular to SI, such as drift or manipulated inputs. Candidate failure modes can also come from past incidents and near-misses and from the experience of operators, as well as from attack catalogs. Recording for each mapped failure mode how it would be detected, and testing that detection and the fallback response, can help show which failure modes would otherwise pass unnoticed. Conduct regular adversarial testing of AI systems to verify system behavior and proactively adjust or implement risk management controls where appropriate. Adversarial testing should include the enforcement layer itself, such as the guardrails of Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs, the watchdog controllers of Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers and the intercept points of Implementation 3.2.1: Identify SI Output Intercept Points, under an adversary able to observe and adapt to those controls rather than one that treats them as fixed. A failure mode map made for one trained version of an AI system may not carry over to the next. Versions trained by the same pipeline, differing only in arbitrary choices such as the random seed, can score the same on standard test data and still behave differently under stress tests. Repeating the targeted tests after each retraining can help reveal this.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Sections 2 and 3 provide the attack taxonomies for predictive and generative systems that this Implementation names as a source of malicious failure modes. Section 3.3.3, discussing evaluations of generative models against direct prompting attacks, notes that evaluations measure vulnerabilities at a particular moment in time and that continuous evaluation following deployment can help, which supports this Implementation’s call for regular rather than one-off adversarial testing.
  • Research Underspecification Presents Challenges for Credibility in Modern Machine Learning, by A. D’Amour, K. Heller, D. Moldovan and others, mostly from Google, arXiv preprint version 2, November 2020. Its introduction and Section 2.3 report that predictors from pipelines that differ only in arbitrary choices, such as the random seed, perform alike on standard evaluations but differ on stress tests. The authors find this in computer vision, medical imaging, natural language processing and health-record applications. This bears on repeating failure mode tests after retraining rather than carrying the map forward.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 4.1.1, Framing OT Risk, suggests that organizations consider incorporating cyber attack and IT failure scenarios into their process hazard analysis (PHA) or failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA) processes. Section 6.1.3 adds that a PHA, FMEA or analysis of past events can be used to understand the potential impact of a cyber incident. Both parallel extending the same methods to AI-specific failure modes.
