Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations

Re-evaluation helps an organization catch a loss of SI performance before it causes harm, including a slow loss that no single check would reveal. Establish periodic and trigger-based re-evaluation protocols to detect distribution drift, being the gradual or sudden deviation of real-world operational environments, seasonal population changes, physical sensor responses, equipment dynamics, mission tasks, or the supply chain, such as a vendor’s model update or a changed data source, from the conditions captured during model training and initial validation. This applies to both OT environments, such as optimization based on sensor data, as well as IT and office environments, such as summarizing medical reports where content and language distributions may vary depending on factors such as seasonal ailments, the arrival of new diseases and treatments, or changes in social media trends.

Because unmanaged drift can cause silent degradation of decision accuracy or violate safe operating boundaries, continuous monitoring and recurrent evaluation should be structured across two operational phases to establish empirical drift baselines:

Phase 1: Commissioning and Baselining (Burn-In Period)
Upon initial deployment or major re-training, the system should enter an initial high-frequency monitoring window (e.g., real-time telemetry checks, hourly/daily batch evaluations, scaled to the severity of consequences). This phase captures live field data to establish empirical baselines for operational drift, input distribution shifts, and real-world system decay under actual operating conditions.

Phase 2: Adaptive Recurrent Evaluation
Once empirical divergence behaviors are established during Phase 1, ongoing evaluation cadences should be set to a fraction (e.g., one-quarter) of the empirically measured Time to Divergence, commensurate with the consequence of undetected divergence. If operational drift accelerates or un-modeled environmental shifts occur, the evaluation interval should dynamically scale down to match the accelerated decay rate. Where Time to Divergence is measured as a loss of accuracy, it needs confirmed outcomes, and in some applications, such as predictive maintenance, alarm prioritization or fraud triage, outcomes are confirmed late or only for some cases. There, measures that need no confirmed outcome, such as shifts in the input distribution or in how often operators override the AI system, can set the cadence, with accuracy measured as outcomes become available. Where the same process or function already has periodic reviews, such as process hazard analysis revalidation, functional safety assessment or model risk review, these evaluations can be scheduled alongside them.

Event-Driven Triggers:
Independent of scheduled time intervals, re-verification should be triggered by events such as the following, with its depth scaled to the change and to the consequence of undetected divergence: Upstream data schema shifts, sensor replacements, or physical equipment retrofits. Safety interlock trips, watchdog fallback events (Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers), or out-of-bounds operational excursions. External events that change the conditions the AI system relies on, such as a regional weather event, a port closure, or the failure of a major supplier. Vendor model updates, upstream component patches, or security vulnerability disclosures (Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures). A change in how the AI system is used, such as a new site, a new decision type or a change in shift pattern. An incident or near-miss involving a comparable AI system elsewhere, such as one shared under Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning. Continuous telemetry otherwise indicates that actual drift velocity is accelerating faster than the estimated baseline. Other changes that the responsible role judges could affect whether the AI system’s earlier evaluation still holds. This is a scheduled, batch-level evaluation; see Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior for continuous, runtime anomaly detection and Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds for the specific drift thresholds that trigger fail-safe responses. Where historical incident or drift data is too limited to support a confident quantitative estimate, organizations should use the best available approximation (e.g., expert judgment or analogous systems) and revise the cadence as real operational data accumulates, rather than treating the initial estimate as fixed.

To execute these evaluations without impacting availability or safety, organizations should follow Implementation 1.3.2: Categorization of Testing Environments. Systems should be tested against the “validated standard” datasets maintained under Implementation 2.3.1: Baseline SI System Performance to quantify statistical drift in the inputs, concept drift in the machine-learning sense (a change in the relationship between inputs and the correct output), and shifts in error distributions (such as false positive/negative rates). Signals from operations can also be compared across successive evaluations, such as how often operators override or dismiss the AI system’s outputs, or a narrowing margin between alarm and trip points. Involving people who did not develop or deploy the AI system in these evaluations, as AI RMF MEASURE 1.3 describes, can add a view independent of the deployment team. If performance metrics degrade below the baselines established in Task 1.2: Create Quantitative Baselines for Existing Systems in Both Normal and Challenging Conditions or intersect with the boundaries defined in Implementation 3.2.2: Define and Independently Enforce Deterministic Operational Constraints, the system should initiate formal change-management or mitigation workflows. These workflows may include model retraining, throttling via Task 2.1: Establish SI Usage Thresholds and Rate Control Mechanisms, or, where a backup has been prepared under Implementation 2.2.1: Maintain Backup Systems Proportionate to Consequence, transitioning the process to it while mitigations are implemented. Switching to a backup carries its own considerations, such as the handover itself (see Implementation 11.2.3: Human Factors and Cognitive Transition Protocols for Control Handoffs).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What evaluation interval applies at first, and what drift rate shortens it?
  • Where drift data is too limited for a quantitative estimate, what approximation is used and who approves it?
  • Who decides whether a change not on the trigger list warrants re-verification, and how much?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-137, Information Security Continuous Monitoring (ISCM) for Federal Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Section 3.2.2, Establish Monitoring and Assessment Frequencies, sets monitoring and assessment frequencies from criteria including control volatility, impact level, organizational risk tolerance and threat and vulnerability information, and defines event-driven assessments, triggered by incidents, new threat information or significant changes to systems and operating environments, that run outside the scheduled frequency, which is the combination of adaptive cadence and event-driven re-verification this Implementation applies to drift.
  • Guidance Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management, SR Letter 11-7 Attachment, April 4, 2011, from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. Section V.2, Ongoing Monitoring, says that monitoring begins when a model is first implemented in production and continues periodically. Its frequency should suit the nature of the model, the availability of new data or modeling approaches and the magnitude of the risk. It also asks whether changes in products, exposures, activities, clients or market conditions call for adjusting, redeveloping or replacing the model. Written for banking organizations, it is a financial-sector precedent for setting cadence from risk and re-evaluating on change.
  • Research NIST AI 800-4, Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems, March 2026, from NIST. Section 3.2.1, Functionality, reports missing ground truth as a barrier to monitoring deployed AI systems, quoting the observation that post-deployment ground truth labels are often delayed, costly or entirely unavailable. This bears on setting evaluation cadence from measures that do not wait for confirmed outcomes.
