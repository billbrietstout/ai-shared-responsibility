Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority

Plan for the permanent removal of SI capabilities from operation, including the withdrawal of their authorities, the preservation of what they knew, and the transfer or deliberate abandonment of what depended on them.

Every SI system will eventually leave service, whether it is replaced, outgrown, withdrawn by its supplier, found unfit, or retired for other reasons. How it leaves can matter as much as how it was brought in.

Retiring an SI system is not an extreme form of updating one. An update transfers trust from one version to the next and seeks continuity. Retirement severs authority, removes a capability that other things may have come to rely on, and destroys operational knowledge that may exist nowhere else. Code, configurations, and procedures that the SI system generated may remain in service after it is retired. The provenance records kept under Implementation 12.1.2: Provenance and Traceability can identify them, and each can carry a recorded decision on whether it stays in service, is re-reviewed, or is replaced, and on who is responsible for it thereafter. Where other SI systems were trained or fine-tuned on its outputs, they may carry its behavior forward, including any flaws, after the original SI system is retired. Depending on the reason for retirement, it may be necessary to identify these and also consider them as part of the retirement.

Retirement may also be imposed rather than chosen, for example by a withdrawn vendor, a deprecated model, a failure in the system or something it depends on, or a regulatory decision. The decision to retire a system may come well after the point at which it has stopped working or could no longer be trusted, for instance after an unexpected failure from which it could not be restored, or after it was found to be irreversibly compromised by an attacker, perhaps for an unknown period. Anything that relies on the system running, or on trusting what it reports, may then no longer be possible, such as exporting what it has learned, capturing the configuration it was actually using, or querying it to understand its past decisions. Like a departing employee’s handover notes, these are best prepared periodically while the system is working and trusted, not left until retirement (Task 13.1: Prepare for Retirement While the System Is Still Running).

Records of the system’s past decisions can also become hard to interpret once it is gone. Logs may survive retirement while the model version, prompts, retrieval sources, configuration, and other state and information needed to understand or reproduce those decisions do not (Implementation 10.1.5: Define What Each Operational Record Is For). Deciding before retirement what to keep alongside the logs, and for how long, can keep them interpretable after the system is gone (see Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities). Some systems, such as hosted models whose versions are not retained, may not allow this. Where past decisions may later need to be explained, that limit can be weighed when deciding how far to rely on the system, rather than discovered at retirement.

The preparation this Practice recommends therefore belongs in the stages where the system still works, rather than at the point that a decision is made to retire the system. (See Task 7.6: Establish Processes for Internal SI Component Deprecation and Decommissioning for the deprecation, archiving, and destruction of internally developed SI artifacts, and Implementation 6.2.3: Manage End-of-Life Transitions for Externally Sourced SI Components for the offboarding of externally sourced SI components.) Where an SI system is returned to service after retirement, it can be admitted as a new deployment under Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success, which avoids carrying forward authority and identity that were deliberately withdrawn. Resuming its former authority and identity may be faster, where what it relied on has been kept, but it can carry unacceptable risk, because it relies on assumptions that may no longer hold or on system state that may no longer be valid.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure and Safe: Contributes the removal of standing authority at the end of a system’s life: credentials, triggers, registered interfaces, and delegated authority withdrawn and confirmed withdrawn, so that a system recorded as retired is less likely to still be able to act, either through misuse or on stale data and assumptions, particularly without oversight or anyone realizing that it is still operating. This Practice does not cover establishing the identities and revocation mechanisms being exercised, which Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes a named decision and a record of what was removed, so that retirement is an authorized act with an owner rather than an administrative consequence of something else. This Practice does not cover establishing who holds that authority in the first place, which Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability establishes.
  • Transparent: Contributes a record of what the system had become and what its removal takes away, captured while the system is still operational and can be queried. This Practice does not cover the routine operational logging that record draws on, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Reliable: Removing a capability that other processes and people have come to rely on degrades their performance, whether or not the retirement itself is clean. The effect is largest where the adaptation was informal and undocumented. Implementation 13.3.3: Determine and Record What Happens to Everything that Depended on the System calls for identifying them and recording a decision for each; Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning covers continuing to operate without the capability.
  • Secure: The records this Practice keeps describe what the system knew, what authority it held, and which revocations could not be confirmed. An attacker could use the extracted knowledge to learn thresholds and exception cases that a successor may share, and the list of unconfirmed revocations to find authority that may still be live. Protecting these records is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Privacy-Enhanced and Secure versus Transparent: Where the retired system held no sensitive data, performed no protective function, and important information it held also exists elsewhere, purging everything may be the simpler and safer course. Where the same learned material is both the thing that must be purged for data protection and the thing needed to interpret past decisions or to brief a successor, the two requirements act directly against each other on one artifact. One way to balance this is to separate the artifact from what is derived from it, retaining thresholds, patterns, and exception cases in a form that does not carry the underlying data (Implementation 13.1.3: Retain the Ability to Extract Operational Knowledge Before It Is Needed); others include a documented adjudication before teardown, or retaining it in restricted storage, for a defined period, with access controls narrower than the system had.
  • Safe and Accountable versus Secure and Safe itself: Where every delegation can be confirmed revoked, completing the retirement and withdrawing all authority are the same act. Where a delegation cannot be confirmed, perhaps because the failed dependency that forced the retirement is also the only means of confirming it, refusing to complete leaves the retirement indefinitely open, with no one able to sign it off and operators unsure what remains in force. Treating it as complete leaves authority possibly live in a system that may no longer be under appropriate oversight. Where confirmation is not possible by other means, the retirement can be closed as a last resort, after every revocation has been issued, with a record of each revocation that could not be confirmed and of the control that covers the authority that may remain in each case (Implementation 13.2.2: Revoke Authority Delegated to Other Entities, and Record What Could Not Be Confirmed). Any remaining authority is then tracked, with confirmation pursued as soon as the means to do so is restored, rather than either ignored or left blocking the retirement.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 1.7 (on establishing decommissioning processes): This Practice covers the procedures that subcategory asks for, covering the withdrawal of authority, the preservation of what the system knew, and a decision on what happens to everything that depended on it; it does not cover the decision to retire a system on performance grounds. For that, see for example Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations for detecting the degradation and Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers for who decides.
  • MANAGE 4.1 (on post-deployment risk management): This Practice covers the decommissioning element of a post-deployment plan, and treats its preparation as a continuing duty rather than a closing activity; it does not cover the monitoring that would detect the conditions leading to retirement. For that, see for example Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior.
  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding SI systems): This Practice covers the retirement path for a system that is being withdrawn permanently, including where the trigger is external; it does not cover disengaging a system that is behaving inconsistently with intended use while remaining in service. For that, see for example Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who decides that an SI system is to be retired, and who confirms that its authority has been withdrawn?
  • Where a revocation cannot be confirmed, is the retirement closed on a recorded exception or held open, and who decides? (see the Safe and Accountable versus Secure and Safe itself tension)

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-88r2, Guidelines for Media Sanitization, Revision 2, September 2025, from NIST. Section 4.3.1 states the principle this Practice is built on, that the need and methods for sanitization should be identified and developed before the disposal phase is reached rather than at it. Section 3.1 gives the Clear, Purge and Destroy method taxonomy, and its Appendix C provides a Certificate of Sanitization form, which is the established documented form of a confirmed and evidenced teardown.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 4.3.7, Table 12, applying RMF Task M-7, System Disposal, to OT, notes that OT components may need to be sustained beyond the availability of IT components, which bears on retiring an SI capability whose support life differs from the OT it serves. Section 6.2.3 asks for an asset disposal program covering data and media.
  • Standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023, Systems and software engineering: system life cycle processes, published jointly by ISO, IEC and IEEE. Clause 6.4.14, the disposal process, is the systems-engineering counterpart of this Practice and is stated more strongly than the SI-specific sources: its first listed outcome is that disposal constraints are provided as inputs to requirements, architecture, design and implementation, making retirement a design input rather than a closing activity. It also holds that where a system is still useful, the disposal process manages its transition to a new life cycle rather than a continuation of the existing one, and that records of disposal actions and analysis are available.
  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 3.5 treats decommissioning as the closing phase of an agent’s identity lifecycle rather than as a separate activity, and sets out what it requires. Its logging appendix bears on the tension above: the per-action log it specifies records the policy version in force at the time, so a decision record remains interpretable only while the policy it was taken under is also retained.
