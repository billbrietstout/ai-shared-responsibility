Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation

Treat human attention as a finite resource that AI systems should use efficiently. AI-driven notification volume can exceed an operator’s ability to triage, reducing effectiveness rather than improving it. This may be by overwhelming a domain with no prior experience managing this risk, breaking the assumptions behind a domain’s existing alarm flood mitigations, or generating a correlated surge across many systems at once. Notification volume is often set upstream, by where a detection or decision threshold is placed: the choice that trades missed detections against false positives under Implementation 3.1.2: Evaluate Misclassification Trade-offs also determines how many alerts reach operators, so that choice should account for the oversight capacity available to triage them.

Automatically filtering AI telemetry using vendor hazard reachability attestations, once independently verified or corroborated (Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures), can reduce alert floods by downgrading or consolidating operational warnings for model anomalies that have already been rendered unreachable by downstream deterministic guardrails (Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs), allowing operators to focus on actionable operational deviations. Where an SI system suppresses, groups or re-prioritizes notifications, that function is part of the alarm system, and changes to it should go through the same controlled modification process as other alarm changes. Alarms on which a protection layer depends, and notifications that monitor the SI system itself, should remain outside its reach, and what it has suppressed should stay visible to personnel on demand. Where the suppressing function or its inputs become untrustworthy, reverting to unsuppressed presentation is one option, at the cost of a higher notification volume. This Task complements Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness by addressing cases where notification volume, not just notification content or clarity, is what threatens an operator’s ability to respond effectively.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes the capacity side of intervention: keeping notification volume within what the available people can triage, including when one external event makes many AI systems request attention at once. This Task does not cover improving any single notification, which Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness covers.
  • Bias Managed: Contributes a bound on one human-cognitive error source, desensitization produced by notification rate, by treating that rate as a designed quantity rather than a byproduct. This Task does not address over-trust in an output that does arrive. For that, see Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation and Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Transparent: Filtering, suppression and consolidation decide what reaches a person, not what is recorded. Whether a suppressed notification remains retrievable afterward is governed by the logging requirements in Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities.
  • Secure: The flood thresholds, suppression rules, and consolidation logic this Task sets show which notifications personnel will not see, and at what rate their attention runs out. An attacker could use them to hide an action inside a suppressed category, or to trigger a flood that masks it. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice. Keeping alarms that a protection layer depends on outside SI-driven suppression, as this Task describes, limits the first.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Reliable: Where notifications repeat one underlying condition, consolidation loses nothing and both improve. Where the urgent signal is a single isolated one, Implementation 4.4.4: Applying Consolidation and Low-Cognitive-Load Design Techniques notes that aggressive batching buys operator capacity by delaying the alert that needed to arrive immediately.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 2.1 (on allocating resources): This Task treats operator attention as a resource that has to be sized for correlated surges up to the organization’s design basis across every system drawing on the same oversight pool, rather than for average per-system load; it does not cover considering the viable non-AI alternative systems that MANAGE 2.1 also names.
  • MEASURE 1.2 (on continuously improving measurement): This Task reassesses whether an established alarm-management practice remains effective once AI introduces non-deterministic failure modes; it does not cover evaluating whether a given display is understood by the audience that reads it. For that, see for example Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which alarms and notifications are kept outside the reach of SI-driven suppression?
  • Where an SI system suppresses, groups, or re-prioritizes notifications, who owns those rules: the owner of the alarm system, the owner of the SI system, or both together?

References:

  • Guidance Health and Safety Executive (2000) Better alarm handling, Chemicals Sheet No 6, from Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. States target alarm rates for process plant: no more than one every ten minutes in normal operation, and no more than ten in the first ten minutes after a major upset.
  • Guidance EEMUA Publication 191, “Alarm Systems: A Guide to Design, Management and Procurement”, from the Engineering Equipment and Materials Users’ Association, a UK association of industrial plant owner-operators. The underlying baseline for alarm metrics in process industries, and the source of the targets above.
  • Incident Report Health and Safety Executive (1997) The Explosion and Fires at the Texaco Refinery, Milford Haven, 24 July 1994. In the final 10.7 minutes two operators had to recognize, acknowledge and act on at least 275 alarms, arriving at one every two to three seconds, and the report records operators cancelling alarms for their nuisance value without recognizing what they meant.
  • Guidance The Joint Commission (2013) Sentinel Event Alert 50: Medical device alarm safety in hospitals, from the US non-profit that accredits healthcare organizations, issued on the basis of aggregated reported events. Documents the same failure in a sector with entirely different equipment.
  • Research McFarlane, D.C. (1999), “Coordinating the Interruption of People in Human-Computer Interaction”, INTERACT’99, pages 295 to 303. The first empirical comparison of the four ways a system can time an interruption, immediate, negotiated, mediated and scheduled, which is the evidence behind Implementation 4.4.4: Applying Consolidation and Low-Cognitive-Load Design Techniques‘s discussion of when to interrupt. No method was best on every measure: interrupting immediately got interruptions handled promptly at the cost of more errors, and scheduling them minimized task switching at a heavy cost to other performance, which is why timing is a design choice to make for each kind of alert rather than a default.
  • Research Simon, H. (1971), “Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World”. The origin of the framing this Task opens with, that a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention.
  • Research Bransby, M.L. and Jenkinson, J., The management of alarm systems, HSE Contract Research Report 166/1998, funded by Great Britain’s Health and Safety Executive. Section 5.2.4 treats alarm systems as part of the plant’s defense against hazard, so that changes to them need control, with closer review for alarms claimed in the hazard analysis. Section 5.3.6.7 notes open questions about whether AI and expert-system alarm processing developed on a limited range of transients will be effective in unexpected situations. Both bear on SI-driven suppression in this Task.
