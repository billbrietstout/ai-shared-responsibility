Implementation 10.1.3: Forensic Integrity and Privacy-Preserving Log Partitioning

Separating what investigators need from what routine users see lets an organization keep a usable forensic record while protecting personal information. Incorporate automated data-masking, encryption, and sanitization boundaries to reduce the chance that log collection inadvertently retains protected privacy data within open, day-to-day operational repositories. Where a structural conflict exists, organizations should explicitly define which data fields are essential to root-cause reconstruction (Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures) and restrict masking only to non-essential elements, so that the record stays useful for incident investigation. For high-consequence applications processing sensitive person-centric data, such as healthcare clinical support decisions or smart meter consumption records that can reveal when a household is occupied, whether to retain the raw, unredacted text and prompt history at all is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations.

Some frameworks, such as SP 800-53’s AU-3(3), limit personally identifiable information in audit records to the elements identified in the privacy risk assessment. Retaining it in highly secured, tamper-evident storage logically separated from routine operational visibility preserves what an investigator would need under Task 10.2: Establish Data Integrity and Tamper-Evidence Controls for Telemetry and Logging, and also creates a store of protected data in its least redacted form; limiting capture to the elements identified in the privacy risk assessment avoids that store, at the cost of reconstructions that may be incomplete.

Separated storage of this kind helps keep protected data out of routine view while preserving, as far as practical, the availability and validity of the audit trail. Record the choice and the reason for it. The separated store is privacy-sensitive in its own right, and a security target too: an attacker who reaches it obtains, in one place, the personal information that masking removed everywhere else. The privacy risk assessment should cover the separated store and identify the elements it retains. How access to it is limited and logged, how long its content is kept, and which breach notification obligations apply are for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. The time reconstruction is likely to need pulls toward keeping it, and the sensitivity of the content pulls toward deleting it sooner. Record these decisions.

Give each routine record an identifier that links it to its unredacted counterpart in the separated store, for example a keyed hash, so that investigators can rejoin the two without the protected content appearing in routine view. Decide in advance how a legal preservation order or an incident investigation suspends scheduled deletion in either store, and which role can authorize the suspension.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Is the raw, unredacted text and prompt history retained at all, or is capture limited to the elements the privacy risk assessment identifies?
  • Who may access the separated store, how long is its content kept, and which breach notification obligations apply?
  • How does a preservation order or an investigation suspend scheduled deletion, and which role can authorize the suspension?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement AU-3(3), Limit Personally Identifiable Information Elements, limits personally identifiable information in audit records to the elements identified in the privacy risk assessment. This bears on which fields this Implementation keeps for root-cause reconstruction, and on whether unredacted records are retained at all.
  • Working Document NIST SP 800-92r1 ipd, Cybersecurity Log Management Planning Guide, initial public draft, October 2023, from NIST. Play TS-4, Define Target State for Log Access, has the organization determine who or what can access log data locally and remotely, how that access is itself logged and protected, how log preservation orders are handled, and how inadvertent and intentional disclosures of sensitive information recorded in logs are handled. This bears on treating the separated store of unredacted records as privacy-sensitive in its own right.
