Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning

Where reports captured under Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route reveal failure modes with cross-organizational relevance, contribute a de-identified version to a trusted sharing community. Sharing de-identified records helps peer operators learn from a failure mode before it recurs elsewhere. This differs in purpose from the mandatory regulatory disclosure in Task 8.4: sharing here is voluntary, structural knowledge transfer intended to raise collective resilience, and it draws on the reporting channel established in Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route without replacing it. Specify a minimum useful shared record (the AI system’s role and consequence class, the failure mode, contributing factors, how it was detected, and which control did or did not hold) and a de-identification standard that strips the reporting organization’s identity, proprietary model internals, and customer or safety-sensitive data while preserving the pattern that makes the report useful to peers. Model product and version are not model internals. Retaining them makes the record far more useful to peers running the same model, but they can also help identify the reporting organization or expose a weakness the vendor has not yet addressed. Contracts with the vendor, such as nondisclosure or license terms, may restrict whether model product and version can be shared at all. Where contracts allow it, whether to retain them is the organization’s decision. Disclosing to the vendor first can address the concern about exposing an unaddressed weakness. Because this record derives from information captured under Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route, its de-identification standard should be at least as protective of the original reporter as the originating channel that produced it. Removing the reporting organization’s name may not be enough to de-identify a record. An unusual configuration, a precise time or the size of the operation can identify the organization, particularly in combination with other details. A sharing designation scheme, such as the Traffic Light Protocol, can state how far each record may be passed on. Where a record describes a weakness that could be exploited, consider disclosing it to the affected vendor before wider release. Confirm with counsel which legal protections, if any, apply to shared records. Protections written for sharing cyber threat information may not extend to an SI failure that involves no adversary. Whether to share through a trusted intermediary, such as a sector ISAC or Sector Coordinating Council where one exists or a cross-sector AI incident clearinghouse where none exists, or through bilateral arrangements, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. An intermediary adds distance between the record and its source and reaches more peers; bilateral arrangements give more control over who receives a record. Record the route chosen and why. Define how the organization reciprocally ingests peer reports back into its own MAP and MEASURE activity, for example, updating guardrails or drift thresholds in response to a failure mode others have shared (see, for example, Task 3.2 and Implementation 3.3.2).

Note: Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities governs what the system records. Whether a person who observes something the system did not record has a viable route to say so is governed by Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses, and the reports captured under Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route are the input stage for this Implementation. Cross-organizational sharing depends on incidents having been reported within organizations first.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Are de-identified records shared through a trusted intermediary or through bilateral arrangements?
  • Are model product and version retained in shared records, where vendor contracts allow it?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Suggested action GV-4.3-002, under GOVERN 4.3, asks organizations to identify the minimum set of criteria necessary for generative AI incident reporting and gives a field list: system ID, title, reporter, system or source, data reported, date, description, impacts and stakeholders impacted. It is a starting point for the minimum useful shared record this Implementation asks for, to which the de-identification standard is then applied.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-150, Guide to Cyber Threat Information Sharing, October 2016, from NIST. Section 3.4.1 asks that the legal team and privacy officers be consulted when developing procedures for protecting sensitive information, and Section 3.4.2 presents the Traffic Light Protocol for expressing sharing designations. Section 4.5.1 warns that aggregation, association or enrichment may enable re-identification. Written for cyber threat information, it bears on the de-identification and handling of the shared record in this Implementation.
