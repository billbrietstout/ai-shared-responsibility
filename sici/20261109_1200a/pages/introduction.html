Section 1: Introduction

The Automated, Agentic, and Super Intelligence (SI) in Critical Infrastructure and High-Consequence Applications (SICI) Profile for the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) provides actionable considerations for realizing the benefits from, managing the risks of, and fostering appropriately calibrated trust and confidence in, automated, agentic, and SI systems and tools in critical infrastructure (CI) and other high-consequence applications. While framed around the 16 critical infrastructure sectors, the risk management practices within this Profile apply broadly to any high-consequence or safety-critical application where failures in automated, agentic, and SI systems pose significant risk, across information technology (IT), operational technology (OT), and industrial control systems (ICS).

SICI is published as four documents and a set of supporting tools. The SICI Summary is a four-page overview for leadership. The SICI Profile, of about thirty pages, gives a condensed introduction, the Trustworthiness Characteristics as re-contextualized for critical infrastructure, each of the 13 Practices in full with the titles of its Tasks and Implementations, and a selection of the scenarios from Appendix B. This document, the SICI Guidebook, contains everything in the Profile, in full rather than condensed, and adds the text of every Task and Implementation, the references and what each covers, the Glossary, and the appendices; a reader who has the Guidebook has the Profile. They differ only in level of detail, and references to “the profile” in either document apply to both. The Guidebook is published in two editions with the same content: a web edition for reading, and a single-file assistant-ready edition to give to an LLM-based assistant, accompanied by a knowledge graph in YAML. The SICI Companion Toolkit provides prompts, scripts and checks for producing other views of the Guidebook, for example for a particular sector or role, and for checking those views against it.

For the purpose of this document, SI is defined as “a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments.” This definition, adapted from 15 U.S.C. (United States Code) § 9401 and as mandated in Executive Order 14434, includes reasoning, planning, optimization, and statistical systems, deep learning neural networks, large language models (LLMs), and hybrid systems that combine features of multiple forms of SI. As defined above, SI includes automated and agentic systems; these are explicitly named in the title to make the scope clear.

This profile is voluntary and is not a compliance checklist. It is not intended to be exhaustive. Rather, it is a collection of common risk management considerations with cross-sector applicability. It distinguishes governance evidence, such as inventories, impact assessments, monitoring plans, and audit records, which shows that disciplined processes exist, from technical evidence, such as evaluation results, decision-level records, and analysis of whether the system’s underlying approach suits the purpose, which shows whether a selected SI system, or its architecture, can meet the operational requirements of a given function. The first does not imply the second, and for SI systems the second can be much harder to establish. This profile populates the NIST AI RMF from the control and task level downwards to examples of concrete implementations, and upward towards governance to a shared practice layer. This profile is intentionally not ‘governance-complete’ and focuses on considerations with broad applicability. Sector-specific organizations are encouraged to adapt and extend this Guidebook and make it their own, complete with sector-specific prioritizations, contextualizations, use cases, operational scenarios, hazards, and case studies.

SI systems are software-based and benefit from the same risk management controls as other software, including those for testing, evaluation, validation, and verification (TEVV) and cybersecurity. Even on purpose-built hardware, which remains subject to existing controls, what an SI system does is defined by its software, models, data, and other knowledge. Much of what sets its risks apart lies in how complex and opaque these can be: behavior that emerges from training or large bodies of information can be difficult to inspect, explain, or fully test. At the same time, SI systems are structured, developed, and operated in ways that differ from more traditional software systems. As such, many of the assumptions that underpin existing governance and risk management controls need to be adjusted, extended, and re-interpreted to achieve their original governance and risk management intent. Furthermore, SI systems can introduce new risks, and heighten risks previously considered lower priority, and thus call for new governance and risk management approaches.

SI systems also challenge how organizations divide responsibility. For a person, IT and cybersecurity staff confirm that their identity is authorized to access a system, while other organizational roles judge whether that person is competent and suited to the role. For an SI agent, the second judgment often falls to IT by default, without the authority or resources to make it, while those who traditionally make it may lack the visibility or understanding to take it on. Whoever holds such a responsibility needs the visibility, time, resources, access, support, and authority to act on it. The profile does not prescribe who that should be. It marks where these decisions need to be made, so that each organization can assign them deliberately.

The AI RMF establishes a foundation for SI risk management by contextualizing SI’s impacts within widely accepted risk management principles. Based on the AI RMF, SICI helps critical infrastructure organizations address their unique risks and requirements for SI trustworthiness in Information Technology (IT), Operational Technology (OT), and Industrial Control Systems (ICS) environments. The specific inclusion of automated and agentic systems highlights the focus on placing the full spectrum of such systems into the one space, allowing for the surfacing of relative benefits, drawbacks, and trade-offs between different approaches.

SICI assists organizations in defining how trustworthy SI relates to operational considerations and the complex balance between factors such as safety, accuracy, optimization, and resource availability. For instance, when prioritizing safety, a hypothetical 75% accurate system with guaranteed safe failures may be preferable to another hypothetical 95% accurate system that has unknown failures. The profile helps organizations locate and contextualize relevant, actionable guidance and resources to understand and manage SI risks within their overall risk management. By improving transparency into the trade-offs between leveraging the benefits of different SI systems and allocating resources to manage their respective risks, this Guidebook can help enhance the efficiency and competitiveness of critical infrastructure organizations and the vendors of SI systems that serve them.

This profile assists critical infrastructure organizations, including operators, managers, owners, suppliers, and government agencies in effectively navigating the balance between the benefits and risks of SI, throughout the SI lifecycle. The introduction of agentic and other SI systems into critical infrastructure, alongside increasingly advanced automation, heightens a risk already present in interconnected systems: anomalies and unforeseen interactions can cascade, with potentially significant consequences for large populations. Critical infrastructure systems are required to have high levels of availability, safety, transparency, security, and reliability, in addition to accuracy and efficiency. They may be expected to have long operational lifespans, operate in changing conditions without needing intervention, and integrate with legacy systems. This profile highlights practices and resources that assist critical infrastructure organizations in understanding how the convergence of different forms of SI into critical infrastructure systems affects their ability to meet these requirements and controls for managing the associated risks.

The AI RMF uses the term trustworthiness to refer to a set of abstract characteristics, such as being safe, secure, or fair, that an SI system may need to have, or that may be desirable, in a given context of use. This profile translates these abstract characteristics into actionable risk management controls for the selection, development, deployment, operation, maintenance, and decommissioning of SI systems in critical infrastructure.

1.1: Audience

This profile is written for audiences and organizations across a wide range of SI and critical infrastructure experience and expertise, SI maturity, and available resources for risk management.

This document deliberately spans a spectrum, from low-cost, high impact initial precautions through to more advanced and comprehensive measures. Organizations with limited expertise and resources will find practical steps on basic measures that they can take immediately and that can provide measurable improvements in their risk management. Organizations with greater expertise and resources will find more advanced guidance to further improve their management of SI risk. See Section 1.4 for ways to use this document.

The primary audience for this Guidebook is the owners, operators, and managers of critical infrastructure systems who wish to risk-manage SI systems that they already use or would like to adopt, and critical infrastructure system acquirers (including procurement, contract, and legal officials) who communicate requirements and challenges to vendors of critical infrastructure and SI systems. As such, it is organized according to suggested practices, drawn from use cases that directly speak to governance challenges in critical infrastructure applications and environments.

Each Practice states an objective for the organization, a shared starting point across roles, from executive and strategic managers to the personnel who carry out the work, as well as others involved in governance, risk management, and policy. Each Practice is divided into Tasks, which describe how the objective can be approached, and illustrative Implementations, which give concrete steps. This layout lets readers work top-down, from an objective to the work that serves it, or bottom-up, from specific SI use cases, lifecycle stages, or identified risks to the objectives they serve. Organizations can superimpose their own top-level risk governance onto these Tasks, deciding which controls to implement immediately, which to phase in across the asset lifecycle, and how to allocate limited resources within their own risk profile (see NIST IR 8286r1, Section 2.2, and NIST IR 8286B-upd1, Section 2.2.2).

Secondary audiences include vendors and developers who provide and integrate SI capabilities into critical infrastructure environments, and those in governance, risk management, and policy roles. By breaking down tasks and implementations and providing references, this Guidebook assists them in translating critical infrastructure requirements into technically meaningful controls. Throughout this Guidebook, “organizations” and “stakeholders” refer collectively to acquirers, operators, managers, owners, developers, and vendors (including those who may not be exclusively serving critical infrastructure), unless a specific role is called out.

For systems engineers, enterprise architects, and compliance officers seeking to map these operational practices into formal system lifecycle and SI management frameworks (such as ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288, NIST SP 800-160, or ISO/IEC 42001), crosswalks are provided in Appendix D: Crosswalks.

1.2: Scope

The profile covers the full range of automated, agentic, and SI systems used in critical infrastructure environments and high-consequence applications, from general-purpose productivity or analytics tools to systems developed specifically for mission-critical tasks. This includes cases where the SI system’s vendor no longer exists or may be unaware that their product has entered a critical infrastructure environment, shifting the responsibility for adaptation and risk management specific to critical infrastructure downstream in the SI system supply chain. In many organizations, SI systems are already in use, whether procured deliberately, adopted informally, or added to existing products through updates. The practical question is then often not whether SI enters the environment, but how far it is permitted to, and an inventory of what is already present (Task 7.1: Maintain an Internal Registry of SI Deployments) is a natural first step. Organizations select the parts of the profile that are relevant to their systems, environments, applications, and obligations.

General-purpose Large Language Model (LLM)-based assistants used to generate configuration files, automation code, operational procedures, or diagnostic advice, or to summarize OT-actionable documentation and communications, fall within this scope even when they have no direct connection to the physical process. Because these tools are frequently procured and classified under standard IT or SaaS contracts, the resulting path from assistant output, to engineer review, to deployed configuration or action can be overlooked by hazard review and risk classification processes that were designed around direct OT connections.

This profile also encompasses stand-alone automated and SI tools, agentic systems capable of autonomous action, and ‘embedded’ SI features integrated into larger software or hardware systems, where the presence, and nature of the SI system may be hidden from the operator, whether based on machine learning, other SI approaches such as symbolic logic and reasoning, or hybrid approaches. This includes the statistical, optimization and other SI systems that have supported parts of many critical infrastructure operations for decades. Where newer SI systems are layered on top of them, risks can compound where one system’s output becomes another’s input, and the practices in this document apply to the combination as well as to each system.

Critical infrastructure relies on IT, OT, and ICS systems that are generally designed to produce consistent outcomes through a documented engineering process, so that their behavior can be specified and verified in advance. While the environment and hardware are understood to have a random component, much of OT and ICS engineering revolves around making systems behave predictably overall and compensating for randomness caused by the environment, wear and tear, and so on. SI systems can be harder to pin down in the same way (see NIST AI 100-1, Appendix B), especially those whose behavior comes from training examples or large bodies of information rather than from explicit rules that can be inspected. Their behavior can depend on conditions that are hard to anticipate, particularly when they are deployed in contexts that differ from those in which they were developed or trained. Some SI systems are also stochastic, producing different outputs for the same input. Even a fully deterministic system can be unpredictable if it reacts to a wider variety of inputs, or makes decisions in more complex ways, than human observers can comprehend in the moment.

While these issues apply to many SI systems, they become especially acute in critical infrastructure, where failures often carry grave consequences. Existing assurance regimes, including system authorization and the continuous monitoring that many of them already incorporate, can remain the foundation for managing SI systems (see NIST SP 800-37r2, Appendix F). SI introduces additional factors that can invalidate the assessment. An SI system’s behavior can shift after it is authorized, such as through model updates, drift, changes in its inputs, changes upstream in its supply chain, and seemingly unrelated changes to the operating environment, without any change the organization itself made, and outside the bounds of traditional change management (see NIST SP 800-137, Section 2.2). This profile therefore builds on those regimes, with particular attention to what should trigger reassessment and to monitoring what an SI system does (see NIST SP 800-39, Section 3.4, and NIST AI 800-4); Appendix D lists common control areas whose assumptions SI systems can break.

Anticipating, being prepared for, and managing potential risks is key to unlocking the opportunities that SI technologies and innovations create, and fostering the calibrated trust and confidence that drives appropriate adoption. Introducing SI systems into critical environments alters the total risk profile, meaning that organizations should ensure performance or efficiency gains do not introduce unmitigated brittleness or run the risk of degrading essential operational skillsets over long system lifecycles. While holistic SI trust extends well beyond cybersecurity, operational resilience and cyber defense remain deeply intertwined. To avoid duplicating dedicated cybersecurity guidance, this Guidebook defines high-level operator expectations across shared domains, such as identity, supply chain, logging, and incident response. Control-level specifics are deferred to established and upcoming cybersecurity guidance, SI-specific and otherwise. Rather than specifying control-level exploit mitigations, the practices emphasize bounding downstream physical and operational consequences.

In doing so, this Guidebook applies the principle behind zero trust architecture, that trust is not granted implicitly on the basis of location or prior authorization but verified at each boundary, to SI risk more generally, beyond cybersecurity. An SI system’s outputs, actions and inputs warrant verification where they cross into other systems, physical processes and human decisions, whether a departure from expected behavior arises from compromise, malfunction or a change in operating conditions. The profile distinguishes the SI Attack Surface, the set of ways an adversary can exploit an SI system, from the broader SI Risk Surface, which adds the non-adversarial ways an SI system can deviate from expected behavior to cause harm, such as drift, error, and unexpected interactions (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface).

In machine-speed environments, the practices in this document can contribute layers of resilience against operational disruption, whether the underlying cause is adversarial or otherwise. Examples of such SI systems include, but are not limited to:

  • Chatbots, general-purpose SI systems, and generative SI systems based on LLMs for internal, inter-organizational, and public use.
  • Automated decision support and governance systems used for tasks such as summarizing regulatory compliance requirements and automating routine administrative approvals.
  • SI-enabled decision support and advisory tools that generate recommendations, prioritizations, triage rankings, or root-cause hypotheses from structured operational, sensor, or tabular data.
  • Embodied intelligent systems such as robots, edge devices with data processing and autonomous decision making capabilities, and other cyberphysical systems.
  • Strategic simulations and digital twins that use learned and derived statistical and symbolic models for long-term resource forecasting, optimization, “what-if” scenario planning, and predicting the cascading impacts.
  • Dynamic resource and logistics management tools that optimize the real-time distribution, routing, and load distribution of essential resources.
  • Intelligent detection and monitoring systems that use a wide variety of different information sources to detect unauthorized access, environmental hazards, faults, and anomalous behavior.
  • Operator advisory and situational awareness systems designed to prioritize notifications and alarms, and provide human operators with the context needed for high-impact interventions.
  • High-consequence enterprise applications, such as automated claims clearinghouses, credit and liquidity risk algorithms, prior-authorization workflows, and automated compliance reporting tools.
  • Safety-critical decision support tools, including clinical diagnostic algorithms, emergency service dispatch triage, disaster relief logistics, and public safety threat prioritization systems.
  • Critical digital substrates and software pipelines, including SI-assisted code generation, infrastructure-as-code (IaC) synthesizers, automated CI/CD deployment pipelines, and configuration management tools.
  • AI agents and agentic systems, machine vision and perception systems, SI systems that include machine learned models, including but not limited to neural networks, and SI systems that include, but are not limited to, symbolic reasoning, planning, logic programming, and mathematical modeling.

This profile aligns with, contextualizes, references, interprets, adapts, and facilitates the operationalization of guidance documents at the intersection of SI, IT, OT, ICS, software development, operations, cybersecurity, and critical infrastructure. The profile provides guidance for applying the four AI RMF Core functions of Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, that is relevant to all critical infrastructure sectors and high-consequence applications. Examples include:

  • Unifying definitions for key terms and concepts at the intersection of SI, critical infrastructure, and related domains to facilitate efficient, effective cross-sector co-operation and interoperability.
  • Guiding requirements analysis to tailor the risk management of SI systems to the performance and reliability expectations and operational realities of critical infrastructure, including legacy systems, physically distributed assets, and resourcing.
  • Addressing stringent requirements in the critical infrastructure sector, including the need for bounded, predictable behavior, with fallbacks that share as few of the SI system’s sources of error as practical and are verified in advance, explainability, graceful degradation, and fail-safe operation.
  • Emphasizing the heightened need for adversarial robustness in all lifecycle stages of SI in critical infrastructure (see NIST AI 100-2e2025, Section 4.1.2).
  • Supporting the critical infrastructure needs for rigorous testing, evaluation, validation, and verification (TEVV) of systems, including those that include SI.
  • Illuminating critical infrastructure-specific capabilities and trade-offs with competing and complementary SI techniques, technologies, and approaches.
  • Promoting visibility and collaboration across the supply chain of SI to address the unique needs, challenges, and risks of SI in critical infrastructure.
  • Highlighting practical, actionable, and measurable steps that can be taken by organizations at any level of SI expertise and risk management maturity.

While this Guidebook natively supports risk management across all lifecycle stages, practices are structured around operational workflows (such as procurement, incident response, and decommissioning) to maximize utility for infrastructure operators, rather than development phases.

This document is not intended to be exhaustive or duplicative. Rather, it focuses on highlighting a variety of practices with high impact and/or general applicability across critical infrastructure sectors. Where possible, it serves as a translator, crosswalk, and index to existing relevant guidance, frameworks, and other resources. These practices have been chosen as starting points for critical infrastructure organizations to make informed benefit-risk determinations for integrating SI into critical infrastructure operations.

The practices guide organizations toward tasks and example implementations that can be tailored to their unique needs, risk profiles, expertise, and available resources. They are subdivided into tasks and implementations that highlight a wide spectrum of options, offering accessible entry points that can result in impactful risk reduction for resource-constrained environments alongside more advanced strategies. Accordingly, organizations may apply this Guidebook at different levels of rigor, recognizing that what constitutes an accessible or “foundational” step for one entity may require significant operational balancing for another. The overarching goal is to ensure this Guidebook provides practical utility for everyone, allowing stakeholders to choose from a flexible menu of tasks that best match their unique operational realities, asset lifecycles, and risk tolerances.

1.3: Trustworthiness

This profile defines trust as “a belief that an entity meets certain expectations and therefore, can be relied upon.”5 The value of that trust, across the wide variety of critical infrastructure organizations, depends on how well it is calibrated to the underlying trustworthiness of the system (see NIST SP 800-39, Section 2.6): both over-trust and under-trust can result in adverse impacts or in benefits being missed. Trustworthiness is defined according to several characteristics that are considered together (see NIST AI 100-2e2025, Section 4.1.1, on trade-offs between them). For example, a system might be highly effective and secure, but may be fragile and sensitive to changes in its environment. While adversarial compromise of such a system may be unlikely, it still cannot be trusted to function as intended if the environment changes.

To preserve consistency with the foundational NIST AI RMF, this Guidebook builds on and re-contextualizes its Trustworthiness Characteristics. While these terms may differ from standard process safety or industrial control lexicons, retaining this shared baseline provides a practical, common framework for cross-sector communication. This profile deliberately re-contextualizes, re-prioritizes, and re-groups these baseline characteristics into an operational set specifically aligned to the needs of critical infrastructure and safety-critical applications, as shown in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table. The concept of trustworthiness in this context is further expanded upon in Appendix A: Trustworthiness Characteristics. By highlighting these specific characteristics and terms, this Guidebook helps organizations to evaluate the suitability of these systems relative to other SI and non-SI systems, including the systems they may already be using.

This profile facilitates improved communication of trustworthiness requirements and capabilities between actors in the supply chain of SI systems. For example, the Reliable, Robust and Resilient characteristics correspond to the terms of a typical service-level agreement (SLA): the performance it commits to and how consistently it is delivered, the conditions under which that commitment holds, and the degraded service and restoration expected when it cannot be met. Performance should include correctness, which can be hard to measure for any system with a complex task. It is harder still when wrong outputs are convincing, as many SI system outputs can be: a system can deliver 99.999% availability while giving convincing but wrong answers.

Characteristic Illustrative Considerations for Critical Infrastructure
Does it do the correct thing?
Valid (the right system for this use, established for this deployment) An SI system that is valid in one deployment (a particular system, place, and time) can be wrong in another: a different plant, patient population, sensor mix, or organizational convention. Validity can change over time, through seasonal variation and changes in equipment, terminology, or regulation. Defining validity fixes what counts as expected, a scope that is specified rather than discovered. This scope determines whether a given departure is a question of reliability, robustness, or resilience.
Accurate (how well outputs agree with a known or accepted reference) Meaningful SI system accuracy is rarely captured by one number and comparing real-world failures requires nuance, such as a missed safety trip versus a spurious shutdown, or a summary that drops a safety caveat versus one that adds an irrelevant detail. Complexity makes the reference hard to choose. A system tuned to whichever was easiest to obtain can improve on the measure while getting worse at the task.
Bias Managed (systematic error identified and deliberately handled) Errors that affect performance should be bounded. Biases, being errors with a consistent direction and a cause, should be removed or compensated for. Biases enter SI systems via datasets, encoded background and domain knowledge, assumptions needed for learning but seldom stated, and how humans use outputs. Errors that fall unevenly on those affected are covered in Fair.
Does it keep doing the correct thing, particularly as conditions change?
Reliable (performs as required over time, in expected conditions and use) A mean time between failures or service level agreement (SLA) figure for an SI system may be misleading when the scope of relevant performance data and failures is poorly defined and when wrong outputs are hard to detect. Updates that can invalidate prior statistics may not look like updates, such as SI systems learning from experience or keying on a dimension that varies without being operationally relevant.
Robust (keeps performing and limits harm when conditions or use go beyond expectations) Conditions and use vary: weather, load, degraded sensors, unfamiliar formats, or changes in policy. Every parameter can sit within its stated limits while the combination is one nobody expected. Designing and interpreting tests based on an understanding of how the SI system generalizes and reacts facilitates the characterization of these otherwise intractably large parameter spaces.
Resilient (degrades gracefully when it must and maintains the ability to resume operation) Many SI systems derive their abilities from training data, large bodies of information, and observation rather than inspectable first principles. The boundaries of expected conditions and use, and where the fallback is needed, may not be obvious. Resilience has to be considered and maintained deliberately, and fallback plans made with appropriate consideration of the different ways SI systems degrade (see NIST SP 800-160v2r1).
Does it avoid unintentional or deliberate harm?
Safe (avoid harm to people, property or environment, in defined conditions) Every system is blind to hazards its design, development, and training left out, and SI systems often cover enough situations that the gaps are hard to find, especially if they include learning without human direction. Safety covers both the actions and influence of SI systems. For instance, a person or downstream system can carry out a harmful action based on an incorrect SI-generated recommendation or omission.
Secure (withstands attack) Built for generality, many SI systems take high-dimensional inputs such as images, sensor records, documents and free text, which often cannot be meaningfully sanitized. An adversary who shapes them changes what the system does without breaching anything, and probing may reveal capability nobody intended to deploy, boundaries the operator never knew were there, and may be impossible to enumerate.
Does it allow its behavior to be observed, understood, and accounted for?
Transparent (the information exists and can be obtained) Critical infrastructure may have formal root-cause analysis obligations. An SI system may act on documents, retrieved information, model versions, internal states, or external agentic interactions that may be too extensive to log in full, and what matters may not be known until afterward.
Explainable & Interpretable (what the output means, and how it was reached) SI systems can be more complex and consider more variables than a person can understand given available time, attention, and training. Appropriately accurate explainability and interpretability can help people inspect and understand SI system behavior and act appropriately, be it in the moment or in an investigation.
Accountable (responsibility is named and can be enforced) The scale and variety of inputs that influence an SI system throughout its lifecycle can impair the ability to meaningfully assign responsibility for the system’s behavior, even where that behavior can be explained. Those responsible should be appropriately empowered and identified in advance.
Does it meet its obligations to those who depend on it?
Privacy-Enhanced (sensitive data stays contained) An organization’s own SI system can combine otherwise harmless records into inferences about individuals, staffing, production, or other mission-sensitive information. SI systems that reach beyond the organization, such as hosted models, may diffuse information to tools, vendors, others in the supply chain, or publicly.
Fair (service does not degrade for some of those it affects) SI-mediated allocation, detection, prioritization or recommendation can perform unevenly across equipment, conditions, locations, populations, or customers, potentially violating service obligations that an operator may have. This profile helps identify and measure that variation; what level is acceptable is a determination for the operator in its regulatory and operational context.

1.4: Using this Guidebook

Readers who want an overview may start with the SICI Summary or the SICI Profile, which present the same Practices in shorter form.

This document is not meant to be read from start to finish. It is structured so that readers can start wherever their interest or responsibility lies and follow the cross-references from there. Each Practice, Task and Implementation carries an item number, and the cross-references between items use those numbers. The labeled fields in each item state what an item covers, what it does not, and where the rest is addressed, so that a reader who arrives at any item can find its neighbors. In the web edition, cross-references are links, and the viewer’s Back command returns to where you were; in either version, searching for an item number such as “Task 3.2” finds the item and the places that refer to it.

Readers may find the following paths useful as starting points. Each path leads into the same Practices, Tasks, and Implementations, and none is meant to limit what a reader looks at.

  • Executive leadership may begin with Appendix B: Scenario-to-Practice Mapping, which connects a selection of illustrative scenarios to one or more Practices, then read the corresponding Practice descriptions and the Tasks beneath them.
  • Acquisition, procurement, contracting and legal personnel may begin from the decision or obligation they are responsible for, such as acquiring an SI service, supporting internal development, setting data-use terms or managing supplier changes. Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success helps identify the requirements that apply, and Practice 6 and Practice 7 address external and internal dependencies respectively.
  • Technical managers and practitioners may begin from a specific SI system, lifecycle stage or risk, using the Search Keywords to locate the relevant Practice or Task, then working down to its Implementations and following the cross-references to related items.
  • Auditors, assessors, and compliance and security functions may begin with Appendix D, which lists common control areas, the assumption each typically makes, how SI systems can break it, and where this Guidebook suggests ways forward. It describes technical issues and possible approaches, and does not interpret what any specific requirement, control or standard demands. Note that this Guidebook is not a checklist to be applied in full. Many of its Practices, Tasks and Implementations will not apply to a given SI system or operating context, and some would add cost or risk where they do not fit. This profile notes where items may not apply or may be in tension with others, and those notes are meant to be read with appropriate professional judgment. Where an item is set aside, recording why can be as useful to a later review as recording what was done.
  • Those concerned with a particular trustworthiness characteristic may begin with the Trustworthiness Characteristics table and search Section 2 for the items that contribute to it, work against it, or balance it against another characteristic.
  • Those mapping this Guidebook to existing frameworks may begin with the AI RMF subcategory fields and Appendix D: Crosswalks.
  • Organizations with limited resources may begin with the SI systems whose outputs most directly reach physical processes, safety functions, or essential services, since the effort that several Tasks call for scales with consequence (for example, Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle, Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions and Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs).

We anticipate that many readers will load the assistant-ready edition of this Guidebook (Markdown file), with its knowledge graph (YAML file) into an LLM-based assistant, and use it to understand how the profile applies to their situation and to help them act on it. The item numbers, cross-references and labeled fields described above are intended to support this use as well. Because this Guidebook is deliberately sector-agnostic, how useful an assistant’s answers are depends largely on the organizational context it is given alongside it: the sector, the SI system and what it is connected to, the regulatory and contractual obligations that apply, existing policies and risk management processes, and the resources available. How that context is provided will vary. It may be described in the conversation, supplied as documents where organizational policy permits, or drawn out by asking the assistant to question the user about their situation before making recommendations. Some of this context may be sensitive, so consider what the assistant is permitted to receive and where that information will go (see NIST SP 1353). Suggested ways to work with the document through an assistant include:

  • Describing your SI system, sector and role, and asking which Practices and Tasks apply.
  • Asking what a given Task would involve for your organization, and which References to consult.
  • Asking which items address a specific risk.
  • Describing an incident, whether your own or one reported elsewhere, and asking which items might have prevented or limited it, as input to a lessons-learned or after-action review.
  • Asking which trustworthiness characteristics may be in tension for your use case, and where this Guidebook discusses them.
  • Where the assistant can search, asking it to find current or related references using the descriptions this Guidebook gives for each citation.
  • Evaluating a project against the trustworthiness characteristics, then asking what the team should do and which references to read.

An assistant can make this document easier to use, but it can also misrepresent it. Some tools truncate long documents or summarize tables without saying so, so ask the assistant to work from the full text, and check that it has seen all of the Practices and appendices. An assistant may also present a Practice or Task as a requirement, or fill gaps with guidance that is not in this document. Ask it to cite the item numbers it is drawing on, and check important points against the text itself (see NIST SP 1353). This profile is guidance to inform professional judgment. Neither this document nor an assistant’s reading of it is a substitute for that judgment, or for the regulatory and contractual obligations that apply to your organization. The assistant you use may itself be an SI system within the scope of this Guidebook, whose risks should be managed accordingly, particularly if its outputs will inform configurations, procedures or operational decisions.

1.5: Introduction references

This section lists the sources cited in Sections 1.1 to 1.4. In Section 2, each Practice, Task and Implementation has its own References field, listing the sources that give further detail on that item and saying which part of each source applies. Appendix F: Consolidated References gathers sources cited in the body of this Guidebook (Sections 1 and 2) into a single list, with the places where each is cited. Entries here follow the same conventions as the References fields, described in Section 2, including the label that denotes the kind of document. References are neither requirements nor endorsements, and are not exhaustive.

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 4.1.1 documents trade-offs among the attributes of trustworthy SI, including between explainability and adversarial robustness, between privacy and fairness, and between accuracy and adversarial robustness. It states that it may not be possible to maximize all of them at once, and notes that organizations may need to decide which to prioritize for the system and use case. It supports this Guidebook’s treatment of trustworthiness characteristics as considered together, with tensions to balance. Section 4.1.2 states that, given the multitude of powerful attacks, appropriate mitigations must be designed before SI systems are deployed in critical domains, and that, with theoretically secure ML algorithms lacking for many tasks, designing them is an inherently ad hoc and fallible process. This supports the emphasis on adversarial robustness in this Scope, and is consistent with its choice to bound downstream consequences rather than rely on exploit mitigations alone.
  • Research NIST AI 800-4, Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems, March 2026, from NIST. The Executive Summary and Section 1, Introduction, explain why pre-deployment evaluation is not sufficient on its own: it is predominantly done in controlled environments that cannot account for real-world dynamics, SI outputs are typically non-deterministic, and models in real-world use do not always perform as tested. They give three purposes for post-deployment monitoring: validating that a system operates reliably and as expected in real-world use, tracking unforeseen outputs arising from non-determinism or changing input conditions, and identifying unexpected consequences in new or changing contexts. This supports monitoring what an SI system does after authorization.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8286B-upd1, Prioritizing Cybersecurity Risk for Enterprise Risk Management, from NIST. Section 2.2.2, Cybersecurity Risk Optimization, defines four methods for deciding which risk responses are funded when budgets are limited (fiscal, algorithmic, operational and forced ranking optimization), and holds that each method needs a process for the scenarios left unfunded. It gives an organization named options for deciding which Tasks to implement now and which to phase in.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8286r1, Integrating Cybersecurity and Enterprise Risk Management (ERM), from NIST. Section 2.2, The Gap Between CSRM Output and ERM Input, and its Fig. 3, Risk register information flow among system, organization, and enterprise levels, describe a cybersecurity risk register for each high value system and organization whose contents are aggregated, normalized, analyzed and prioritized at higher levels as input to enterprise risk management. Written for cybersecurity risk, it is one model for connecting risk decisions made under individual Tasks with an organization’s own enterprise risk governance.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-37r2, Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations: A System Life Cycle Approach for Security and Privacy, from NIST. Appendix F, System and Common Control Authorizations, describes the regime the Scope builds on. Under ongoing authorization, continuous monitoring keeps the authorizing official informed of the current posture of the system, so that continued operation is decided on ongoing risk determinations. Under ongoing authorization, reauthorization is in most instances event-driven, in response to an event that raises risk above the level previously accepted. Its list of event-driven triggers and significant changes already includes changes the organization did not make, such as new threat information and changes in the supply chain. This profile adds the ways an SI system’s own behavior can shift after authorization, through model updates, drift or changes in its inputs. Section 1.2 notes that although the publication pertains to federal information systems, state, local and tribal governments and private sector organizations are encouraged to use it.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-39, Managing Information Security Risk: Organization, Mission, and Information System View, from NIST. Section 2.6, Trust and Trustworthiness, treats trust as relative to a specific circumstance and able to rest on objective evidence or on subjective belief, and Section 2.6.2, Trustworthiness of Information Systems, ties the trustworthiness a system needs to its environment of operation and the maximum acceptable level of risk, determined by weighing the available assurance evidence. It is written for information security, and its definitions of trust and trustworthiness differ from the ones this Guidebook takes from SP 800-160, but it is an established account of matching reliance to evidence. Section 3.4, Monitoring Risk, Task 4-1, separates compliance monitoring (are risk responses implemented correctly and operating as intended), effectiveness monitoring (did they actually reduce the risk) and change monitoring, and states that the first does not establish the second. Its Monitoring Changes discussion adds that changes to the environment of operation, including technology changes that may be totally independent of the organization, can still change the risk it carries. Together these support reassessing on changes the organization did not make, and monitoring what an SI system does rather than only how it is configured.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-137, Information Security Continuous Monitoring (ISCM) for Federal Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Section 2.2, Ongoing System Authorizations, states that initial authorization to operate rests on evidence available at one point in time while systems and environments of operation change, and that ongoing assessment turns an otherwise static risk determination into a dynamic process supporting ongoing authorization. Although written for federal information systems, it is an example of the existing assurance regimes this Guidebook builds on when an SI system’s behavior shifts after it is authorized.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Appendix C, Section C.1, Defining Cyber Resiliency, collects definitions of resilience across communities, among them the SP 800-53 definition of information system resilience: continuing to operate under adverse conditions or stress, even in a degraded or debilitated state, while maintaining essential operational capabilities, and recovering in a time frame consistent with mission needs. That pairing of degraded operation and recovery is what the Resilient characteristic in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table names. Section C.3 treats cyber resiliency as one aspect of trustworthiness, alongside safety, system resilience, survivability, reliability and security.
  • Guidance NIST SP 1270, Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence, March 2022, from NIST. Section 2.1.1 defines bias in its statistical sense as an effect that systematically distorts a result, as distinct from random error, which is the sense of the Bias Managed entry in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table. SP 1270 treats that sense as insufficient on its own, and section 2.1.2 with Fig. 2 groups SI bias into three categories: systemic, statistical and computational, and human. The statistical and computational category and the human category broadly correspond to the datasets, encoded knowledge and use of outputs that the Trustworthiness Characteristics table names; uneven effects on particular groups, which SP 1270 treats chiefly under systemic and human bias, are placed under Fair in Table 2.
  • Working Document NIST SP 1353 ipd, NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0: Quick-Start Guide for Using Artificial Intelligence (AI) for CSF Analysis and Reporting, from NIST, initial public draft, as of August 2026. The Important Notes Before Reading Further and the Important Reminders and Tips for Getting Started ask users to review an SI tool’s data retention, training, access privilege and confidentiality settings, follow organizational policy before inputting sensitive information, use a tool authorized by the security and privacy team, and ingest only artifacts approved for that tool. These are specific checks for deciding what an assistant may receive. The section “Reference Data and AI” asks that SI-assisted mappings and crosswalks retain identifiers, source context, provenance and status so that unsupported mappings are not used without expert review, and the Glossary defines proposed and derived status labels for mappings pending validation. These are practices for keeping an assistant’s output traceable to the items it cites.
