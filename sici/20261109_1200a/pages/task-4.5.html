Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation

Knowing where a person can intervene in time, and where they cannot, helps an organization rely on human oversight where it works and on automated protections elsewhere. Identify operational contexts where real-time, manual human-in-the-loop (HITL) oversight is physically or technically infeasible due to tight system time constants (such as stability anomalies operating in milliseconds, real-time industrial control loops, or machine-speed cyber defense events). Oversight of any mode degrades into a purely nominal or superficial check where the unfolding event outruns what the operator can be relied upon to do about it, particularly in situations where fatigue or stress are factors; the oversight thresholds established under Implementation 4.5.1: Define Engineering Time-Constant Thresholds are what make this testable rather than assumed.

For these regimes, formally define the boundaries where the human-on-the-loop oversight mode (action proceeds, with intervention capability retained) or the human-out-of-the-loop oversight mode (autonomous execution with retrospective post-hoc review) should substitute for the human-in-the-loop mode, and specify the deterministic automated protections required to support them. A named oversight mode, or a level on an autonomy scale, carries little meaning unless it is stated together with the operating envelope, the task, the supervision arrangement and the fallback assumptions it was defined for. The oversight mode in force should also be evident to the personnel involved, together with what the SI system may currently do without them and what it will do next if no one intervenes. Aviation treats confusion about the active autoflight mode as a recognized vulnerability, and asks that crews be able to identify the current mode and anticipate the next (see FAA AC 120-71B).

Operating in a less direct oversight mode does not remove human decision-making; it moves it earlier and later. Even where real-time intervention is infeasible, a specific role holds the oversight authority to decide whether the system may operate in that mode, on what conditions, and when to suspend or roll it back (Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers). That role needs the visibility, time, resources, access, and support to exercise the authority. Entering a less direct oversight mode should be a deliberate and recorded act by that role, not something the SI system does on its own. Leaving it when its conditions no longer hold, for example for a fallback state under Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior, need not wait for a person. This Task separates the paths where human intervention can be relied upon from those where it cannot, so that automated boundaries carry the role where a person cannot.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes the test that decides whether human intervention counts as a safety control: whether what the operator would have to do is something a person can be relied upon to do in the time available, and documenting the paths where it cannot. This Task does not cover the replacement protection, which Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes the human decision that remains when real-time intervention is infeasible: a named role decides whether a regime may be entered, on what conditions, and when to suspend it. This Task does not cover defining that authority, which Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers does.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Transparent: Out-of-the-loop operation moves the whole review to afterward. Implementation 4.5.2: Establish Deterministic Boundary Constraints for Automated Regimes requires a log when a deterministic boundary blocks a command, but autonomous actions that stay within bounds depend on telemetry captured under Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities.
  • Secure: Where entry to or exit from an oversight mode depends on sensor, communications or system health signals, a manipulated signal can push the system into a less direct mode, or force repeated reversions. Making entry a deliberate act limits the first; the state-aware interlocks and rate-of-change validation in Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers limit the second. The time-constant analysis in Implementation 4.5.1: Define Engineering Time-Constant Thresholds also shows which paths outrun human intervention and rely on automated boundaries alone. An attacker could use it to aim at those paths. Protecting it is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accountable versus Safe: Where the operator can be relied upon to act, keeping a person in the loop contributes both accountability and safety. Where they cannot, the two separate; human oversight may identify a responsible party while making the system less safe, because a person who cannot act in time can delay or interrupt the process at a point where the delay is itself hazardous. One way to balance this is to move the human decision to a point where there is sufficient time and information to make it well, such as deciding in advance whether the system may run in this mode and on what conditions. Others include changing the process so that intervention becomes feasible, or not operating in that mode.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 3.2 (on defining human-AI configuration): This Task differentiates the human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop and human-out-of-the-loop oversight modes and fixes the boundaries between them; it does not cover defining the intervention procedure that applies once a person is in the loop. For that, see for example Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures.
  • MAP 1.1 (on defining operating context): This Task adds the process time constants to the documented operating context, so that the feasibility of real-time human oversight is established rather than assumed; it does not cover the intended purposes and user expectations that MAP 1.1 also names.
  • MEASURE 2.6 (on measuring safety): This Task covers the human reaction and execution times that a response-time safety metric has to be set against, and requires deterministic boundaries able to execute a fallback state with no human present; it does not cover producing evidence that those boundaries hold. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which operating contexts run human-on-the-loop or human-out-of-the-loop, and which role holds the authority to approve them? (see the Accountable versus Safe tension)
  • Where neither a person nor an automated boundary can act in time on a path, does the organization change the process, add a protection, or not operate in that mode?

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 4.2, Embed Safety and Failsafe Mechanisms, asks that AI systems be able to fail gracefully without disrupting critical operations, and that the new failure states an AI system introduces, including how to bypass or replace it, be written into existing functional safety and incident response procedures. Those are the deterministic protections this Task calls for where real-time human intervention is infeasible.
  • Standard ISA-84 / IEC 61511, Functional Safety: Safety Instrumented Systems for the Process Industry Sector (defining automated protective layers independent of human operator loops).
  • Guidance FAA AC 120-71B, Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers, 10 January 2017, from the United States Federal Aviation Administration. Paragraph 6.9.1 records that safety data have shown autoflight mode confusion to be a potential vulnerability, and asks that pilots be able to identify, interpret and anticipate autoflight modes. Paragraph 6.9.2 describes the flight mode annunciators as the source for depicting “who is doing what.” This bears on making the oversight mode in force evident to personnel.
