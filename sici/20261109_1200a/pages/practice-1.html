Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success

Define requirements and benchmarks for performance, safety, and security across the entire end-to-end system, including hardware, control logic, data sources, human operators, as well as automated, agentic, and SI components.

Requirements set before a solution is chosen give the organization a baseline for judging existing and proposed systems. They help it check that those systems align with its mission and operational resilience goals while achieving the desired improvements. This helps ensure that where SI is adopted, it delivers measurable operational value. These requirements provide a verifiable framework for system acceptance and ongoing oversight, supporting clear accountability and audit-readiness throughout the asset lifecycle. Because these requirements are defined independently of any particular solution, a valid outcome of this Practice is the determination that SI is not the appropriate means of meeting them here, whether because a non-SI approach serves them better, or because a local constraint prevents an otherwise suitable SI system from meeting them in this specific deployment. This Practice is related to several others, for example Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations, which helps ensure that any trade-offs still result in acceptable overall levels of robustness, resilience, and quality of service, and Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior, which sets the policies that bound SI behavior once the system is running.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Valid: Contributes the statement of what success means for this deployment, written before a solution is chosen, so that whether an SI system is the right means here is decided on evidence rather than assumed, and revisited when the operating context changes (Implementation 1.1.3: Establish Triggers for Re-Validating Requirements on Change of Operating Context). This Practice does not cover the safeguards that maintain service and safety when performance departs from those requirements. For that, see Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations.
  • Accurate: Contributes the reference that performance is judged against, set across the end-to-end system rather than the SI component alone, with minimum and preferred levels stated in advance. This Practice does not cover setting the consequence-based thresholds for how much of a given kind of error the organization is willing to carry, or the control responses when they are crossed. For that, see Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses.
  • Accountable: Contributes acceptance criteria fixed in advance, together with evidence that they were met, so that a decision to proceed rests on a documented bar and can be reviewed against it later. This Practice does not cover capturing what the system did once in service. For that, see Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Contributes security requirements set alongside performance and safety for the end-to-end system before a solution is chosen, including the adversities the system is expected to withstand, such as manipulated inputs, so that security is a condition a candidate SI system must meet rather than a property added afterward. This Practice does not cover mapping the ways an adversary could exploit the system once it is chosen. For that, see Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Valid versus Robust: Where a deployment’s conditions are stable and well understood, drawing the requirements tightly makes both the bar and the evidence clear. Where conditions vary widely, a tightly drawn scope makes acceptance easier while leaving behavior outside that scope uncharacterized, and the organization carries whatever falls there. One way to balance this is to require behavior beyond the stated scope to be characterized separately before acceptance, for example through the edge-case testing in Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV). Others include widening the scope and funding the evidence it then needs, or declining the deployment where neither is affordable.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 1.4 (on defining SI business value): This Practice calls for SI adoption to be justified by measurable operational value rather than purely technological optimization, and that the value be stated in terms that the organization can check later; it does not cover determining whether the trade-offs made to obtain that value deviate from acceptable service levels. For that, see for example Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations.
  • GOVERN 1.2 (on defining trustworthy SI policies): This Practice turns the Trustworthiness Characteristics into benchmarks that a specific system can be accepted or rejected against; it does not cover setting the standing policies that bound SI behavior once the system is running. For those, see for example Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior.
  • MEASURE 1.1 (on selecting and prioritizing measurements): This Practice covers performance, safety, and security benchmarks across the end-to-end system, and treats what cannot be measured directly as requiring additional verification rather than assumption; it does not cover enumerating the risks those measurements should be prioritized against. For that, see for example Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.
  • MANAGE 1.1 (on authorizing the system): This Practice covers the acceptance criteria and audit-ready evidence on which a proceed decision rests; it does not cover identifying who holds the authority to make that decision. For that, see for example Implementation 3.9.1: Establish Accountable Ownership.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who owns the requirements for each SI deployment, and who decides whether the evidence meets them?
  • Where the requirements show that SI is not the appropriate means, who makes that call, and what is done instead?
  • What time and expertise are set aside for requirements work, baselining, and testing, including that of operators and subject matter experts, and where does it come from?

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 2.1, “Consider the OT Business Case for AI Use,” is the closest external counterpart to this Practice: it treats whether to adopt SI as a question to be answered rather than assumed. Sections 4.1 and 4.2 cover monitoring and oversight mechanisms, and safety and failsafe mechanisms.
  • Guidance Roles and Responsibilities Framework for Artificial Intelligence in Critical Infrastructure, from the United States Department of Homeland Security. Part III, Critical Infrastructure Owners and Operators, sets out what that role is expected to carry, which is the perspective from which this Practice is written.
  • Standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023, Systems and software engineering: system life cycle processes, published jointly by ISO, IEC and IEEE. Clause 6.4.2, the stakeholder needs and requirements definition process, is the general systems-engineering form of what this Practice asks for, and is useful where an organization already runs a lifecycle process that SI requirements should join rather than sit beside.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-218A, Secure Software Development Practices for Generative AI and Dual-Use Foundation Models: An SSDF Community Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Tasks PO.1.1 and PO.1.2, under practice PO.1, Define Security Requirements for Software Development, cover identifying and documenting security requirements for the infrastructure and processes used to develop SI models and for the software built with them, and PO.1.2 recommendation R1 notes that such requirements may come from laws, regulations, contracts and standards. This is the security portion of the requirements this Practice establishes, from the side of an organization that develops or fine-tunes models; NIST SP 800-218A places the deployment and operation of SI systems outside its scope.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Appendix H.2, Stakeholder Needs and Requirements Definition, gives the security form of this Practice: its outcomes include characterizing the adversities presented by the environment, defining stakeholder protection needs, and transforming prioritized protection needs into stakeholder requirements, alongside security-oriented performance measures.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control SA-4, Acquisition Process, calls for security and privacy functional, strength of mechanism, and assurance requirements, together with acceptance criteria, to be included in the acquisition contract for a system, component, or service, which is where requirements set under this Practice reach a vendor.
