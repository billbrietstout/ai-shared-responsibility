Task 6.1: Develop Policies for SI Supply Chain Requirements

Supply chain requirements help an organization know what it is acquiring and set consistent expectations with vendors. Organizations with established procurement and third-party risk policies can build on them. Establish organizational policies that, for each application of AI, define minimum trustworthiness and visibility requirements for AI-related vendors and assets. These policies should provide a scalable framework where the rigor of due diligence, such as requirements for AIBOMs, model cards, data provenance attestations, and adversarial robustness testing, is based on the system’s criticality and the organization’s risk tolerance. By formalizing these expectations in procurement and service-level agreements, the organization helps set a consistent baseline for trust while remaining adaptable to varying resource levels and application threat profiles. Developing these policies with operations, engineering, cybersecurity and safety staff, alongside procurement and legal staff, can help them account for ways an SI component reaches operations that a single function may not recognize. Some SI components arrive without a vendor to contract with, such as openly published models and datasets. Where these come in through procurement or an approved intake, the same tiered requirements can apply to what is known of their provenance. Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems covers models obtained outside procurement.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Valid: Contributes procurement tests of fit for this deployment: attested representation of similar environments in the training data (Implementation 6.1.2: Require SIBOM for Mission-Critical Applications) and demonstrated proficiency in sector terminology and standards (Implementation 6.1.3: Domain-Specific Terminology and Contextual Attestation). This Task does not cover confirming fit in operation, which Task 1.3 and Implementation 2.3.1 do.
  • Robust: Contributes upstream characterization of behavior beyond expected conditions, in failure envelopes, boundary constraints and vendor-supplied test vectors (Implementation 6.1.4: Manufacturer Accountability and SI Resilient-by-Design), placing the initial profiling burden on the manufacturer. This Task does not cover setting the robustness expectations those envelopes are judged against, which Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Reliable: Due-diligence rigor is set once, from criticality assessed at acquisition, while Implementation 6.1.1: Add SI Disclosure to Existing Procurement Policies notes that AI features arrive during otherwise routine updates, so a component can drift away from the profile it was scoped against. Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management carries the re-evaluation trigger.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 6.1 (on establishing third-party risk management processes): This Task sets the minimum trustworthiness and visibility that a third-party AI policy should require, tiered by criticality; it does not cover enforcement once a vendor is engaged. For that, see for example Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management.
  • MAP 4.1 (on understanding AI component-level risks): This Task makes AIBOMs, model cards and provenance attestations the mechanism by which third-party component risk is mapped; it does not cover performing that mapping for a given system. For the mapping itself, see Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.
  • MAP 1.5 (on documenting risk tolerances): This Task consumes documented risk tolerances to set proportionate due-diligence tiers; it does not cover determining those tolerances. For those, see for example Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which functions take part in developing the supply chain policies, and who maintains them?
  • Who carries out due diligence on SI vendors for each tier, and with what expertise, time, and budget?
  • How are disclosure requirements tiered by application, and where would full disclosure narrow the supplier pool too far? (see the Transparent versus Resilient and any characteristic a more opaque system would improve tension in Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management)

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-218A, Secure Software Development Practices for Generative AI and Dual-Use Foundation Models: An SSDF Community Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2 describes a shared responsibility model in which an AI system acquirer can establish an agreement with the AI system producer or AI model producer specifying which party is responsible for each practice and task and how each will attest to conformance. Task PW.4.4 recommendations R1 and R2 ask that the integrity, provenance and security of acquired AI models and components, including datasets and adaptation layers, be verified, and that they be scanned and tested before use. Both give acquirers vocabulary for the requirements this Task places in procurement.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control PM-30, Supply Chain Risk Management Strategy, develops an organization-wide strategy that expresses supply chain risk appetite and tolerance and acceptable mitigation strategies, which is the policy level at which this Task has organizations set minimum requirements for AI vendors and assets. Control RA-9, Criticality Analysis, identifies critical components and functions to inform the prioritization of protection activities, which supports scaling due diligence to the criticality of each AI application.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning: Supply Chain Risks and Mitigations, March 2026, from the Australian Signals Directorate’s Australian Cyber Security Centre with co-sealing agencies that include the United States National Security Agency. Written for organizations that deploy or develop AI and ML systems, including those sourcing them from third parties. Its overview asks organizations to execute due diligence on vendors, include cyber security requirements in contracts, and require and maintain an AI Bill of Materials and Software Bill of Materials or equivalent documentation. Its Third-party services section suggests contract terms that restrict use of the organization’s data for training, define separate cloud residencies, grant audit rights and set continuity requirements. This bears on the requirements this Task places in procurement and service-level agreements.
  • Guidance AI Data Security: Best Practices for Securing Data Used to Train and Operate AI Systems, Cybersecurity Information Sheet, version 1.0, May 2025, from the NSA Artificial Intelligence Security Center, CISA, the FBI and partner agencies. In the Data supply chain section, under General risks for data consumers, it asks that foundation model developers provide assurances on the data and sources used and certify that their training data did not contain known compromised data. It suggests that consumers strongly consider requiring such a certification before using third-party data or foundation models. This bears on the data provenance attestations this Task names among the due-diligence requirements.
