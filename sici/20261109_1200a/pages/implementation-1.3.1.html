Implementation 1.3.1: Define TEVV Scope and Requirements per SI System

For AI systems deployed in safety-critical operational roles in critical infrastructure, define TEVV scope requirements that map to the categories defined in Implementation 1.1.2: Structured Requirements Mapping and are proportionate to consequence severity of errors identified during risk assessments within Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior and explicitly account for failure modes specific to critical infrastructure, including model drift, adversarial manipulation, and degraded-mode operation.

Where the organization already grades consequence through hazard analysis methods, such as hazard and operability studies (HAZOP) or failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA), those results can be used to scale TEVV rigor rather than a separate scheme being devised.

Test suites should be proportionate to the severity of the system’s potential physical consequences and evaluate performance across:

  • Nominal & Operating Envelope Testing: Validating system execution against the defined input/output ranges, setpoint limits, and latency ceilings established under Implementation 1.1.2: Structured Requirements Mapping.
  • Adverse & Adversarial Stress Testing: Assessing system stability and containment logic against the specific environmental stresses and attack vectors cataloged under Task 3.8 and Implementation 1.1.2.
  • Degraded-Mode & Fallback Verification: Verifying that when operating parameters exceed Implementation 1.1.2: Structured Requirements Mapping boundaries, the system predictably initiates derated operations or transitions to an independent safe fallback state (Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior).

Establish pass/fail criteria for each lifecycle stage including pre-deployment, post-incident, and periodic drift or degradation checks. Test and Evaluation (T\&E) planning documentation, of the kind already used for many traditional critical infrastructure systems to specify what will be tested, under what conditions, and against what success criteria before testing begins, provides a useful model for defining these requirements up front, rather than determining them ad hoc during testing.

Where an AI system’s outputs vary between runs on the same input, state how many repetitions each test needs and what proportion should pass. Demonstrating a low rate of rare, high-consequence failures by testing alone can need an impractically large number of trials, so record what mix of testing, analysis, simulation and architectural safeguards is relied on instead. Record what will not be tested and why, the assumptions on which the results depend, and the controls that compensate, so that these limits are visible to whoever decides whether to accept the risk. That person needs the information, time, and authority to make the decision, not only the role.

Define who designs, executes, reviews and accepts the tests, and how independent of the developer or supplier those roles are, with greater independence for higher-consequence systems. Independence can come from an internal function not involved in development or from a qualified external party. State which evidence supplied by a vendor is accepted as it stands and which is verified independently. Where few qualified people are available, for example in a small organization or a field office, one person may have to hold more than one of these roles. Where that happens, record it, and how the conflict is offset, such as by an external review of the test plan or of the results (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks).

Where an AI system cannot be shown to satisfy reliability requirements for autonomous operation, constrain it to a more restrictive oversight mode, appropriate to the consequence severity of the application, until TEVV evidence supports a less restrictive oversight mode (see Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation for specification of modes).

Confirm that the oversight capacity required by that mode is actually available, rather than assuming it: high algorithmic error rates can quickly exceed operator triage capacity, and that capacity is shared with every other system the same operators watch. Where it is not available, limit use of the AI system to what the available oversight capacity can support, as discussed in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation. To reduce the risk of accidental operational outages caused by evaluation overhead, procurement and engineering policies should explicitly avoid high-load active adversarial testing, automated input fuzzing, or dense red-teaming (testing a system by deliberately seeking to compromise it as an attacker would) vectors on live, production model paths.

All destructive profiling or exploratory boundary testing should be restricted to isolated staging environments, offline hardware-in-the-loop (HIL) test rigs, high-fidelity digital twins (detailed virtual replicas of physical equipment or processes that are kept up to date with real data), or during scheduled downtime, with sufficient time and resources to recover if necessary.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How is TEVV scope scaled to consequence severity, and who accepts what is left untested?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53Ar5, Assessing Security and Privacy Controls in Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Section 2.4.2, Assessment Methods, defines depth (rigor and level of detail) and coverage (scope and breadth) attributes for examination, interview and testing, each with basic, focused and comprehensive values, and notes that as assurance requirements increase, the rigor and scope of assessment tend to increase as well, which is a model for making TEVV scope proportionate to consequence severity as this Implementation asks.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement CA-2(1), Independent Assessors, describes impartial assessors as free from conflicts of interest regarding the development, operation or management of the system. It notes that independent assessments can come from within the organization or be contracted outside it, and that the authorizing official sets the required level of independence by risk. This bears on scaling evaluator independence to consequence.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Appendix F.7.4, the OT discussion of CA-8, Penetration Testing, notes that OT systems are sensitive to timing and limited resources, gives a replicated, virtualized or simulated system as a compensating control, and schedules any offline testing during planned outages where possible. Appendix C.3.4 records outages caused by testing on live systems.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 1.2 covers the secure AI system development lifecycle and Section 3.3 covers AI testing and evaluation.
  • Standard ISA/IEC 62443-4-1, Security for industrial automation and control systems, Part 4-1: Secure product development lifecycle requirements, from the International Society of Automation and the International Electrotechnical Commission. Clause 9, Practice 5: Security Verification and Validation Testing, is the industrial control equivalent of the verification requirements set here.
  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Sections 2.1 and 3.1 classify attacks on predictive and generative systems by learning stage, attacker goals and objectives, and attacker capabilities and knowledge, which can be used to structure the adversarial portion of lifecycle test coverage. Section 3.3.3, discussing evaluations of generative models against direct prompting attacks, notes that evaluations measure model vulnerabilities at a particular moment in time and that assessments may change as new attacks are developed, more data is collected after training, or capabilities improve. This supports setting pass/fail criteria for periodic and post-incident checks as well as for pre-deployment.
  • Regulation DoD Manual 5000.101, Section 4.1, a policy issuance of the United States Department of Defense. Covers the process for planning operational test and evaluation of SI-enabled systems.
