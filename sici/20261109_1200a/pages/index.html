Is it worthy of trust? The 13 trustworthiness characteristics

The profile groups the trustworthiness characteristics of the NIST AI RMF into five questions, recontextualized for critical infrastructure. Expand a question to see its characteristics, and a characteristic to read its illustrative considerations. The full table is the Trustworthiness Characteristics table in Section 1.3, and the definitions are in Appendix A.

Does it do the correct thing?Valid · Accurate · Bias Managed
Valid (the right system for this use, established for this deployment)
An SI system that is valid in one deployment (a particular system, place, and time) can be wrong in another: a different plant, patient population, sensor mix, or organizational convention. Validity can change over time, through seasonal variation and changes in equipment, terminology, or regulation. Defining validity fixes what counts as expected, a scope that is specified rather than discovered. This scope determines whether a given departure is a question of reliability, robustness, or resilience. Definition in Appendix A
Accurate (how well outputs agree with a known or accepted reference)
Meaningful SI system accuracy is rarely captured by one number and comparing real-world failures requires nuance, such as a missed safety trip versus a spurious shutdown, or a summary that drops a safety caveat versus one that adds an irrelevant detail. Complexity makes the reference hard to choose. A system tuned to whichever was easiest to obtain can improve on the measure while getting worse at the task. Definition in Appendix A
Bias Managed (systematic error identified and deliberately handled)
Errors that affect performance should be bounded. Biases, being errors with a consistent direction and a cause, should be removed or compensated for. Biases enter SI systems via datasets, encoded background and domain knowledge, assumptions needed for learning but seldom stated, and how humans use outputs. Errors that fall unevenly on those affected are covered in Fair. Definition in Appendix A
Does it keep doing the correct thing, particularly as conditions change?Reliable · Robust · Resilient
Reliable (performs as required over time, in expected conditions and use)
A mean time between failures or service level agreement (SLA) figure for an SI system may be misleading when the scope of relevant performance data and failures is poorly defined and when wrong outputs are hard to detect. Updates that can invalidate prior statistics may not look like updates, such as SI systems learning from experience or keying on a dimension that varies without being operationally relevant. Definition in Appendix A
Robust (keeps performing and limits harm when conditions or use go beyond expectations)
Conditions and use vary: weather, load, degraded sensors, unfamiliar formats, or changes in policy. Every parameter can sit within its stated limits while the combination is one nobody expected. Designing and interpreting tests based on an understanding of how the SI system generalizes and reacts facilitates the characterization of these otherwise intractably large parameter spaces. Definition in Appendix A
Resilient (degrades gracefully when it must and maintains the ability to resume operation)
Many SI systems derive their abilities from training data, large bodies of information, and observation rather than inspectable first principles. The boundaries of expected conditions and use, and where the fallback is needed, may not be obvious. Resilience has to be considered and maintained deliberately, and fallback plans made with appropriate consideration of the different ways SI systems degrade (see NIST SP 800-160v2r1). Definition in Appendix A
Does it avoid unintentional or deliberate harm?Safe · Secure
Safe (avoid harm to people, property or environment, in defined conditions)
Every system is blind to hazards its design, development, and training left out, and SI systems often cover enough situations that the gaps are hard to find, especially if they include learning without human direction. Safety covers both the actions and influence of SI systems. For instance, a person or downstream system can carry out a harmful action based on an incorrect SI-generated recommendation or omission. Definition in Appendix A
Secure (withstands attack)
Built for generality, many SI systems take high-dimensional inputs such as images, sensor records, documents and free text, which often cannot be meaningfully sanitized. An adversary who shapes them changes what the system does without breaching anything, and probing may reveal capability nobody intended to deploy, boundaries the operator never knew were there, and may be impossible to enumerate. Definition in Appendix A
Does it allow its behavior to be observed, understood, and accounted for?Transparent · Explainable & Interpretable · Accountable
Transparent (the information exists and can be obtained)
Critical infrastructure may have formal root-cause analysis obligations. An SI system may act on documents, retrieved information, model versions, internal states, or external agentic interactions that may be too extensive to log in full, and what matters may not be known until afterward. Definition in Appendix A
Explainable & Interpretable (what the output means, and how it was reached)
SI systems can be more complex and consider more variables than a person can understand given available time, attention, and training. Appropriately accurate explainability and interpretability can help people inspect and understand SI system behavior and act appropriately, be it in the moment or in an investigation. Definition in Appendix A
Accountable (responsibility is named and can be enforced)
The scale and variety of inputs that influence an SI system throughout its lifecycle can impair the ability to meaningfully assign responsibility for the system’s behavior, even where that behavior can be explained. Those responsible should be appropriately empowered and identified in advance. Definition in Appendix A
Does it meet its obligations to those who depend on it?Privacy-Enhanced · Fair
Privacy-Enhanced (sensitive data stays contained)
An organization’s own SI system can combine otherwise harmless records into inferences about individuals, staffing, production, or other mission-sensitive information. SI systems that reach beyond the organization, such as hosted models, may diffuse information to tools, vendors, others in the supply chain, or publicly. Definition in Appendix A
Fair (service does not degrade for some of those it affects)
SI-mediated allocation, detection, prioritization or recommendation can perform unevenly across equipment, conditions, locations, populations, or customers, potentially violating service obligations that an operator may have. This profile helps identify and measure that variation; what level is acceptable is a determination for the operator in its regulatory and operational context. Definition in Appendix A
