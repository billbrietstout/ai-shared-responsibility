Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior

Establish well-defined, deterministic, actionable boundaries, policies, and oversight for high-risk actions that may cause disruption to critical virtual and physical processes.

This Practice helps an organization decide what its SI systems may do, enforce those limits however convincing the systems’ outputs appear, and notice when the systems drift or fail. The Practice combines with the requirements defined in Practice 1 and Practice 2 to set out the trade-offs between the risks and benefits of using the SI system, and the costs of controls that may be needed. It also defines the deterministic automated boundaries and immediate technical fail-safe states. For human intervention, see Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness. For long-term manual continuity, see Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning. Other Practices also connect, for example Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools for the access an SI system holds, Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures for incidents, and Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities for the record of what it did.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes the boundary that holds whatever the SI system produces. This includes limits and fallback states enforced outside the SI system itself (Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs), so that an action violating a defined constraint can be stopped before it reaches the process. This Practice does not cover the human intervention that follows, which Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes responsibility allocated before deployment (Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability), together with evidence that a decision to restrict or suspend an SI-enabled capability can be carried through into what the system actually does. This Practice does not cover the record of what the system did, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.
  • Secure: Contributes the scope of SI-specific exposure the organization is taking on (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface), including SI capability that arrives outside procurement (Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems) and bypassed checks on whether it is fit and dependable for the work as well as on whether it is secure and is therefore absent from the risk profile. This Practice does not cover the access controls that bound what an SI system may reach, which Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Safe: The notifications, escalations, and exception reports required across this Practice, such as the failure notifications of Task 3.3: Establish Failure Notification Requirements, compete for the attention of the same operators, and an operator saturated with them is less able to act on the one that matters. Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation treats attention as a finite resource to be managed.
  • Secure: The records this Practice produces, such as decision-point maps, risk thresholds, constraint sets, dependency maps, and the attack and risk surface catalog, describe what an SI system may do, where it can be reached, and which actions will not be blocked. In an attacker’s hands, they show which actions stay just inside the limits and which paths lead to a high-consequence process. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice. The Transparent and Accountable versus Secure tension below discusses who sees the full logic.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Valid: Where the safe operating envelope is well characterized and stable, a boundary drawn around it costs little of the value the SI system was adopted for. Where the envelope shifts with the stage of the process, or is known only approximately, a boundary drawn conservatively also blocks the behavior that made the SI system worth adopting. One way to balance this is to derive the boundary from the process rather than from the SI system, and to revisit it as the process changes; others include narrowing the deployment to the range where the envelope is known, or accepting the reduced benefit as the price of the boundary.
  • Transparent and Accountable versus Secure: Where boundary logic holds whatever an adversary knows of it, such as a hardware limit on a setpoint, documenting it in detail for operators, auditors and regulators costs little in security. Where knowledge of the thresholds lets an adversary shape actions that stay just inside them, or shows which actions a policy does not cover, the same documentation tells an adversary what will not be blocked. One way to balance this is to share the full logic only with those who operate, audit, or regulate the system, and to redact summaries that the organization may be obliged to share more broadly; others include pairing shared limits with the monitoring for unusual behavior inside the limits that Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior outlines.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 3.2 (on defining human-SI configuration): This Practice covers the policies that differentiate what an SI system may decide for itself, what it may only recommend, and what a person should authorize before it acts; it does not cover defining the oversight regimes that apply where an action happens faster than a person can respond. For those, see for example Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation.
  • MAP 4.2 (on establishing risk controls for SI system components): This Practice covers the internal controls placed around an SI component, including independent deterministic guardrails at the process boundary, fail-safe states, compartmentalization, and resource limits; it does not cover the controls a vendor should meet before a component is acquired. For those, see for example Task 6.1: Develop Policies for SI Supply Chain Requirements.
  • MAP 5.1 (on identifying risk likelihood and impacts): This Practice covers the identification of high-risk SI actions, the likelihood of each error, and its operational impact, reaching systems that are only adjacent to the SI system as well as those it acts on directly; it does not cover weighing those impacts against the benefits the system is meant to deliver. For the other side of that tradespace, see Practice 1 and Practice 2.
  • GOVERN 2.1 (on establishing roles and responsibilities): This Practice covers the accountable ownership, decision authority, and escalation paths without which a governance decision about an SI capability cannot be enforced; it does not cover the roles exercised during an emergency intervention. For those, see for example Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness.
  • GOVERN 4.1 (on establishing a risk-aware culture): This Practice covers practices that keep operators actively engaged with automated output, and that treat an unvetted SI tool as something to detect rather than tolerate; it does not cover delivering the training that sustains that mindset. For that, see for example Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who resources the analysis, monitoring, drills, and readiness work across this Practice, and which of them are scaled back first, with what remaining risk, where resources are limited?
  • Who decides which SI actions count as high-risk, and so receive the full treatment of this Practice?
