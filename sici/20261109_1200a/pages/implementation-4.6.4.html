Implementation 4.6.4: Distill Learned Models into Interpretable Models that Can Be Verified

Distilling a learned model into one that people can read can give an organization a model whose own reasoning it can check, and sometimes verify, though it reproduces the original’s decisions only approximately. The best-performing machine learning technique for an application is often not interpretable at the level of abstraction people need. Where a trained opaque model performs well but its reasoning cannot be usefully inspected, consider using it as a demonstrator to train a self-interpretable model, such as a decision tree or rule set, that reproduces its decisions over the conditions the deployment will meet. This is behavioral cloning, first used to learn control rules from the logged actions of human operators (see Sammut 1992), with a trained model, planner, search over a simulation or digital twin, or other demonstrator in place of the human expert (see Sheh 2017; Bastani 2018).

The self-interpretable model may perform better trained this way than on the original data. The opaque model may build on a foundation model, be fine-tuned, or be trained at a scale the self-interpretable model’s algorithm cannot match, and it can supply what the original data cannot: synthetic examples to fill gaps, labels for the conditions the self-interpretable model actually meets during training, estimates of how much worse each alternative would be, and, in simulation, rare or hazardous conditions, including information that will not be available at deployment (see Sheh 2017; Bastani 2018).

If the self-interpretable model is the one deployed, its trace accurately explains what the AI system did (Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components), and its rules can be reviewed by domain experts and checked against constraints such as those in Implementation 3.2.2: Define and Independently Enforce Deterministic Operational Constraints, in some cases formally (see Bastani 2018). It explains itself, not the opaque model: matching the opaque model’s outputs does not explain how the opaque model reached them (see NIST IR 8312). Where the opaque model remains the one deployed, the self-interpretable model can still be shown alongside it, with its measured agreement. This shows that a decision could have been reached by a line of reasoning a person can review, not that the opaque model reached it that way, so it should not be presented as an account of how the opaque model reached its outputs. Where the two disagree on a decision, no such line of reasoning should be shown for it, since the one on offer leads elsewhere. How much disagreement can be tolerated before the comparison stops being useful depends on the application and on the consequence of the decisions involved. Such distilled models can be more computationally efficient, allowing them to run offline on limited hardware.

The self-interpretable model need not copy its demonstrator. Its learning algorithm has its own inductive bias, a preference among hypotheses that fit the data equally well, such as for simpler rules. This can help: in early behavioral cloning of human aircraft pilots, the induced rules left out many of the pilots’ spurious actions, and the authors observed that the autopilot based on the self-interpretable model flew more smoothly than the pilots it learned from, while also providing insight into the decision-making process (see Sammut 1992). Some techniques for training self-interpretable models can also make use of background knowledge that the trained opaque model demonstrator lacks, such as engineering units, equations that model the system, quantities operators already use, or known constraints, so that their rules are stated in terms domain experts recognize and stay consistent with that knowledge (see Sammut 1992; Sheh 2021).

Measure and record how the self-interpretable model performs against ground truth, and under which conditions, and treat other conditions as untested under Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle. Where the two models disagree, investigate rather than assume either is right: the self-interpretable model may have filtered out an error, or lost something the opaque model had right. The opaque model can be kept offline to flag disagreements and generate further training cases. The rules of the self-interpretable model can also be read for what they rely on. A rule keyed to something with no causal bearing on the decision, such as an instrument identifier or a timestamp artifact, can be a sign that the opaque model has learned a shortcut, and is worth investigating in the opaque model as well as in the explanation (see Geirhos 2020). Where the self-interpretable model fails verification, the failure may lie in the opaque model it was distilled from rather than in the distillation; in one simulated control task, neither the opaque controller nor the tree distilled from it was stable (see Bastani 2018). Repeat the distillation and its verification when the opaque model is retrained or the process or its instrumentation changes, treating the change as an update under Task 7.5: Conduct Impact Assessments and Risk Management for SI System and Model Updates.

Where the self-interpretable model cannot perform well enough, the decision may depend on more than can be stated in an interpretable form, which is itself evidence for the choice described in Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output. Where the opaque model works on high-dimensional raw inputs such as images, waveforms or text, distilling only the decision stage that acts on its perception outputs may be practical, with those outputs entering the self-interpretable model as premises with their own uncertainty, as in Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components. Conversely, one that performs well may still be too complex to use under time pressure. Its size can be limited during training, trading some performance for an explanation people can follow (see Sammut 1992), and the rationale shown can be condensed as in Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components or reduced to what a responder needs as in Implementation 4.2.1: Develop Situational Awareness Processes and Procedures for Personnel Who May Need to Intervene in the SI System’s Operation.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Is a distilled model deployed in place of the opaque model, or shown alongside it with its measured agreement? (see the Accurate versus Explainable & Interpretable tension in Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output)
  • How much disagreement between the two is tolerated before the comparison stops being shown?
  • Who has the expertise and time to distill and verify the interpretable model, including after each retraining?

References:

  • Research Sammut, C., Hurst, S., Kedzier, D. and Michie, D., “Learning to Fly”, Machine Learning: Proceedings of the Ninth International Workshop (ML92), Morgan Kaufmann, 1992, pages 385 to 393. An early demonstration of behavioral cloning. The actions of human pilots in a flight simulator were logged and used to induce decision trees (with C4.5), which were converted into code and flown as an autopilot through take-off, climb, turn and landing. One autopilot was built per pilot, because different pilots flew the same plan in different ways. The authors report that the induced rules pruned away many of the pilots’ spurious actions, so the autopilot flew more smoothly than the person it learned from, which they note as of particular interest for safety-critical tasks. They also note that the rules are purely reactive, with no model of the domain’s causality, that rules built only from the primitive attributes logged by the simulator could not be understood by a human expert, and that higher-level attributes, supplied by the expert or constructed automatically, may be needed to simplify them.
  • Research Sheh, R., “‘Why Did You Do That?’ Explainable Intelligent Robots”, The Workshops of the Thirty-First AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI-17), Human-Aware Artificial Intelligence, WS-17-10. Describes behavioral cloning with decision trees, in which state-action pairs logged from a demonstrator train a model whose goal is to explain some aspect of the decision-making process, with the ability to replace the demonstrator a byproduct. Its section on cloning a non-causal agent uses as demonstrator a search with perfect information in a simulator, which could not itself be deployed, and treats the cloned tree as approximating that demonstrator’s policy from the noisy information the deployed agent actually has, while noting the gap between the two.
  • Research Sheh, R., “Explainable Artificial Intelligence Requirements for Safe, Intelligent Robots”, Proceedings of the 2021 IEEE International Conference on Intelligence and Safety for Robotics (ISR), pages 382 to 387. Identifies decision trees and inductive logic programming, which represent their decisions as IF-THEN-ELSE rules and first-order logic respectively, and application-specific models that include background knowledge of the application, such as equations of motion, with learned parameters that have a physical interpretation, as representations that directly support introspective explanations.
  • Research Bastani, O., Pu, Y. and Solar-Lezama, A., “Verifiable Reinforcement Learning via Policy Extraction”, Advances in Neural Information Processing Systems 31 (NeurIPS 2018). Describes VIPER, which uses a trained deep neural network policy as the demonstrator (the “oracle”) to train a decision tree policy by imitation learning, drawing on model compression and distillation, so that properties of the tree can be verified efficiently where verifying the network is typically very inefficient if not infeasible. In three simulated control tasks (a symbolic version of Atari Pong, a toy Pong game and cart-pole), extracted trees were verified for robustness, correctness and stability respectively. For cart-pole, neither the network nor the tree extracted from it was stable, and the stable tree was extracted from a different, model-based controller. Verification of the toy Pong tree found a case in which it could miss the ball; after a fix it was proved never to lose. Verifying correctness and stability relies on a model of the system dynamics (for cart-pole, an approximation to it), though robustness can be checked without one, and the guarantees apply to the tree rather than to the network it was extracted from.
  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Suggested actions MS-2.9-001 and MG-3.2-001 list model compression and surrogate models, and model compression and distillation, among the explanation techniques to apply and document for generative AI systems.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8312, Four Principles of Explainable Artificial Intelligence. Section 6.3 notes that an explanation that can mimic a closed-box model’s predictions is not thereby accurate as an explanation of it, and describes models built to match the original on real input data but to answer differently on the perturbed inputs that perturbation-based explainers generate, so that the explanation describes behavior the model does not show in use. This is why performance in this Implementation is measured and recorded over stated conditions, and why the distilled model is treated as explaining itself.
  • Research Geirhos, R. et al., “Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks”, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2020. Describes shortcuts as decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging conditions. Its examples include a pneumonia classifier that had learned to identify which hospital a scan came from, for example from a hospital-specific metal token on the scan. This bears on reading a distilled model’s rules for signs that the opaque model learned a shortcut.
