Implementation 3.8.2: System-Wide Risk Surface Mapping (Non-Adversarial Trustworthiness Exposure)

Map system vulnerabilities across all SICI Trustworthiness Characteristics where non-deterministic AI behavior can cause physical, operational, or administrative harm without an active attacker. For example:

  • Safety & Resilience: Unintended feedback loops or distribution shifts driving physical controllers out of safe limits.
  • Explainability & Forensic Auditability: Opaque decision pathways that impede real-time operator situational awareness or post-event root-cause analysis.
  • Privacy & Data Protection: Systemic ingestion or leakage of sensitive telemetry, facility blueprints, or personally identifiable information (PII) into retrieval repositories or prompts.
  • Fairness & Bias: Algorithmic distortion causing unequal resource dispatch, asymmetric hazard detection across different equipment ages/vendors, or biased administrative reviews.
  • Accuracy & Reliability: Plausible hallucinations in report summaries, diagnostic feeds, or AI-generated execution logic.
  • Resilience & Concentration: Many organizations relying on the same model, vendor, or hosted service, so that a single silent update or failure produces a correlated, sector-wide event that no single organization’s risk assessment can see. Shared reliance can also lower the quality of decisions in normal operation, without any single triggering event, and the effect can be hard to notice while it occurs (see Kleinberg and Raghavan 2021). Findings of this kind can be shared through Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning.

Where the sector already uses a structured hazard analysis method, such as a hazard and operability study (HAZOP), failure modes and effects analysis (FMEA) or system-theoretic process analysis (STPA), consider extending it to include the SI system as a component whose outputs can be wrong, late, missing, overconfident or outside their intended scope. Link each hazard found to the safeguards that address it, and record where there is none.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2 counts ecosystem-level risk among the scopes at which risk can arise, with algorithmic monocultures as its example. Its footnote describes these as repeated use of the same model or algorithm leading to correlated failures because multiple actors rely on it. Suggested action GV-1.3-005 names algorithmic monoculture among risks to track. This bears on the concentration example in this Implementation.
  • Research Kleinberg, J. and Raghavan, M., Algorithmic monoculture and social welfare, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences volume 118 number 22, 2021. It models many decision-makers relying on the same algorithm, with examples from employment and lending. Beyond the familiar risk of correlated failures in adverse conditions, it shows that shared reliance can lower the overall quality of decisions in normal operation, even when the shared algorithm is more accurate for each user alone. The Conclusion adds that such effects might be difficult to notice while they occur. This bears on the concentration example in this Implementation.
  • Commentary Artificial Intelligence Model Risk Management: Observations from a Thematic Review, Information Paper, December 2024, from the Monetary Authority of Singapore, drawn from a review of banks. Paragraph 7.2.1 counts among the added risks of third-party and generative AI the concentration risks that arise when multiple financial institutions, or even third-party providers, rely on common underlying generative AI models. This bears on the concentration example, as observed in one sector.
