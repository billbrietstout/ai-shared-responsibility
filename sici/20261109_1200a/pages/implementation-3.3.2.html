Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds

Drift thresholds help an organization act before shifts in operating conditions, data, or task take an SI system outside what it was trained and tested for. Establish quantitative monitoring and thresholds against which the AI system is continuously or periodically evaluated for how far the real-world operating environment, data inputs, task, and system context can shift from the historic conditions on which the system was trained before mitigations are put in place, defining the specific thresholds that determine when the periodic evaluations in Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations or the continuous monitoring in Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior should trigger a fail-safe response. Where ground-truth outcomes are delayed or never observed at runtime, as is common in predictive maintenance, alarm prioritization and fraud triage, define these thresholds on observable proxies that do not depend on labels, such as shifts in the distribution of inputs, shifts in the distribution of predictions or scores, operator override rates and guardrail interception rates, and apply label-dependent measures in the evaluations under Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations as outcomes become available.

Determine if exceeding a maximum drift threshold should result in notifications, forced fail-over, or other restrictions on the operation of the system. Minimum responses should include operator notification. For safety-critical applications, responses should include automatic restriction of AI authority or, where a deterministic backup system has been prepared, forced failover to it. A forced failover is itself a transition with its own hazards, such as a step change at handover, which Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior addresses. Drift thresholds should account for seasonal, operational, and environmental variation relevant to the critical infrastructure sector, in addition to the physical degradation and changes over time of underlying OT hardware. Set each threshold from the tighter of the range over which validation demonstrated acceptable performance and the error that the process can tolerate. Apparent good performance beyond the validated range should not by itself widen a threshold. Compare current behavior with the original baseline as well as with recent operation, so that slow drift that never crosses a short-window threshold can still be detected. Cumulative methods, such as CUSUM or EWMA control charts, can help detect small, persistent shifts. Averages across operating regimes, such as seasonal or load conditions, can mask a decline in one regime, and breaking measures down by regime can help reveal it. Drift can also arrive through the supply chain with no change in the process, for example a vendor update to a hosted model, a changed retrieval source, or a replaced sensor. Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management covers detecting unannounced changes of this kind.

For example, an AI model trained on summer demand patterns in a water distribution system should have explicit thresholds that detect and flag degraded performance during winter conditions before that degradation can cause operators to miss a safety-relevant alert. Similarly, a healthcare diagnostic AI should flag drift in clinical parameters when shifted between different patient demographics, and a financial liquidity model should trigger boundaries during sudden, non-historical macroeconomic shocks. Where output quality can degrade before any shift is detectable in input statistics, as with SI systems making investment recommendations or credit decisions, thresholds can also be set on performance against a human-reviewed benchmark sample on a defined cadence, such as the validated standard dataset in Implementation 2.3.1: Baseline SI System Performance. For SI systems that answer from what they learned in training, facts that changed after training, such as revised procedures, setpoints, or regulations, are a form of drift that input statistics will not show. Drift thresholds should be reviewed and recalibrated at a defined interval or whenever a significant change in the operational environment occurs. This implementation mitigates hazard risk associated with silent AI performance degradation. An AI model trained on historical operational data can progressively lose accuracy as the operational environment shifts, without generating any visible failure or alert. Operators could continue to rely on the AI’s outputs without realizing its reliability has degraded below acceptable thresholds.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What response, beyond operator notification, does exceeding each drift threshold trigger?
  • At what interval are the thresholds reviewed?

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Principle 4, Embed Oversight and Failsafe Practices Into AI and SI-enabled OT Systems, pages 18 to 20. Section 4.1 directs establishing safe operating bounds for OT devices that detect AI drift and model changes affecting safety and performance, and maintaining a known good state or thresholds for safe behavior. Section 4.2 covers the failsafe mechanisms a breached threshold can hand control to.
  • Research NIST AI 800-4, Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems, March 2026, from NIST. Section 3.2.1, Functionality, reports practitioners’ difficulty in establishing performance baselines and the deviation thresholds that trigger corrective action or review. It names missing ground-truth datasets as a barrier, quoting Dolin et al. that post-deployment labels are often delayed, costly or entirely unavailable. This bears on the label-free proxies in this Implementation.
  • Guidance SR 11-7 Attachment, Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management, April 2011, from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. Written for banks, its Section V treats a high override rate found in ongoing monitoring as a sign that the model may need revision. It also describes benchmarking against alternative data or models, and cautions against false comfort from a close match. These bear on the override-rate proxy and the benchmark-based thresholds here.
  • Research Dolin, P., Li, W., Dasarathy, G., and Berisha, V., Statistically Valid Post-Deployment Monitoring Should Be Standard for AI-Based Digital Health, arXiv:2506.05701v3, February 2026, from Arizona State University. Written for clinical AI, its Introduction notes that average performance metrics can mask degradation in specific patient subgroups. Section 5.1 tests performance against the pre-deployment baseline, and Appendix D describes CUSUM charts for persistent small changes and EWMA charts for gradual drift. These bear on the baseline comparison, control charts and regime breakdown here.
