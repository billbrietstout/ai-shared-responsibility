Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses

Non-punitive reporting helps an organization learn of SI near-misses and gradual degradation, including what its systems do not record, before they become incidents. Establish channels through which personnel can report AI-attributable near-misses, degradation, and anomalous behavior without bearing personal or organizational cost for the disclosure. Where the recipient of a report needs to be independent of the function responsible for the deployment, that channel may sit outside the organization; see Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route. Ensure that those who report through these channels are protected by consistently applied organizational policy, especially where they are not otherwise protected by statute or regulation. Such a policy can state in advance where protection ends, for example by covering honest errors and reports of how an SI system behaved while excluding reckless conduct or willful violation. Where labor representatives exist, the policy can be developed with them. Acknowledging reports, and telling the reporting community what was found and what changed as a result, can help sustain reporting. Where the organization already runs a near-miss or safety reporting program, these channels can extend it to SI rather than replace it.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes routes by which near-misses, degradation and anomalous behavior that personnel observe become known, including what the system itself did not record, by removing the costs that suppress disclosure: benefit-claiming separated from risk-reporting (Implementation 8.6.1: Separate Benefit-Claiming from Risk-Reporting), an identity-protecting route that may sit outside the organization (Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route), and supplier terms that do not penalize vendor disclosure (Implementation 8.6.3: Ensure Supplier Terms Do Not Penalize Degradation Disclosure). This Task does not cover determining what the system records, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.
  • Reliable: Contributes a way for gradual degradation to surface, where no single observation seems worth the cost of reporting, and for failure modes shared by peer operators to be ingested back into guardrails and drift thresholds (Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning). This Task does not cover setting those thresholds, which Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Near-miss reports, and the shared records built from them (Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning), describe weaknesses in SI systems and the controls around them, some not yet fixed. An attacker who obtained them could target those weaknesses before they are addressed. Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning covers de-identifying shared records and disclosing to the vendor before wider release. Protecting the internal reports is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accountable versus Transparent: Where a report concerns how an AI system behaved, protection from personal cost removes the main reason not to make it. Where the same report also discloses the reporter’s own action, such as a check skipped or an override not taken, that protection limits how far individual responsibility can be enforced on what the report reveals. One way to balance this is to state in advance what a report will and will not be used for, which Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route asks the organization to make clear to potential reporters; others include keeping the report as an information source separate from any inquiry into the same event.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 4.3 (on establishing AI incident response and information-sharing processes): This Task covers incident identification by personnel and sharing with peer operators through de-identified records and a trusted intermediary; it does not cover the AI testing GOVERN 4.3 also names. For that, see for example Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle.
  • MEASURE 3.3 (on enabling feedback, appeal, and override): This Task supports problem reporting by the personnel who operate and supervise AI systems, protected from the cost of reporting; it does not cover routes for impacted communities outside the organization to report problems, or a process to appeal system outcomes.
  • MANAGE 3.1 (on monitoring and responding to third-party risks): This Task supports monitoring of third-party AI risk by removing contractual disincentives for vendors to disclose degradation, behavioral change or known limitations; it does not cover applying the controls MANAGE 3.1 calls for. For those, see for example Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Where does protection for reporters end? (see the Accountable versus Transparent tension in Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses)
  • Where labor representatives exist, is the reporting policy developed with them?
  • Who receives and acts on near-miss reports, and do they have the time, resources, and authority to act on what the reports reveal?

References:

  • Working Document Shared AI Findings Exchange (SAFE), a proposed non-regulatory framework at request-for-comments stage as of October 2026, from the SAFE Working Group of the Open Secure AI Alliance. Sets out how AI incidents and near-misses can be shared across organizations, with specific notification timelines, which relates to the de-identified sharing in Implementation 8.6.4.
  • Research Trends in AI Incidents and Hazards Reported by the Media, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 53, February 2026, from the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation, a paper describing preliminary results and research in progress, as published in February 2026. Box 1.1 gives the OECD definitions of an AI incident, an event in which the development, use or malfunction of an AI system leads to harm, and an AI hazard, an event or circumstance that could plausibly lead to one. A near-miss reported under this Task falls within the second, which is broader.
  • Guidance DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles, June 2009, from the U.S. Department of Energy. Chapter 4, Culture & Leadership, under Create a Just Culture, balances zero tolerance for reckless conduct against confidence that honest errors will go unpunished. Attachment C, Establishing a Reporting Culture, notes that complete immunity is not feasible because some acts are culpable, and lists feedback to the reporting community as a feature of successful programs. Written for DOE facility operations, it bears on the boundary and feedback this Task adds.
