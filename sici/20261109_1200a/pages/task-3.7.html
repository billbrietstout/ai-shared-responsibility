Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems

Bringing unrecorded SI tools under review helps an organization account for SI capabilities that could otherwise act on its systems and data without appearing in any risk assessment. Establish organizational policies, procurement gates, and contractor onboarding workflows to detect, restrict, and manage risks associated with unvetted or unauthorized SI systems, locally run models obtained outside procurement, including freely available open-source models, and agentic tools brought into the operational environment by internal personnel or third-party contractors. In combination with Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance, this Task helps bring any AI capability utilized within the facility’s physical or logical boundary into the organization’s risk profile and configuration management baselines.

Transient SI systems, such as short-lived agents, pilots and experiments, are within scope while they run. Record them when they start and set an end date. When they end, withdraw the authority they held, as in Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result. Unvetted SI tools on hosts that can reach control systems, such as engineering workstations, maintenance laptops and remote access hosts, warrant particular attention. In the electric sector, for example, sharing operational reports with an external generative AI service raises concern about disclosure of Critical Energy Infrastructure Information (CEII). Control logic or configurations generated by such tools have not been through the verification in Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle. Organizations with established arrangements for shadow IT and unapproved software can extend those arrangements to SI tools.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure: Contributes visibility of AI capability that entered the environment outside procurement: contractor attestation, detection of traffic to public AI endpoints, and scanning for unapproved applications and local models, so an AI system operating outside the credential directory becomes known. This Task does not cover denying those entities access, which Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools covers.
  • Privacy-Enhanced: Contributes the point at which an undeclared external AI service becomes visible, where condition reports, operational records or facility information may otherwise leave through an endpoint nobody cataloged. This Task does not cover governing the dataflows of services the organization has authorized, which Task 6.3: Identify and Control Dataflows Across the SI Supply Chain covers.
  • Valid: Contributes the precondition for evaluating an SI tool at all. A tool brought in without a formal evaluation process has not been tested for this use. Even if it is never connected to anything, an engineer or operator may act on what it tells them, or copy its output into a configuration or a decision. This Task does not cover performing that evaluation, which Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Detection works by observing the traffic and endpoints that personnel and contractors use, so it also produces a record of how individuals work. How that record is governed, and by which function, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Existing workforce monitoring and privacy practice already handles comparable records, while a record of how individuals use SI tools may raise questions that practice was not written for. Record where governance of that record sits.
  • Secure: The attestations and detection results gathered here describe which SI tools are in use, and which endpoints and hosts are watched. Someone wishing to use an unvetted tool unseen, or an attacker looking for an unmonitored route out, could use them to avoid detection. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Secure, Valid and Privacy-Enhanced versus themselves: Where sanctioned tooling covers the work people need to do, restricting unvetted SI tools removes exposure, whether to attack, to untested output or to records leaving through an uncataloged service, at a modest cost. Where it leaves the need unmet, the same restriction moves the use to personal devices and accounts, which detection, logging and evaluation reach less well than the corporate network did. One way to balance this is to pair each restriction with a sanctioned route that meets the underlying need; others include a time-bounded permit for a declared tool under the attestation in Implementation 3.7.1: SI Attestation, or accepting the restriction and resourcing the gap it leaves.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 1.6 (on building the AI inventory): This Task brings AI that entered the environment without procurement, including contractor tooling and locally run models obtained outside procurement, into the same inventory as sanctioned systems; it does not cover registration of components the organization has deliberately acquired. For those, see for example Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management.
  • GOVERN 6.1 (on establishing third-party risk management processes): This Task treats contractor attestation and onboarding as the point at which third-party AI use becomes declarable at all; it does not cover setting the trustworthiness and visibility requirements that a vetted vendor should meet. For those, see for example Task 6.1: Develop Policies for SI Supply Chain Requirements.
  • MANAGE 3.1 (on monitoring and responding to third-party risks): This Task detects use of external commercial AI services by logging traffic to known API endpoints and scanning for unapproved models and containers; it does not cover monitoring the actions of AI entities that hold authorized credentials. For those, see for example Task 5.4: Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which restrictions on unvetted SI tools apply, and what sanctioned route meets the need each one leaves? (see the Secure, Valid and Privacy-Enhanced versus themselves tension)
  • Who owns detection of unvetted SI use and follow-up of what is found, given that it falls between IT security, business units, and contractors?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 6.3.1, Anomalies and Events, lists “Unauthorized devices connecting to the network” and “Unauthorized communication to external IPs” among events OT detection should recognize. The OT discussion of CM-7(5) in Appendix F.7.5 notes that the set of applications that run in OT is relatively static, making allowlisting practical.
  • Guidance AI Shared Responsibility Framework, version 1.0, Workstream 2: Preparing Defenders for a Changing Cybersecurity Landscape, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Page 4 names the condition this Task exists for: unsanctioned AI usage thriving in the gray areas between IT security, business units and external vendors, where, left unassigned, no single group monitors for rogue usage or owns the risk of sensitive data exposure. Page 15 gives a detection list covering discovery of ungoverned AI tool usage, network monitoring for AI service API calls and web usage, departmental AI spending and subscription tracking, and risk assessment of what is found, alongside criteria for when tool usage should escalate into a governed enterprise system. Its responsibility matrix places accountability for shadow AI detection on the operating organization rather than on any provider.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In Chapter 3, under Informational Leakage, it reports concerns raised in interviews and surveys about Critical Energy Infrastructure Information (CEII) being sent to or returned by generative AI systems. It notes that a generative tool may include information in a report that an operator would not have wanted there. This bears on the CEII example in this Task.
