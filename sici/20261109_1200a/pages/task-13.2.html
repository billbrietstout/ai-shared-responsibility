Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result

Withdrawing a retired system’s authority is the SI counterpart of offboarding a departing employee, and helps an organization keep a system it has retired from acting again. Remove every means by which a retired AI system can still act, and verify the removal rather than assuming it. This covers credentials and tokens, scheduled and event-driven triggers, authority delegated to other entities, and the compute resources that may still hold any of these. An AI system that retains an active trigger and a valid credential continues to act after it has been recorded as retired. Withdrawal extends to other instances of the system, for example copies in test environments, on supplier infrastructure or with a contractor. The inventory of what to withdraw can be drawn from identity and access records and from observed activity, as well as from the original design. Credentials created outside normal provisioning may appear only in logs. After retirement, monitoring for attempted use of the system’s identities, and for activity attributed to it in logs, can reveal authority that was missed. The monitoring period can be set from the longest interval between scheduled triggers and the longest remaining credential lifetime, and any activity found can be treated as a potential incident under Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure and Safe: Contributes the exhaustive and evidenced withdrawal of everything by which a retired system could act, extended through delegated authority and through the resources that may hold credentials independently of the identity being revoked, so that a trigger left in place is less likely to set it acting with no one watching it. This Task does not cover establishing the identities and revocation mechanisms being exercised, which Task 5.1: Establish Identities and Authentication for SI Entities covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes a record of what was revoked, what was confirmed, and what could not be confirmed, so that residual authority after a retirement is a tracked item rather than an unknown. This Task does not cover the logging infrastructure that record relies on, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Resilient: Withdrawing authority exhaustively, in a single ordered withdrawal, removes the option of a partial or staged retirement, so a dependency discovered afterward cannot be served by briefly restoring access. Ordering the steps within the withdrawal, such as removing write access to control systems before read access and monitoring, can keep the process in a known state while it is carried out. Identifying dependents under Implementation 13.3.3: Determine and Record What Happens to Everything that Depended on the System before this point is what makes the irreversibility acceptable.
  • Secure: The withdrawal record lists the system’s identities, triggers, delegations, and resources, and which revocations could not be confirmed. An attacker could use it to find authority that may still be live or a released name to register. Protecting it is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice, and the compensating controls in Implementation 13.2.2: Revoke Authority Delegated to Other Entities, and Record What Could Not Be Confirmed cover the residual authority itself.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Secure versus Accountable: Where nothing done under the retired system’s identity is likely to be questioned, deleting its accounts and keys closes the path to its authority completely. Where its past actions may need to be attributed, for an investigation or under a retention obligation, deleting the identity record can also remove what links those actions to it. One way to balance this is to revoke the credentials and destroy the keys while retaining the identity record, marked as retired, for the retention period; others include exporting the records that tie actions to the identity into the audit store before the identity is deleted.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding AI systems): This Task covers the mechanism by which a decommissioned system is actually prevented from acting, including authority it delegated to other entities; it does not cover the decision to decommission or the conditions that prompt it. For that, see for example Implementation 13.1.1: Identify Retirement Triggers, Including Those Outside the Organization’s Control.
  • GOVERN 1.7 (on establishing decommissioning processes): This Task covers the part of those procedures that helps keep phasing out from increasing risk, by recording any revocation that could not be confirmed and making the decision on whether to complete the retirement deliberately rather than by default; it does not cover what is preserved before teardown. For that, see for example Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Is the retired system’s identity record kept, marked as retired, for the retention period, or deleted once the records that tie actions to it are exported? (see the Secure versus Accountable tension in Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result)
  • In what order are the steps within the withdrawal taken, to keep the process in a known state?
  • How long does monitoring for use of the retired system’s identities continue after retirement?
  • Who confirms that each revocation took effect, and where the same person must both revoke and confirm, how is this recorded and compensated?

References:

  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Its Appendix C decommissioning workflow revokes certificates and tokens, deletes secrets and marks the identity as retired, while logs remain available for audits subject to data-retention policies. Its Appendix E expects an organization to be able to answer from logs which agents were active in a period and what permissions they held. This bears on the Secure versus Accountable tension in this Task.
