Implementation 4.4.2: Adapting Existing Alarm Management for Complex, Adaptive or Otherwise Unpredictable Systems

Organizations with mature, trusted alarm-management practices can build on them for AI-generated notifications, but may find that those practices don’t transfer cleanly to some AI systems. Traditional mitigations often assume alarms correspond to discrete, mechanical fault states (e.g., a stuck valve or tripped breaker).

Established practice treats an alarm as an indication that requires a response from personnel. An SI-generated notification that cannot be given a defined response can be logged or shown as status rather than presented as an alarm. An SI system’s notification behavior can change after an update, retraining, a change to its system prompts or prompt templates, configuration or input data, or a change in a model or service it obtains from a supplier, with no change to the process it monitors, so such changes can be treated as triggers to review its notifications against the alarm system’s criteria and rates.

Examples of ways in which AI-generated alarms can differ from those in more traditional systems include:

  • An AI system may report the same underlying error in slightly different ways.
  • Adaptive AI systems may respond inconsistently to an underlying error, resulting in hidden or inconsistent alarms.
  • Inconsistent AI alerts may result in only downstream systems alerting while the system that is the root cause stays silent.
  • Agentic systems can build dependencies dynamically and in ways that may not be documented, so a failure may cause unexpected and seemingly unrelated systems to send notifications of failure.
  • Machine learned detection algorithms and thresholds may be undocumented and applied inconsistently, making it difficult to determine what any given alert means.

These differences arise from complexity, adaptation and randomness, whether in the inputs (such as sensor noise) or in how the SI system produces its outputs, rather than from SI as such. Some existing automation, including systems that already use SI techniques, shows them too; SI tends to make them more common and more pronounced.

The dependency mapping in Implementation 3.5.1 and Implementation 3.5.2 can help make the dependency chains described above more visible in advance, even though it does not resolve the underlying detection problem. Some partial approaches, not specific to critical infrastructure or to AI, exist in the broader IT/software observability field, such as similarity-based alert clustering and distributed tracing, but these generally assume mature instrumentation that many current AI deployments, and especially OT environments, may not yet have in place. Adapting existing alarm rationalization, for example by rationalizing SI alerts by category rather than individually, may also serve as a partial approach where those categories are stable enough to document. This is particularly challenging in agentic systems, which can make dependency decisions dynamically and without documenting them. For multi-agent systems, observable symptoms of a fault spreading between agents can be monitored as alarm conditions in their own right, such as rapid fan-out from a single decision, oscillating retries or feedback loops between agents, and repeated identical requests. Rate limits and circuit breakers that throttle or pause propagation when such a symptom appears can help contain a feedback loop while personnel diagnose its cause. Limiting retries for each request and across a whole service, and avoiding retries at several layers at once, can also help, because attempts issued at each layer multiply.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which SI-generated notifications are presented as alarms, and which are logged or shown as status?

References:

  • Commentary Understanding and Applying the ANSI/ISA 18.2 Alarm Management Standard, 2010, from PAS, a commercial supplier of alarm management software and services. Section 11 summarizes the rationalization activities of ANSI/ISA-18.2-2009, including justifying the need for each alarm, determining its setpoint or logical condition and priority, and documenting the operator action and consequences. That per-alarm basis is the legacy practice whose assumptions this Implementation’s examples show SI-generated alarms may not meet.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, Version 2026, December 2025, from the Agentic Security Initiative of the OWASP Gen AI Security Project, a volunteer community project of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. ASI08, Cascading Failures, lists observable symptoms of a fault propagating across agents as detection hooks, including rapid fan-out, oscillating retries or feedback loops between agents, and downstream queue storms or repeated identical intents. Its mitigations include rate limiting that throttles or pauses on anomalies, and circuit breakers between planner and executor. These bear on the multi-agent feedback-loop case in this Implementation.
  • Commentary Ulrich, M., “Addressing Cascading Failures”, Chapter 22 of the Google SRE book, 2017, from Google, published by O’Reilly Media. Defines a cascading failure as one that grows through positive feedback. The Retries section advises limiting retries per request, a server-wide retry budget, and avoiding retries at several layers, since attempts multiply. It notes that overload produces secondary symptoms that can look like the root cause. These bear on the multi-agent feedback-loop case and on alerts that point away from the root cause.
