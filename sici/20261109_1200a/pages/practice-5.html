Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools

Establish IdAM controls for SI agents and tools appropriate to their roles, auditability, risk, and oversight, throughout the SI system lifecycle.

This Practice extends identity and access management for people and systems to SI agents and tools, so that each one is identified, limited to what its role needs, and traceable. Establishing these boundaries reduces the risk of malicious or unauthorized actions reaching risk-sensitive processes, and limits their impacts across the operational environment. Two kinds of boundary are involved. Access boundaries limit what an SI agent or tool can reach (least privilege). Action boundaries limit what it may do or set in motion with that access, at the point it acts (least agency) (Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities).

Together they reduce both the attack surface, by limiting unauthorized access points and lateral pathways, and the overall risk surface, by bounding an agent’s autonomous authority, operational scope, and “blast radius,” whether the risk results from adversarial action or another source of error (Task 3.5: Define and Control Risks to Adjacent and Related Systems). Both surfaces are defined in Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface. This applies zero trust principles to SI entities: an authenticated SI agent is not trusted with every action it can request, because an agent can act outside its intended scope without being compromised, so each consequential action is checked when it is taken, not only the access granted beforehand (Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies). Controls modeled after human user permissions are a starting point, but access and authority to act should be further restricted based on the actual need of individual SI agents and tools.

In general, authority that downstream agents or tools exercise at the request of a person or other agent should not exceed what the requester holds (Implementation 5.3.2: Task-Bounded and Revocable Agent Authority), so that the downstream agents or tools cannot be used to do what the requester could not. A different arrangement is sometimes deliberate: a downstream agent or tool may hold permissions the requester lacks, such as write access to a specialized device that may be hazardous if not used properly. While requesting people or agents may have permission to achieve an outcome with the device, such as causing it to enter a different operating mode, only the downstream agent or tool is trusted to do so properly, and in accordance with relevant risk management controls such as logging, rate limits, or oversight (Implementation 5.3.3: Separate Privilege via Encapsulated Downstream Agents and Tools). The first limits what can be passed on, while the second places access with the agent or tool that is trusted, and controlled, to exercise it.

Access controls rely on effective asset visibility. SI systems that are deployed without authorization, such as personal LLM-based assistants or models downloaded and run locally without approval, may bypass identity and other risk management controls. Keeping such systems out is covered by Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems. The IdAM framework can be combined with monitoring in Task 5.4: Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations to detect, flag, and deny directory access to unauthorized SI entities, whether a rogue device on an OT network port or a model running in an unvetted cloud container, and to detect authorized SI entities acting outside their action boundaries.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure: Contributes identity and access boundaries for SI agents and tools, so that each one is recognized, held to the access its role needs, and detected, and its authority narrowed or withdrawn, when it acts outside that role, including unvetted SI systems that try to gain access. This Practice does not cover governing how unvetted SI systems are kept from entering the operating environment in the first place. For that, see Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems.
  • Safe: Contributes a limit on how far a malfunctioning or compromised SI agent can reach into risk-sensitive processes, so consequence is bounded whether the cause is an attack or an error. This Practice does not cover the means for people to intervene once an action already permitted is going wrong, which Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes attribution: identities distinct enough that an action can be traced to the agent or tool that took it, allocated before deployment rather than reconstructed after an event. This Practice does not cover capturing the decision-level record of how the SI system arrived at that action, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Secure and Safe versus Reliable and Resilient: Where an SI agent’s role is settled and its needs are known, confining it to that role costs little in operation. Where an operational need appears outside what was anticipated, or the decision to grant access cannot be reached in time, the same confinement that bounds an attack or an error stops legitimate work, including recovery from an abnormal situation. One way to balance this is to settle in advance what the SI system should do when its authority cannot be confirmed, so the outcome is a designed behavior rather than an arbitrary one. Others include granting authority for a bounded period and renewing it as needed, as in Implementation 5.3.2: Task-Bounded and Revocable Agent Authority, or restricting what the organization depends on the SI agent to do.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 3.2 (on defining human-SI configuration): This Practice covers the machine side of that configuration: which actions an SI entity may take on its own, and which need human approval; it does not cover defining the oversight modes in which people supervise. For those, see for example Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation.
  • MAP 4.2 (on establishing risk controls for SI system components): This Practice treats identity, authorization and privilege limits as the internal controls applied to each SI component; it does not cover the deterministic constraints enforced at the physical process boundary. For those, see for example Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs.
  • GOVERN 1.6 (on building the SI inventory): This Practice extends the inventory to the credentialed SI entity, so that an entity missing from the directory is detected and denied at authentication; it does not cover the procurement gates that keep unvetted SI out. For those, see for example Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems.
  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding SI systems): This Practice gives each SI entity its own identity and credentials for accessing resources, so that one misbehaving agent or tool can be disengaged by revoking its access without shutting down others that share those resources; it does not cover retiring an SI asset at end of life. For that, see for example Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What should each SI system do when its authority cannot be confirmed in time? (see the Secure and Safe versus Reliable and Resilient tension)
  • Who owns the identity and access lifecycle of SI entities, from issuance to revocation, and is that the function that already manages service accounts, or another?
  • What staff time, expertise, and tooling are allocated to managing SI identities and reviewing their access, and how is the depth of the controls in this Practice set against them?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 5.2.3.4, Zero Trust Architecture, notes that the identity of person and non-person entities is an important aspect of zero trust, that shared credentials in OT could impact the ability to implement it fully, and that some OT components may not support the technologies it requires.
  • Working Document Accelerating the Adoption of Software and AI Agent Identity and Authorization, concept paper (draft, February 2026; public comment period February 5 to April 2, 2026) from the National Cybersecurity Center of Excellence at NIST. Section 1, Project Concept, lists potential focus areas for a planned project: identifying software and SI agents so that access management systems can distinguish agent from human identities, authorization of agents, access delegation linking specific user identities to agents, logging that links agent actions to the non-human identity, and tracking the provenance of prompts and data inputs. These correspond broadly to Tasks 5.1 to 5.4. Section 2 names the standards the project is considering, including OAuth 2.0 and 2.1, OpenID Connect, SPIFFE/SPIRE for cryptographic workload identity, SCIM for provisioning and revoking agent identities, and NGAC. The Note to Reviewers poses open questions on these topics, including key issuance and revocation for agents, how an agent proves its authority to perform a specific action, whether authorization can change with an agent’s context, and tamper-proof logging with non-repudiation back to human authorization. It is a call for input on an enterprise-focused project rather than guidance. It places Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and architectures using only an LLM with its training data out of scope, and excludes external agents from untrusted sources from this initial effort, noting that public-facing or individual agents could be addressed in future iterations.
  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Addresses this Practice’s subject directly. Section 3.1 holds that enterprises must treat agents as first-class identities, distinct from both human users and traditional service accounts, each with a unique persistent identity that survives its lifecycle and static and dynamic claims attached to it. Section 3.4 covers authorization and delegation, including the case of an agent acting on behalf of a user whose identity and intent are carried into the workflow, and separating an agent’s own baseline rights from the rights it exercises on that user’s behalf. For delegation across several hops, it holds that scope should narrow at each hop and not expand beyond the delegating principal’s effective permissions.
