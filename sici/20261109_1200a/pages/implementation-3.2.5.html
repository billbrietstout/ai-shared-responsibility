Implementation 3.2.5: Artifact Verification

Because SI-generated code, configuration, and procedures can look correct while containing errors or planted content, independent checks on those with high potential for impact help an organization find problems that the generating system may not flag. AI-generated content, such as code, configuration, or procedures, with a high potential for impact (see Implementation 3.1.4: SI-Generated Artifact Driven Impact Analysis) should be checked via independent means. This should ideally involve non-AI or deterministic techniques. For generated code, this includes checking each package or library it imports against a list the organization has approved, rather than only confirming that the package exists in a public repository. Code-generating LLMs can name packages that do not exist, and an attacker can publish a malicious package under such a name (see Spracklen et al. 2025). Checks can also look for content the request did not call for, such as unexpected network connections, embedded credentials, disabled safety or logging functions, or text addressed to a downstream LLM or agent.

Where it is not possible to avoid the situation of AI systems checking AI outputs, significant care should be taken that any AI system used for verification shares as few failure modes with the AI system used for generation as practical (e.g., it does not use the same model or training data), and that what is shared is recorded, and that a human-in-the-loop oversight mode applies to the verification step, with the reviewer holding the authority to reject the artifact and sufficient oversight capacity to examine it. The presence of a reviewer is not by itself verification. Where human review is relied on for verification, include the reviewing role in the competency mapping under Task 9.1: Perform Role-Based SI Risk Competency Mapping and, where the consequences warrant it, in the readiness verification under Task 9.3: Establish Audit-Ready Systems for Verifying Workforce Readiness. See Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle for further discussion.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How rigorously is each artifact verified, given the impact traced under Implementation 3.1.4: SI-Generated Artifact Driven Impact Analysis?
  • Where no non-SI check exists for a type of artifact, which SI-based check and human review are accepted instead, and who decides?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 3.3.3, in describing LLM-based systems that classify inputs or outputs as harmful, notes that such detection systems are themselves vulnerable to attack and may have correlated failures with the main models they monitor, and points to research on constraining generation so that deterministic guardrails can be used. Both bear directly on this Implementation’s preference for non-AI verification and its caution about correlated failure modes when one AI system checks another.
  • Guidance NIST SP 1270, Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence, March 2022, from NIST. Section 3.3.1 states that expert-driven and human-in-the-loop practices are not intended to serve as oversight of AI systems, that experts are not necessarily trained to govern the use of an AI system they played no role in developing, and that without significant procedural and cultural support, optimistic expectations about how humans can serve in an oversight capacity are not borne out in practice. This supports this Implementation’s position that a reviewer’s presence is not by itself verification, and that authority to reject and capacity to examine have to be provided.
  • Research Spracklen, J., Wijewickrama, R., Sakib, A. H. M. N., Maiti, A., Viswanath, B., and Jadliwala, M., We Have a Package for You! A Comprehensive Analysis of Package Hallucinations by Code Generating LLMs, Proceedings of the 34th USENIX Security Symposium, August 2025, from the University of Texas at San Antonio, the University of Oklahoma and Virginia Tech. Across 576,000 code samples from 16 code-generating LLMs, it finds that at least 5.2 percent of packages named by commercial models, and 21.7 percent of those named by open-source models, do not exist. Section 1 notes that an adversary can publish a malicious package under such a name, and that comparing a name against a list of known packages does not detect this. This bears on the approved-list check here.
