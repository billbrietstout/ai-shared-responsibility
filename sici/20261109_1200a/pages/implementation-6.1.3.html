Implementation 6.1.3: Domain-Specific Terminology and Contextual Attestation

Establish procurement policies that require vendors to provide formal attestations or evidence, such as via Model Cards or AIBOM, demonstrating that pre-trained AI systems are proficient in the industry terminology, technical standards, and community-specific linguistic nuances of the target critical infrastructure sector. This gives an early indication of whether systems, particularly those used for report summarization, predictive maintenance, or customer interaction, accurately interpret the specialized “language of the mission” before they are introduced into the operational environment. Because an attestation describes the conditions the vendor tested rather than those of the deployment, procurement terms should also support the deployer’s own acceptance testing against its own data and terminology before the system enters service, for example through access to an evaluation instance and agreement in advance on how failures found in that testing will be addressed. The sector terminology dataset in Implementation 2.4.1: Benchmarking on Application-Specific Terminology is one basis for that testing. A model card can support the attestation where it states the intended uses and out-of-scope uses and reports performance under conditions like those of the deployment.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What acceptance testing does the deployer run before the system enters service, and how are failures found in it to be addressed with the vendor?

References:

  • Guidance Roles and Responsibilities Framework for Artificial Intelligence in Critical Infrastructure, from the United States Department of Homeland Security. In the section for Critical Infrastructure Owners and Operators, responsibility B.1, Use responsible procurement guidelines, asks these entities to confirm with AI developers that their AI products and services are tested, evaluated, validated and verified by operational and subject matter experts. This bears on the attestations this Implementation asks vendors to provide.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 3.3, Conduct Thorough AI Testing and Evaluation, suggests working with vendors to understand their testing and its dependencies, and moving an AI system into production only after sufficient testing in a non-production environment. This bears on the deployer’s own acceptance testing before the system enters service.
  • Research Model Cards for Model Reporting, Mitchell, M., Wu, S., Zaldivar, A., and others, January 2019, in FAT* ’19: Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency, from the ACM. Proposes model cards as short documents accompanying a trained model. Section 4.2, Intended Use, covers primary intended uses and users and out-of-scope uses. Section 4.3, Factors, asks for performance to be reported across relevant factors, including the instruments that capture the input and the environment in which the model is deployed. This bears on what a vendor’s model card can show about fit to the target sector.
  • Guidance Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management, SR Letter 11-7 Attachment, 4 April 2011, from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, written for the banking organizations they supervise. Section V, under Validation of Vendor and Other Third-Party Products, asks banks to require vendors to provide developmental evidence on product components, design and intended use. It states that banks are expected to validate their own use of vendor products, including how far the data used to develop a model is representative of the bank’s situation. This bears on pairing a vendor’s attestation with the deployer’s own acceptance testing.
