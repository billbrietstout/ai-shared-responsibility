Task 2.4: Perform Linguistic and Domain-Context Robustness Testing

Testing an SI system on the language people actually use in operation helps the organization find misreadings before they lead to wrong actions. Integrate domain-specific linguistic and contextual validation into the system’s TEVV protocols. This Task helps ensure that the AI system, especially when utilizing pre-trained models, remains robust and reliable when encountering specialized industry terminology, operational jargon, or community-specific language. The goal is to reduce failure modes where linguistic ambiguity or a lack of domain awareness leads to incorrect system actions, safety-critical “hallucinations,” or the misprioritization of critical infrastructure alerts. Test material should also reflect how people write and speak in operation, including misspellings, abbreviations, mixed languages, transcription errors from voice input and terse messages written under time pressure. Where available, it should include text written during upsets and emergencies as well as during routine operation. Changes to the organization’s own vocabulary, such as after new equipment, revised procedures or a merger, can call for repeating these tests, as can changes to the AI system, its prompts or its retrieval sources (Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations).

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Robust: Contributes invariance across the language the AI system meets in service, testing whether performance holds on sector jargon, role-specific meanings and terms whose local sense differs from conventional use (Implementation 2.4.1: Benchmarking on Application-Specific Terminology). This Task does not cover identifying which terminology and language varieties are in scope for a deployment, which Task 1.4: Establish the SI System’s Domain Knowledge and Linguistic Alignment Requirements covers.
  • Accurate: Contributes a check aimed at the error that reads as correct, using subject matter experts to spot-check and red-team outputs that sound plausible and are technically wrong (Implementation 2.4.3: Technical Accuracy and “Plausible Hallucination” Audits). This Task does not cover verifying an individual artifact before it is deployed, which Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle covers.
  • Fair: Contributes measurement of whether delivery holds across the service area, testing robustness against regional dialects and community-specific terminology for customer-facing and public-sector systems (Implementation 2.4.2: Localization and Community Validation). This Task does not cover determining what level of variation is acceptable, which remains a determination for the operator in its regulatory and operational context.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Test material drawn from shift logs, work orders, incident reports and customer messages can carry personal information, and also describes the organization’s equipment, procedures and vocabulary in detail. Handling it under the same controls as the records it came from can help keep testing from becoming a route by which that information leaves the organization.
  • Secure: Benchmark results reported by category of term show where the SI system misreads the organization’s language (Implementation 2.4.1: Benchmarking on Application-Specific Terminology). An attacker could use them to word a message or document so that the system misreads it. Protecting the results and the test material is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accurate versus Accurate: Where the terminology set is broad and refreshed from current operational language, measured precision on it tracks understanding in service. Where it is small and fixed, an AI system can be tuned toward it and improve on the measure while getting worse at the task. One way to balance this is the expert spot-check and red-team review in Implementation 2.4.3: Technical Accuracy and “Plausible Hallucination” Audits, which tests against judgment the dataset does not contain. Others include refreshing the terminology set from operational text as it changes, or holding part of it back from those tuning the system.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MEASURE 2.11 (on measuring fairness, accuracy, and bias): This Task covers robustness testing against regional dialects and community-specific terminology, so that information reaches the whole service area accurately; it does not cover identifying which communities and language varieties are in scope. For that, see for example Task 1.4: Establish the SI System’s Domain Knowledge and Linguistic Alignment Requirements.
  • MEASURE 2.5 (on measuring validity and reliability): This Task covers the linguistic half of generalizability, testing whether a model trained on general text stays reliable on sector jargon and role-specific meanings; it does not cover testing generalization across physical components and hardware. For that, see for example Task 2.5: Verify that Learned Behavior Is Robust to Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance.
  • MEASURE 4.1 (on gathering feedback on measurement approaches): This Task covers subject matter expert review and community validation as the means by which test material is tied to the deployment context; it does not cover the physical and process context that MEASURE 4.1 also expects measurement approaches to reflect. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How often is the terminology set refreshed, and what level of variation across language groups is accepted? (see the Accurate versus Accurate tension in Task 2.4: Perform Linguistic and Domain-Context Robustness Testing)
  • Who prepares and maintains the test material and expected answers, and how is the subject matter expert time for this resourced?

References:

  • Research Agentic AI in Organisations: Early Insights from Practitioner Interviews, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 65, 2026, from the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation and the Tokyo Centre of the GPAI Expert Community, drawing on interviews with 25 organizations. Section 4.1 reports that, outside predominantly English-speaking contexts, gaps in language performance affect agents’ ability to select and call tools accurately and to understand task intent, not only the text they produce, and cautions that evaluation focused on English may leave compliance gaps, relevant to this Task’s concern with linguistic failure leading to incorrect system actions.
