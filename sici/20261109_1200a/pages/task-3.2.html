Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs

Independent guardrails help an organization keep an SI system’s actions within defined safe limits even when the SI system itself is wrong or compromised. Enclose AI systems within hard-coded, deterministic guardrails (also known as safety wrappers or boundary logic) that are as independent of the AI system as practical. These guardrails detect when safety constraints are likely to be violated and take appropriate actions to maintain functional safety. Such actions may include limiting the AI system’s output, engaging fallback controllers and plans, notifying human operators, and logging. Independent, deterministic safety wrappers add a critical layer of defense that holds while a defect or vulnerability in the SI system is being fixed, whether that is a corrected model, data set or prompt awaiting review or an emergency software patch during an active exploit. By enforcing hard-coded constraints at the operational boundary, these wrappers can block, at machine speed, unsafe commands or invalid data transactions that violate defined constraints. This extends the zero trust principle of verifying at each boundary from access requests to the outputs of the AI system itself, and applies it to error as well as to attack.

Guardrail rigor should scale with how directly a system’s output reaches a physical process, not with how the system is labeled or procured. A system that directly optimizes, controls, or influences physical equipment, such as an AI adjusting valve pressure or grid dispatch, should default to the full deterministic, independent guardrail treatment described above. A general-purpose AI assistant used to summarize records, draft reports, or generate configuration files, automation code, or operational procedures may warrant lighter controls, such as content review, while its output stays purely informational. But because these assistants are often procured under standard IT or SaaS contracts, the path from assistant output to engineer review to deployed configuration can be missed by hazard review processes built around direct OT connections. Once that output is used to inform an operational decision or generate a deployed configuration or control artifact, guardrail rigor should be set by the consequence of that decision or artifact, as it would be if the system acted on the process directly.

Guardrail design should also account for whether the underlying process is continuous or batch-based: continuous processes, such as power generation, can often define a single fixed safe operating envelope, while batch processes, such as many chemical operations, may require different operating boundaries at each stage of the batch cycle, making a static guardrail definition insufficient. Align these measures with Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness and in particular Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation to integrate appropriate, timely, and feasible human intervention, and with Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities to support visibility into the AI system’s attempted behavior, even when guardrails block the action.

Identity and per-action authorization (Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools, in particular Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies) and decision-level records (Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies) operate on the same paths as these guardrails, between an AI system’s output and its effect. Organizations may find it useful to check these controls together for each such path, rather than one at a time, including looking for any route by which an action could reach its effect without passing through all of them.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes enforcement that does not depend on the AI system being correct: deterministic limits applied at the process boundary to every non-protective command source, so an unsafe command is stopped whatever produced it. It bounds the hazards that were identified and written as constraints, and it stays a mitigation rather than a closure. For the hazards not yet identified, see Task 3.8 and Implementation 3.8.3.
  • Resilient: Contributes the defined state the control system enters when a constraint is violated or the AI system becomes unavailable: holding last-known-safe setpoints, reverting to a conservative baseline, or de-escalating to a legacy deterministic controller, with the state-consistency rules that keep re-entry from producing an uncommanded action. This Task does not cover sustained operation in that state, which Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Reliable: The safety wrapper is a component in its own right, and a wrapper that has stopped enforcing looks much like one with nothing to stop. Implementation 3.4.3: Monitor Validation Control Integrity requires continuous monitoring of validation control states.
  • Safe: A wrapper that often blocks commands the process could have tolerated creates pressure to bypass or loosen it, and a bypassed wrapper can still appear to be in place. Recording how often each constraint blocks a command, and how often a block is later judged unnecessary, can make that pressure visible before it leads to a workaround.
  • Secure: A safety wrapper that other safeguards rely on becomes a target in its own right. Where the wrapper shares components, networks or credentials with the SI system or the control system, an attacker who compromises one of them may reach the wrapper too. SP 800-82r3 raises the same concern for safety systems that are combined with control systems (their Section 4.2.2). Task 7.3: Manage Version Control and Logging for Internal SI Safety Mechanisms protects the configuration of such wrappers from unauthorized modification. Implementation 3.4.3: Monitor Validation Control Integrity checks that configuration against its approved version. The constraint set and the intercept points of Implementation 3.2.1: Identify SI Output Intercept Points also show which commands will pass and which output paths are checked. An attacker could use them to shape an action that stays just inside the limits, or to find a path that bypasses the guardrail. Who may read them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Robust: Where the process holds one stable safe operating envelope, as many continuous processes do, a fixed deterministic boundary costs little of the AI system’s ability to handle varied conditions. Where the envelope changes at each stage of a batch, or an experienced engineer is working through a situation the boundary never anticipated, a static constraint also blocks the response the situation calls for. Options include defining the constraint separately for each stage of the cycle, or deriving it from the current process state rather than fixing it at design time. Where a static constraint still blocks a response the situation calls for, and the organization has established an explicit, authorized override under emergency safety protocols, as Implementation 3.2.2: Define and Independently Enforce Deterministic Operational Constraints provides for, the override is a further option. It carries its own considerations, such as the loss of protection while it is active and a bypass left in place after the work.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 4.2 (on establishing risk controls for AI system components): This Task specifies the control itself: a deterministic constraint enforced out of band at an identified intercept point, applied to every non-protective command source at the physical process boundary; it does not cover confirming that the control is still functioning once deployed. For that, see for example Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior.
  • MEASURE 2.6 (on measuring safety): This Task defines the fail-safe states the control system should enter when a constraint is violated or the AI system becomes unavailable, which is the behavior a safety evaluation is run against; it does not cover performing that evaluation. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).
  • MAP 3.5 (on establishing human oversight processes): This Task requires that where one AI system verifies another’s output, the human reviewer holds authority to reject the artifact and capacity to examine it, because the presence of a reviewer is not by itself verification; it does not cover validation of the artifact against specification and institutional knowledge. For that, see for example Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Where does each SI system sit between purely informational use and direct influence on a physical process, and so what guardrail rigor applies?
  • Where a guardrail cannot be made as independent as the consequence warrants, for example on legacy equipment, what compensating controls or reduced scope are accepted, and who accepts them? (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks)

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 4.2.5 notes that many AML mitigations are empirical and lack theoretical or provable guarantees, and that research points to theoretical limits, including the impossibility of model-based detection preventing all impermissible outputs. It holds that these limits do not remove the value of pre-deployment adversarial testing, and concludes that organizations delegating trust to models in high-stakes contexts may need to consider practices and measures beyond adversarial testing. Independent constraints placed outside the AI system, as in this Task, are one such measure.
  • Research Vassilev, A., “Robust AI Security and Alignment: A Sisyphean Endeavor?”, IEEE Security & Privacy, May/June 2026, pages 52 to 58. IEEE Security & Privacy is copublished by the IEEE Computer and Reliability Societies; the author is at NIST. Theorems II.1 and II.2 establish information-theoretic limits for AI systems based on large language models that accept text prompts: for any checker enforcing an organization’s policy on which prompts are out of policy, in ideal systems and in real systems with finite context windows, there exist adversarial prompts that evade it. Following Theorem II.2, the paper recommends continuous monitoring, defense in depth by layering multiple imperfect systems, and preparing to withstand the failures that will occur. The paper presents these results as independent of system architecture, counts safety interlocks among the guardrails it considers, and suggests its approach may apply wherever compliance policies are enforced through technical constraints, so it does not exempt deterministic checks. Its call for layered, imperfect defenses parallels this Task’s treatment of its constraints as one layer, a mitigation rather than a closure.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Their Section 2.3.7, Safety Systems, describes a safety instrumented system as often independent of all other control systems, so that a failure of the basic process control system does not impair it. It notes that integrated control and safety systems, which communicate with the control system, still have to meet IEC 61511. This is the OT precedent for a guardrail that holds when the system it guards fails. Their Section 4.2.2, Safety Systems, notes that combining control and safety functions, components or networks could allow a sophisticated attacker access to both, and says that organizations should ensure adequate separation consistent with the risk of compromise.
  • Standard IEC 61511-1:2016, Functional safety: Safety instrumented systems for the process industry sector, Part 1: Framework, definitions, system, hardware and application programming requirements, 2016, from the International Electrotechnical Commission. The process-industry standard for safety instrumented systems, which SP 800-82r3 describes as the automated functions that bring a process to a safe state when predetermined thresholds are violated, and as designed in accordance with IEC 61511. Process-sector practitioners already assess the independence of such protection layers, and this gives them an established meaning for the independence this Task asks of its guardrails.
