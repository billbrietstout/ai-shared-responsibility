Implementation 4.4.1: Recognizing Novel SI-Driven Alert Floods in Unfamiliar Domains

Recognizing an alert flood early lets an organization apply mitigation techniques that other sectors have already developed. Domains with no prior experience managing alarm floods may not recognize the pattern when AI introduces it, or know that established mitigation techniques exist. For example, a fraud-detection system entering a confused state can flood a review queue with false positives; a summarization tool can generate excessive content when it should stay silent; a hospital’s AI-assisted clinical decision support can generate alert volumes comparable to those long documented in traditional medical device alarm fatigue, but without the institutional awareness built up around that older problem. Simple measurements can help recognize the pattern early, such as the average notification rate over a week of normal operation, the few sources that produce most notifications, the number in set periods after an upset, and how useful personnel find the notifications. Measures to reduce this risk range from training personnel to recognize alert-flood patterns as they emerge in the tool they’re using and developing procedures for manual prioritization, to automated triage and consolidation of output (e.g., rate-limiting generated content, grouping duplicate flags). Other signs that notifications exceed what personnel can handle include notifications acknowledged with no resulting action, a growing backlog of items awaiting review, notifications that stand unresolved for long periods, and approval requests from SI systems being approved in batches. Counting notifications per person across all the SI systems that person attends to, rather than per system, can show a flood that no single system’s count reveals. A rate agreed in advance as a flood, with a defined response such as partial suspension of a non-essential SI system under Implementation 4.1.2: Establish Tiered Intervention Procedures, Training, and Authorization, can help personnel act on it.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What notification rate per person counts as a flood, and what response does it trigger?

References:

  • Research Bransby, M.L. and Jenkinson, J., The management of alarm systems, HSE Contract Research Report 166, 1998, funded by Great Britain’s Health and Safety Executive. Section 4 gives simple measures of alarm system performance: the average rate over a week (4.4), the ten most frequent alarms (4.5), the number in set periods after a major upset (4.6), and how useful operators find the alarms (4.8). A domain new to the problem can apply them to SI-generated notifications.
  • Guidance The Joint Commission (2013) Sentinel Event Alert 50: Medical device alarm safety in hospitals, 8 April 2013, from the US non-profit that accredits healthcare organizations. Estimates that between 85 and 99 percent of alarm signals do not require clinical intervention. It recommends inventorying alarm-equipped devices and setting guidelines for alarm settings that identify when alarm signals are not clinically necessary. This is the institutional awareness that this Implementation’s hospital example refers to.
