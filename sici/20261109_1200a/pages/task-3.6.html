Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation

To sustain safe operation when an SI system is unavailable or wrong, establish continuous readiness and training protocols that keep critical human capabilities exercised. Reliance on an SI system is often the intended result of adopting one, be it to make new aspects of the task possible or perform existing tasks with more efficiency. Where it takes over work people did before, it reduces the occasions on which those capabilities are used (see Bainbridge 1983). That the reliance is intended does not make this erosion less of a hazard. It makes it an expected consequence that this Task plans for.

Practice to sustain these capabilities has to be arranged rather than left to routine operation. This Task requires organizations to identify critical human capabilities that should be preserved, helping operators retain the technical expertise, cognitive situational awareness, and manual or legacy override proficiency necessary to manage operations during prolonged AI outages or catastrophic failures. Note that this includes full manual overrides (where system velocity makes this possible, see Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation), the ability to monitor and operate deterministic fallback or legacy systems (Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs), and decision-making processes necessary to perform appropriately risk-managed unplanned shutdowns (Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning). Organizations with established operator qualification and emergency drill programs can extend them to the skills an SI system takes over. Those without such programs can start from the manual procedures in Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Resilient: Contributes periodic exercise of the fallback path under real load: drills in which deterministic controllers and analog backups take over the operation while independent safety protections, such as trips and limits, stay active, which is what separates a documented fallback from a demonstrated one. This Task does not cover sustained operation once the fallback is carrying the process, which Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity covers.
  • Bias Managed: Contributes a bound on one human-cognitive error source, automation bias, by placing active verification at high-consequence boundary thresholds so that agreement with an AI output is an act rather than a default. This Task does not address the notification rate that produces desensitization, which Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Reliable: Simulating a failure of the AI system is itself an operational action, and a drill placed in a window judged low-risk can still meet a real disturbance. Implementation 3.6.2: Scheduled “Manual Control” Drills directs drills that cannot be run safely on the live system to non-production environments, which Implementation 1.3.2: Categorization of Testing Environments categorizes.
  • Privacy-Enhanced: The engagement indicators in Implementation 3.6.4: Monitor Aggregate Engagement Indicators are drawn from how individual operators work, so they also produce a record about individuals. How that record is governed, and by which function, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Existing workforce monitoring and privacy practice already handles comparable records, while indicators drawn from how individual operators work may also bear on labor arrangements that practice does not cover. Record where governance of that record sits.
  • Secure: Drill schedules and after action reports show when an operation will run without its SI system, and where the fallback or the operators fell short. An attacker could use them to time an attack for a drill window or to target a known weakness in the fallback. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Safe: Where verification steps are reserved for high-consequence boundary thresholds, operator engagement is spent where the consequence is greatest. Where they are applied widely, the same steps become routine, and a check performed routinely returns agreement without examination while still consuming capacity needed elsewhere. One way to balance this is the restriction to high-consequence thresholds in Implementation 3.6.3: Cognitive Engagement by Design, deployed with the calm technology and mixed-initiative choices in Implementation 4.4.4: Applying Consolidation and Low-Cognitive-Load Design Techniques; others include varying when the check falls so that it is not anticipated, having the operator record an assessment before the SI recommendation is shown, or moving the exercise into scheduled drills.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 3.4 (on defining operator proficiency): This Task identifies the manual and interpretive skills that day-to-day AI reliance erodes, and requires periodic audited verification that operators can still run the infrastructure without AI assistance; it does not cover delivering the instruction that builds those skills. For that, see for example Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias.
  • GOVERN 4.1 (on establishing a risk-aware culture): This Task designs the operator interface against passive monitoring, placing active verification steps at high-consequence boundaries so that trust in an AI output stays deliberate; it does not address the alert fatigue that too many such checkpoints would cause. For that, see for example Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who provides the time away from operating duties, the trainers, and any simulator that this Task calls for?
  • Where that resourcing is not available, how much reliance on the SI system is accepted, and what risk remains?

References:

  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In Chapter 3, Out of the Loop Syndrome extends the existing practice of maintaining reliability through a tool failure to AI systems, asking whether, if the tool failed spontaneously, operators understand the state of the system well enough to keep it operating reliably for a period of time. Automaticity describes how operators who verify a tool’s recommendations at first can, over time, shift to accepting them by habit, and cautions that remedies which force extreme attention onto operators tend to backfire.
  • Research Bainbridge, L., Ironies of Automation, Automatica volume 19 number 6, 1983, pages 775 to 779. Automatica is the journal of the International Federation of Automatic Control, an international body whose members are national control engineering organizations. A seminal paper on the human factors of automated process control. Its subject is the process-control computers of 1983, and the mechanisms it identifies are relevant to AI systems. Section 1.1.1 sets out why manual skills decay when unused, so that “a formerly experienced operator who has been monitoring an automated process may now be an inexperienced one”, and section 2.3 discusses what maintaining them requires, including hands-on control periods and simulator practice.
  • Guidance NIST SP 1270, Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence, March 2022, from NIST. The Glossary defines automation complacency as humans over-relying on automated systems or having their skills attenuated by that over-reliance, and Fig. 2 lists it among individual human biases. Section 3.3.1 describes how experts may lean on the presumed objectivity of automation. The definition covers both halves of this Task: the complacency and the skills degradation.
  • Guidance FAA AC 120-71B, Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers, 10 January 2017, from the United States Federal Aviation Administration. Paragraph 6.7, item 9, asks that pilots be trained to transition seamlessly between levels of flight guidance and flight control automation, including manual flight. Paragraph 6.9.2.2 suggests staying in the loop by mentally flying the aircraft while the autopilot flies it. Appendix A lists SAFO 13002, Manual Flight Operations, among related guidance. These are an aviation counterpart to keeping manual capability exercised under automation.
