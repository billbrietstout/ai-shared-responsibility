Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions

Establish a risk-proportionate redundancy strategy to maintain continuous operations during AI degradation, distribution drift, or system failure. Redundancy requirements should be commensurate with consequence severity. This may range from heterogeneous AI model and infrastructure redundancy (for complex digital workflows where non-AI fallbacks are technically infeasible) up to deterministic, rule-based software algorithms or out-of-band independent deterministic physical interlocks (for high-consequence cyber-physical systems). This Task supports the system’s resilience through graceful degradation (operating in reduced capability or derated modes) or transition to a known fail-safe state, including handover to legacy/manual control, which reduces the impact of a total loss of service due to AI component failure. This Task complements Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior. Redundancy and fallback are familiar from safety and continuity engineering. What SI changes is that redundant models built on similar data, or hosted by the same provider, can fail together. Where a sector’s safety practice credits independent protection layers, such as a safety instrumented system designed to IEC 61511, neither the AI system nor a monitor of it should be credited as such a layer unless it meets that practice’s requirements for independence and integrity.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Resilient: Contributes the graded fallback that keeps a function available while the AI system is not: secondary models on distinct architectures, training distributions and hosting where a non-AI path is infeasible, rising to deterministic algorithms or physical interlocks where consequence is high (Implementation 2.2.1: Maintain Backup Systems Proportionate to Consequence). This Task does not cover defining the state that the control system enters once the AI system is disengaged, which Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior covers.
  • Reliable: Contributes an independent, non-AI check on whether the AI system is still performing as required, in continuous monitoring of output integrity, execution latency and setpoint stability (Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers). This Task does not cover producing the evidence that the fallback operates as intended when called on. For that, see Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV), and, for periodic confirmation that standby infrastructure still functions, Implementation 11.3.2: Maintain Isolated Standby Infrastructure and Allocated Reserves.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure and Safe: Each redundant path, and each monitor able to force a transition, is a further route by which the process can be commanded or stopped, whether by a deliberate actor or by a fault, such as a stale reading or a mis-set threshold that trips the fallback when nothing is wrong. Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers hardens the watchdog itself through read-only telemetry access and isolation from the AI application stack, which also keeps a malfunctioning SI system from disabling its own monitor, and tunes trip thresholds against false trips. Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface covers scoping the exposure that the wider redundancy strategy adds. Records of watchdog trip thresholds, and of how each watchdog responds to loss of contact, show an attacker how to force a fallback at a moment of their choosing. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice, as for other safety system settings.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Resilient: Where the process can absorb a transition at any point, tripping to the fallback on a watchdog signal serves continuity and safety together. Where the trip would land during an active process transition or a critical control maneuver, the transition is itself the hazard, and a corrupted input or a deliberate actor can force it. One way to balance this is the state-aware interlocks and rate-of-change validation on the trigger in Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers, so that the trip is qualified by what the process is doing. Others include sequencing the fallback so that it reaches a deterministic state in steps rather than at once.
  • Resilient versus Resilient: Where a non-AI path is infeasible, secondary models on distinct architectures, training distributions and hosting remove the single point of failure in the implementation. Where the shared exposure is to the operating environment instead, such as distribution drift that reaches every model built on similar data, that heterogeneity does less, while a deterministic algorithm or a physical interlock holds under the same drift and carries less of the function. One way to balance this is the redundancy gradient in Implementation 2.2.1: Maintain Backup Systems Proportionate to Consequence, which sets the degree of independence by the severity of consequences rather than from what is convenient to build. Others include pairing the two, with the deterministic path bounding the heterogeneous one.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding AI systems): This Task covers the independent, non-AI watchdog that disengages or supersedes an AI system whose output, latency or setpoint stability falls outside intended use; it does not cover defining the state that the control system enters once the AI system is disengaged. For that, see for example Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior.
  • MEASURE 2.6 (on measuring safety): This Task covers the redundancy gradient and watchdog controllers that make failing safely an implemented property rather than an assumed one; it does not cover producing the evidence that those mechanisms work. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).
  • GOVERN 6.2 (on establishing contingency plans for critical third parties): This Task covers the runtime contingency for a failing third-party model, in secondary models built on distinct architectures, training distributions and hosting infrastructure; it does not cover the pre-acquisition visibility that identifies which third-party dependencies are high-risk. For that, see for example Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How is the level of redundancy for each SI-driven function matched to consequence severity?
  • Where the trip could land during an active process transition, how is the watchdog’s response timed or interlocked? (see the Safe versus Resilient tension in Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions)
  • Who funds and maintains each backup path, including keeping it current with the primary and exercising it?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Section D.5.2.6, Maintain Redundancy, states that because malware can propagate across homogeneous resources, redundancy should be applied together with diversity and considered at multiple layers, and that the extent of redundancy is set by analysis of single points of failure and shared resources. The same reasoning applies to a failure mode shared by similar AI models, and it supports the heterogeneous and layered redundancy this Task asks for. Table D-2 introduces the use of heterogeneity to minimize common mode failures, with the purpose of limiting the loss of critical functions due to the failure of replicated common critical components.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 2.3.7, Safety Systems, describes the safety instrumented system as specifically designed in accordance with IEC 61511, and as often independent from all other control systems, so that a failure of the basic process control system will not impact its functionality. This bears on whether an AI system or its watchdog can be credited as an independent protection layer.
