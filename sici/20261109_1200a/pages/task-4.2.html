Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness

Implement role-appropriate mechanisms for providing personnel with rapid, actionable situational awareness as to the state of the AI system, its current mode and the authority delegated to it, its task, recent decisions, environment, and anomalies. The goal is to reduce the likelihood of humans making mistakes when overriding the AI system, particularly in chaotic, emergency, unusual, time-sensitive, and high pressure situations. This may take the form of displays, interfaces to existing information management tools, printouts, or a combination that aims to reduce the cognitive latency required for an operator to transition from passive monitoring to active intervention.

Risk management should account for the operational latency required to comprehend a machine-speed cascade from a cold start. Shift handover is one point at which awareness of the SI system’s recent behavior can be lost. Handover briefings can include what the SI system has been doing, including any anomalies and any changes in its mode of operation. Prioritize system learnability and transparency in interface design to help personnel rapidly form accurate mental models of AI behavior during both routine operations and emergency transitions. Care should be taken to ensure that the mechanisms do not cause cognitive overload or confusion, particularly for those who may be unfamiliar with the system, such as external emergency responders and maintenance personnel. See Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation for additional considerations.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Explainable & Interpretable: Contributes the operator-facing layer: what an output means now and what response it calls for, from uncertainty indications to triage cues. This Task does not cover determining whether the confidence and rationale it presents reflect how the output was produced. For that, see Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output.
  • Safe: Contributes the comprehension that makes an intervention correct rather than merely fast, including for external responders meeting a specific deployment for the first time. This Task does not cover the means of intervening, which Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Printed briefings, displays, and handover notes describe the SI system’s current mode, its recent anomalies, and how this installation differs from a generic one. An attacker who obtains them could learn which behavior personnel treat as normal, and so where an attack would look routine. Protecting them is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice, balanced against the need for responders to reach them quickly in an emergency.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Explainable & Interpretable versus Safe: Where the audience works with the AI system daily, more rationale can improve the decision. Where the audience is a responder under time pressure, it consumes the capacity to act, which is why Implementation 4.2.1: Develop Situational Awareness Processes and Procedures for Personnel Who May Need to Intervene in the SI System’s Operation gives responders only enough to choose an emergency mode. Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation handles the volume side of the same limit.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MAP 2.2 (on defining AI system utility and oversight needs): This Task puts the AI system’s knowledge limits in front of the operator at the moment of decision, through calibrated confidence bands, out-of-distribution warnings and standardized triage indicators; it does not cover the volume at which that information arrives. For that, see for example Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation.
  • MEASURE 1.2 (on continuously improving measurement): This Task submits the situational awareness mechanisms themselves to regular evaluation, checking both that they reflect the system’s true state and that their intended audience understands them; it does not cover evaluating the AI system’s own performance against its baselines. For that, see for example Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What does a shift handover briefing cover about the SI system’s recent behavior?
  • Who prepares and updates the situational awareness material for each group as the SI system and its installation change, and what resources does that need?

References:

  • Research Endsley, M.R. and Kiris, E.O., “The Out-of-the-Loop Performance Problem and Level of Control in Automation”, Human Factors 37(2), 1995, from the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society. Attributes much of the difficulty of taking over from failed automation to lost situation awareness: such operators may be slower to detect problems and need extra time to reorient themselves. In its experiment, with students on a fairly simple task, the shift from active to passive information processing was the most likely cause. This bears on the transition from passive monitoring to active intervention in this Task.
