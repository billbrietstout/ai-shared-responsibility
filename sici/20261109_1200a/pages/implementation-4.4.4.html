Implementation 4.4.4: Applying Consolidation and Low-Cognitive-Load Design Techniques

Once alarm flood risks are identified, apply techniques that reduce the rate and cognitive cost of notifications without hiding genuinely urgent, isolated signals. Examples of measures to consider include:

  • Implementing basic consolidation, such as grouping repeated identical alerts into a single notification.
  • Prioritizing notifications by the potential consequences if personnel fail to respond, using a small number of priority levels.
  • Suppressing notifications that follow predictably from a known root cause, or from a deliberate change of operating state such as a unit that personnel have shut down, while keeping the root-cause notification.
  • Applying user-interface design choices that reduce distraction (often referred to as calm technology or mixed-initiative interfaces) such as:
  • exception batching,
  • trend dashboards,
  • dynamic deadbands, or
  • time-delay hysteresis.

These should be matched to the specific application, oversight mode, and the range of personnel familiarity likely to encounter these notifications, from regular operators to occasional external responders. Testing consolidation logic with personnel against realistic scenarios, including one in which an isolated urgent signal arrives during a flood, can show whether it reduces load without hiding a distinct problem.

Note that there is a trade-off between many of these measures. For example, aggressive batching reduces fatigue but risks delaying a genuinely urgent, isolated signal. Where a notification is suppressed or shelved, keeping a list of shelved notifications that personnel can readily see, making it easy to restore one, and reviewing the list at shift handover can help keep a temporary measure from hiding the notification indefinitely.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which notifications are consolidated or suppressed, and how is the consolidation logic tested with personnel? (see the Safe versus Reliable tension in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation)

References:

  • Guidance Health and Safety Executive (2000) Better alarm handling, Chemicals Sheet No 6, from Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. For effective alarm prioritization, it suggests using about three priorities based on the potential consequences if the operator fails to respond. Step 2 gives quick technical measures from the EEMUA guide, including tuning nuisance alarm settings, adjusting deadbands on repeating alarms and suppressing alarms from out-of-service plant. These are process-industry counterparts to the measures in this Implementation.
  • Research Bransby, M.L. and Jenkinson, J., The management of alarm systems, HSE Contract Research Report 166, 1998, funded by Great Britain’s Health and Safety Executive. Section 5.3.2 rates deadband and shelving as excellent techniques for repeating alarms. Appendix 10 names three features of the shelving systems seen: quick access to the list of shelved alarms, easy unshelving, and a check of that list at shift changeover. These bear on the hysteresis and suppression measures here.
