Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management

Apply governance over external SI vendor dependencies and third-party assets, from pre-acquisition and procurement through the life of the contract, including vendor-issued updates and changes in the supply chain.

This Practice extends vendor and third-party risk management to the externally sourced models, data, and services that SI systems depend on. SI adds concerns that traditional supply chain oversight often misses. These include:

  • Data provenance
  • Data drift, including changes since a model’s training cutoff, and changes in the supply chain itself, such as a vendor replacing the model or a data source behind an unchanged product
  • Model integrity
  • Supervision of retrieval augmentation (systems that retrieve data to help generate their outputs)
  • Supervision of agentic systems, particularly in their dynamic agentic supply chain decisions

Retrieval-augmented and agentic systems have their own autonomy over the data they consider and over the flow of privileged information among numerous SI agents and tools.

Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management covers tracking vendor updates and changes in the supply chain once the SI system is in service. Where a vendor changes a hosted model without keeping earlier versions available, including where systems learn continuously through time, records of decisions made before the change may become hard to interpret (see Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities).

This Practice also sets out supply chain mechanisms, specifically machine-readable SI Bills of Materials (SIBOMs or AIBOMs) and the hazard reachability disclosures of Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures, needed to verify whether upstream third-party model hazards actively expand the organization’s reachable risk surface (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface) or have been rendered operationally unreachable by other controls. Unlike software bills of materials, which can be checked against shared vulnerability databases, SIBOMs describe model components and hazards for which no such database exists, so this profile treats model hazards through contextual hazard reachability (Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures) rather than through traditional vulnerability exchange (VEX)-style mechanisms alone.

Practices 6 and 7 are two sides of the same SI supply chain. This Practice covers what the organization can only obtain from vendors and third parties, through procurement terms, disclosures and attestations, from before acquisition through the life of the contract. Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance covers what the organization manages directly once a component is in its hands, including its own data, fine-tuning and configuration. A component can need both: a vendor model fine-tuned on the organization’s data depends on the vendor’s disclosures for what it was built from, and on Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance for what has been done to it since.

Note on terminology: In Practices 6 and 7, “SI supply chain” means the supply chain of the SI system itself: its models, data, software and services. SI systems used to manage an organization’s physical supply chain, such as logistics, routing or procurement optimization, are within scope as SI systems (Section 1.2) and are covered by the Practices that apply to any SI system, rather than by Practices 6 and 7.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes the pre-acquisition disclosure set for components the operator cannot instrument from the inside: SIBOMs, model cards, provenance attestations, and hazard reachability statements, obtained by contract rather than by logging. This Practice does not cover assets under direct organizational control, which Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes allocation of responsibility to the vendor in advance, through procurement terms and service-level agreements, rather than after an incident. Allocating responsibility does not make a vendor’s attestations true, so how far each is verified can follow how much risk management relies on it. Where verification is not feasible, the reliance can be limited instead, for example by treating what cannot be confirmed as unconfirmed, testing updates in staging before they take effect, tightening independent guardrails, or restricting what the SI system can reach. Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures applies this to reachability statements used to suppress alerts.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The disclosures this Practice requires list what a system is made of, how it fails, and what stands in the way of each hazard. In an attacker’s hands, this shows which weaknesses remain reachable and which safeguards to get around. Protecting those documents is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Transparent versus Resilient and any characteristic a more opaque system would improve: Where several suppliers can meet requirements, including those around supply chain disclosure, requiring full disclosure costs little. Where few can, the same requirement can narrow the choice to a small number of large vendors on which many operators in a sector then depend, or rule out a system with a more opaque supply chain, but that would otherwise improve on the one in service in other respects, such as accuracy, safety or security. The existing system, which may itself have a less transparent supply chain, might then stay in service longer. The balance depends on the alternatives available and on the consequence of the application. The tiering in Task 6.1: Develop Policies for SI Supply Chain Requirements, such as requiring SIBOMs only for mission-critical applications, is one way to strike this balance.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 6.1 (on establishing third-party risk management processes): This Practice covers the SI-specific disclosures, attestations, and inventory that such processes need to require; it does not address the intellectual property and rights infringement that GOVERN 6.1 also names.
  • GOVERN 6.2 (on establishing contingency plans for critical third parties): This Practice outlines the pre-acquisition visibility that makes a third-party failure detectable and an exit possible; it does not cover defining the contingency response itself. For the contingency response, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures for incidents and Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning for continuity of operations.
  • GOVERN 1.6 (on building the SI inventory): This Practice extends the asset inventory to externally sourced models, tools and services; it does not cover SI assets under direct organizational control. For those, see for example Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who owns SI supply chain risk, such as a third-party risk management function or procurement, and what technical expertise does it draw on?
  • How far is each vendor attestation verified, and where verification is not feasible, how is reliance on it limited? (see the Accountable contribution)

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 3.2 treats SI supply chain risk as software supply chain risk plus SI-specific dependencies such as data collection and third-party models, and section 3.2.2 reports research showing that backdoors in third-party pre-trained models can persist after downstream fine-tuning or additional safety training. Section 4.2.2 adds that organizations may not be able to audit vulnerabilities encoded in a model’s weights in the way it is often possible to audit open-source software. Both bear on this Practice’s reliance on disclosures and attestations obtained by contract and corroborated by the operator.
  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2.12, Value Chain and Component Integration, describes the risk this Practice addresses: generative SI systems depend on procured datasets, pre-trained models and software libraries that may be improperly obtained or not properly vetted, the risk is exacerbated by data scale and reuse of a limited number of foundation models, and issues in system behavior can be hard to attribute to any one component.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-37r2, Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations: A System Life Cycle Approach for Security and Privacy, from NIST. Section 2.8, Supply Chain Risk Management, holds that organizations are responsible and accountable for the risk incurred when using external products, systems and services, and associates such risks with an organization’s decreased visibility into how the technology it acquires is developed, integrated and deployed. It also notes that a system element presenting acceptable risk in a single use can raise risk to an unacceptable level through common or extended use across a system, organization, sector or nation, which bears on the case where many CI operators depend on the same externally provided model.
