Implementation 10.1.5: Define What Each Operational Record Is For

For each kind of operational record this profile asks for, such as decision-level logs, runtime telemetry, inference receipts, preserved state, and versioned test datasets, state what question the record is meant to answer, who uses it, and when: routine review (Task 10.3: Implement Systematic Audit Review and Performance Trend Analysis), incident reconstruction (Task 8.1: Identify Scenarios Requiring Deterministic Root Cause Analysis and Incident Reconstruction), audit, or the decision to return a system to service (Task 11.4: Establish System Integrity Re-Validation and Re-Commissioning Protocols). Specify what the record has to contain for that use, including the model version, configuration, and any other state or information that influenced the output, so that it stays interpretable after the SI system that produced it changes or is retired. A record that no one is expected to use adds storage, handling, and privacy exposure without the benefit it was kept for.

Weigh how much is recorded, and for how long, against what it costs to keep and review, the privacy of the people it describes, and the risk of not having it when it is needed, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Recording more is not necessarily better (see NIST SP 800-92r1 ipd). Where full capture is not feasible, for example because of data volume, constrained edge devices, or state spread across many machines, decide what is captured instead, such as a summary, a sample, or capture triggered by an alert, and record what can no longer be reconstructed as a result. That gap is a decision to keep with the other accepted risks for the capability (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks).

Depending on the system, what influenced an output can include the model version and its settings, system prompts and prompt templates, the input the system received for that output, including any documents or data added to it at the time, tool definitions, safety wrapper and guardrail settings, and preserved agent memory. Not all of it needs to be kept in full. Many records can be kept at a coarser level of abstraction, such as which step ran and with which version, rather than every exchange; others can be kept statistically, as counts, rates, or samples. Records of that kind can show what usually happened, or how often, but not with certainty what happened in a particular case. An investigation that relies on them may be able to say only that a cause is likely, not that it is established. Sampling also captures frequent behavior better than rare events, which may be the ones that matter most; some systems record more when something unusual is detected (see Sigelman 2010). Each choice is acceptable where the organization understands what it can then no longer reconstruct and has accepted that risk (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks).

This matters most for agentic systems, where one task may pass through several agents, tools, and services, some run by other organizations. There, what the organization can record may be limited to what crosses its own boundary and what its agreements with those organizations provide (Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management). A shared identifier passed along with each task, such as the one W3C Trace Context defines, lets records kept by different organizations be linked afterward. Agent identifiers and activity logs can be implemented at different levels of detail and intrusiveness (see Chan 2024). Which records are kept, at what level, and for how long, is part of the weighing above.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • For each operational record, what question does it answer, who uses it, and when?
  • Where full capture is not feasible, what is captured instead, and what can then not be reconstructed?
  • Who reviews the cost of keeping records against their use, and how often?

References:

  • Working Document NIST SP 800-92r1 ipd, Cybersecurity Log Management Planning Guide, initial public draft, October 2023, from NIST. Play TS-2, Define Target State for Log Generation, notes that “recording more log data is not necessarily better”, and that organizations should generally require logging only the necessary data, with recommendations for what else to log if resources permit.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control AU-2, Event Logging, calls for a rationale that the selected event types support after-the-fact investigation of incidents.
  • Guidance OMB M-26-14, Ensuring Effective and Efficient Agency Logging and Network Visibility to Defend Against Evolving Cyber Threats, May 2026, from the Office of Management and Budget. Notes that retaining vast quantities of logging data without clear utility proved neither operationally feasible nor cost-effective for most agencies, and sets logging maturity levels with graded retention.
  • Research Sigelman, B. H., et al., “Dapper, a Large-Scale Distributed Systems Tracing Infrastructure”, Google Technical Report dapper-2010-1, April 2010. Describes tracing requests across many services and recording only a sample of traces to keep overhead low, and notes that low sampling rates can miss important events in lower-traffic workloads.
  • Standard W3C, Trace Context, W3C Recommendation, 23 November 2021. Defines a shared trace identifier that lets trace data from multiple providers be linked across vendor and platform boundaries, and notes that recording only a subset of requests results in broken traces while recording all of them becomes prohibitively expensive at load.
  • Research Chan, A., et al., “Visibility into AI Agents”, FAccT ’24, 2024. Assesses agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logging, with implementations that vary in intrusiveness and informativeness, across centralized to decentralized deployments and the actors in the supply chain.
