Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output

Establish requirements that any confidence indication or rationale shown to personnel is derived from the process by which the AI system produced its output, and that rationale displays are designed and tested for whether they help personnel decide. This applies to routine decision support as well as to intervention. A rationale can be persuasive without being accurate as an explanation: an explanation constructed after the fact can look reasonable without corresponding to how the output was produced. For this reason, post-hoc attribution tools such as SHAP or LIME are not the primary source of the rationale shown to personnel; where their output is shown, it is treated as presentation rather than as an account of how the output was produced. Rationale displays have also been found to increase acceptance of incorrect recommendations as well as correct ones (Implementation 4.6.1: Design Rationale Displays that Give Personnel Something to Check).

How accurately a rationale can reflect the AI system’s process depends on how the AI system reasons. For learned components, confidence and rationale can be derived from calibrated probabilities and decision-level telemetry (Implementation 4.6.2: Derive Confidence and Rationale for Learned Components from Their Decision Processes). Where the AI system, or part of it, reasons symbolically or is a self-interpretable model, the rationale can be a trace of the steps it actually applied (Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components). Where a learned model performs well but cannot be inspected, an interpretable model distilled from it can sometimes be deployed in its place, so that the rationale is again such a trace (Implementation 4.6.4: Distill Learned Models into Interpretable Models that Can Be Verified).

In either case, a rationale shows at most how the AI system reached its output, not that the output is correct. This Task complements Task 4.2 and Task 4.4, which address what information reaches personnel and in what volume, by addressing whether the confidence and rationale in that information reflect how the output was produced. Recording the confidence and rationale that were displayed, alongside the output, under Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies lets a later review establish what personnel were shown when they decided. Those records stay meaningful only if the model version, prompts, configuration, and other state and information that influenced the output at the time are also kept, which adds storage and management overhead (Implementation 10.1.5: Define What Each Operational Record Is For; see also Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority).

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Explainable & Interpretable: Contributes explanation accuracy (see NIST IR 8312): the confidence and rationale that personnel see are derived from the process that produced the output, or are treated as presentation where they are not. This Task does not cover deciding how that information is laid out or when it reaches personnel. For that, see Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness.
  • Safe: Contributes a check against rationale displays that make incorrect recommendations more convincing: rationale is designed to be checked, and can be tested for whether personnel still reject incorrect recommendations. This Task does not cover the means of intervening. For that, see Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: Detailed confidence values and rationale can tell an adversary how the SI system responds to its inputs. Where an adversary can query a learned component and see its confidence scores, those scores can be used to craft inputs that it misclassifies (see NIST AI 100-2e2025). Limiting who can see the more detailed views, under Task 5.2: Determine and Implement Specific Access Requirements, can reduce this without withholding what personnel need to decide.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accurate versus Explainable & Interpretable: Where the knowledge a decision depends on can be stated as rules, constraints, or a self-interpretable model, the two need not separate: a symbolic or hybrid design can make the rationale an operator sees the reasoning the AI system actually used (Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components). Where that knowledge cannot readily be stated, a learned model may perform better, and its rationale can be checked only indirectly (Implementation 4.6.2: Derive Confidence and Rationale for Learned Components from Their Decision Processes). Whether interpretable models must in general give up accuracy is disputed (see NIST IR 8312), and the answer depends on how much of the domain can be stated, the same condition behind the tension in Task 1.4: Establish the SI System’s Domain Knowledge and Linguistic Alignment Requirements. One way to balance this, where the choice of approach is still open and operators must be able to check the reasoning at the moment of decision, is to weigh a symbolic or hybrid design for the part of the decision that can be stated. Others include distilling an interpretable model from a learned one and deploying it where it performs well enough for the application (Implementation 4.6.4: Distill Learned Models into Interpretable Models that Can Be Verified), relying more heavily on verifying the outputs of a learned model, or limiting its role to recommendations that personnel can check against independent evidence.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MEASURE 2.9 (on measuring interpretability and explainability): This Task requires that confidence and rationale shown to personnel derive from the process that produced the output, and rules out post-hoc attribution tools as the primary source; it does not cover capturing the decision-level telemetry that such a display depends on. For that, see for example Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which personnel see detailed confidence values and rationale, and which see only a summary? (see the Secure effect)
  • Where a learned model’s rationale can be checked only indirectly, which decisions is it limited to?

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2.2, Confabulation, notes that generative AI outputs may include confabulated logic or citations that purport to justify or explain an answer, for example logical steps presented for an answer that is itself incorrect, and that this may mislead people into inappropriately trusting the output. This is the case this Task addresses when it asks that rationale shown to personnel derive from how the output was produced.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8312, Four Principles of Explainable Artificial Intelligence. Section 2.3 distinguishes explanation accuracy (whether an explanation reflects how the system reached its output) from decision accuracy, and Section 2.4 describes knowledge limits (a system identifying cases it was not designed for or cannot answer reliably). Section 6.1 describes self-interpretable models, which are themselves the explanation, and records that whether they trade accuracy for interpretability is disputed. It covers models such as decision trees, decision lists, and regression, not knowledge-based or hybrid systems.
  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 2.2.2, Black-Box Evasion Attacks, describes score-based attacks, in which an attacker who can query a model and obtain its confidence scores uses them to craft adversarial examples. This bears on the Secure effect of showing detailed confidence values to personnel.
