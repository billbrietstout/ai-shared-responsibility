Implementation 6.3.1: Catalog Data Egress Points and Third-Party Processing Destinations

A catalog of egress points helps an organization decide what data may leave, and notice when data goes somewhere unexpected. Identify every point where organizational data leaves the environment to reach a third-party AI vendor or service, and document what data is sent, why, and where it is processed, so the organization has as complete a picture of its external data exposure as practical. Include data that vendors reach through remote, cloud or offshore access, not only data that the organization sends. For each destination, record where the data resides and whether the vendor may use it to train or improve its SI systems. Connected equipment with embedded SI capabilities, such as robots that rely on cloud services and remote maintenance, can send operational data to the vendor in normal operation. Include such equipment, not only the SI services the organization calls directly. Also record how long each destination retains the data and any further parties, such as sub-processors or model providers, to which it passes the data. An SI product can contact destinations that its documentation does not list, such as for telemetry, update checks or remote support. Comparing supplier disclosures and AI bills of materials with observed network traffic can help find them.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who maintains the catalog, and how often is it compared with observed network traffic?
  • How long may each destination keep the organization’s data, and to which further parties may it pass the data?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-47 Revision 1, Managing the Security of Information Exchanges, from NIST. Section 3.1.5.8, Logged in Tracking System, describes a log of information exchanges leaving the authorization boundary, kept in a spreadsheet, database, GRC tool or the system security plan. Section 3.1.4, Step 4: Identify Specific Protection Requirements, asks the parties to settle whether ownership transfers, how exchanged information is stored, whether it may be passed on to a third organization or system, and how it is destroyed, and to minimize the data exchanged. Those are the questions to answer for each external AI processing destination this Implementation catalogs.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 2.2, Manage OT Data Security Risks for AI Systems, asks operators to understand how AI vendors access and use their OT data, especially through remote, cloud or offshore access. Section 2.3 suggests a data usage policy that refers to data residency, communications paths, encryption and storage, noting that operators may not want vendors to train AI on operational data. Both bear on the fields this Implementation asks the catalog to record.
  • Working Document National Security Commission on Robotics for Advanced Manufacturing, Preliminary Report, October 2026. Under Finding 3, the section on foreign component and software dependencies notes that a modern robot often combines sensors, wireless connectivity, proprietary software, cloud services and remote maintenance. It reports a 2026 case in which data from consumer robot vacuums was sent to cloud servers abroad as part of standard operation. Under Finding 9 it notes that factories and other robot users worry about giving their supplier a view into how they operate. This bears on cataloging what connected equipment sends to its vendor in normal operation.
