Implementation 8.1.2: Identify SI Systems that May Not Satisfy Root Cause Analysis Requirements

Where an SI system’s behavior cannot be replayed after the event, other evidence and controls planned in advance can help compensate. Identify specific AI system classes that carry inherent forensic gaps that cannot be fully closed by techniques like runtime telemetry. These include large language model components with non-deterministic sampling, agentic systems with ephemeral context, and remote-hosted inference services without preserved telemetry. Retrieval-augmented systems whose document store changes between an incident and its investigation, and systems whose memory or learned parameters change during operation, carry similar gaps unless that state is preserved. Disabling sampling in large language model components does not by itself make them replayable. Batching, parallel floating-point arithmetic and changes to software libraries or hardware can also change their outputs, so whether replay is possible is better tested by re-running recorded inputs on the production configuration than taken from a provider’s description. In addition, some architectures and systems rely on inherent operational randomness that may not be able to be meaningfully replayed. These gaps concern reproducing the model’s output. The record of what action was requested, and whether and on what basis it was permitted, can still be complete and deterministic where it is generated by the enforcement or validation layer (Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking).

Post-hoc explainability tools such as those based on SHAP and LIME should not be treated as forensic proof of how or why an incident occurred. Such techniques estimate, after the fact, which inputs most influenced an output, for example by querying the system with perturbed inputs or by computing feature attributions. The result is one hypothesis for what drove the output, not a record of the actual inputs, system state or sequence of events in an incident. Where such systems are identified, technical managers should cross-reference with Task 8.2: Identify Additional Controls that May Be Required in Scenarios Where Deterministic Root Cause Analysis Is Not Possible to implement appropriate safety margins or alternative validation frameworks. What can serve as forensic evidence is a record made at the time of the output, such as the inference receipts and decision-level telemetry in Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking, the execution traces of symbolic, hybrid and self-interpretable components in Implementation 4.6.3: Display Execution Traces from Symbolic, Hybrid, and Self-Interpretable Components, and the provenance metadata in Implementation 12.1.2: Provenance and Traceability. Explanations that an SI system produces about its own output, including the reasoning text that some language models generate before answering, should likewise not be treated as forensic proof. Such text may not reflect the computation that produced the output (Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output).

References:

  • Commentary Utilities must ask AI vendors these questions to meet critical infrastructure protection standards, by E. Swidey, founder of Thirty Seven Inc., a company offering AI decision-making frameworks, 2026, from Utility Dive, a trade publication covering the electric utility sector. Argues that vendor explainability features typically generate post-hoc rationalizations rather than real-time audit trails, that auditors who catch backdated access logs will apply the same skepticism to reconstructed AI explanations, and that by the time of an audit the model that made a determination may have been retrained. Asks for a contemporaneous record of each determination’s inputs, decision logic and output, tamper-proof logging, and challenge by a separate system before a determination is final. Written about NERC CIP-015-1 monitoring tools; the argument parallels this Implementation wherever forensic reconstruction is a regulatory obligation.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 4.1 asks that logging be configured so AI decisions can be tracked for compliance and forensic analysis, and notes that explainability tools are designed to help developers understand a model and that it is an open question whether they would be adequate for OT environments.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8312, Four Principles of Explainable Artificial Intelligence, September 2021, from NIST. Section 6.2.1 describes LIME, which builds a local interpretable model by querying points near a decision, and SHAP, which assigns per-feature importance using Shapley values. Section 2.3 distinguishes explanation accuracy from decision accuracy. Together they support treating these outputs as explanations of a model’s behavior near an input, not as evidence of how a specific incident occurred.
  • Research A Systematic Review of Industrial IoT Anomaly Detection and the Forensic Interpretability Gap, by M. A. Ben Haha, A. Bohli, N. Haddour and R. Bouallegue, Electronics 15(11), 2240, May 2026, from MDPI. Reviews 48 studies from 2021 to 2025 across wastewater, manufacturing and energy settings and distinguishes post-hoc feature attribution, which explains why the model produced an anomaly score, from forensic diagnosis, which links the anomaly to a physical root cause. Notes that attribution methods such as SHAP and LIME cannot confirm whether a flagged sensor is the cause or a symptom of a failure elsewhere, and that most of the literature it reviewed offers only the former.
  • Research Fooling LIME and SHAP: Adversarial Attacks on Post hoc Explanation Methods, by D. Slack, S. Hilgard, E. Jia, S. Singh and H. Lakkaraju, Proceedings of the 2020 AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES ’20). Shows that post-hoc explanation techniques that rely on input perturbations are not reliable: a classifier can be wrapped so that it behaves as before on real inputs but detects the out-of-distribution perturbations these techniques generate and answers them differently, producing an explanation of the wrapper’s choosing. Supports this Implementation’s point that such explanations are not forensic proof, and that they can be manipulated.
