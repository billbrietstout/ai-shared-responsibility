Task 10.2: Establish Data Integrity and Tamper-Evidence Controls for Telemetry and Logging

Logs that cannot be quietly changed or lost help give an organization a record it can rely on when something goes wrong. Organizations that already protect their security logs this way can build on that protection for SI decision-level records. Implement access controls and security protocols, proportionate to how the logs will be used, that protect captured SI system logs from alteration and loss. This Task protects the “digital chain of custody.” It helps keep operational records from being altered or lost, whether by unauthorized modification, by a rollback or restart of the system they describe, or by storage pressure that deletes them early. It also helps audit data remain a source that regulatory oversight and liability management can rely on. Monitor the logging pipeline itself, so that a gap in the record shows as a failure rather than as a quiet period. Unexpected drops in log volume, gaps in record sequence and changes to logging configuration can be alerted on and handled as potential incidents under Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Some frameworks and obligations, such as SP 800-53’s AU-9(4) and AC-5, limit who manages audit logging. Whether the authority to manage logging, and to move or delete records, sits with roles separate from those that operate or administer the SI system is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Separation means no single role can both direct the SI system and alter the record of what it did, which AU-9(4) notes bears on the reliability of the record. Separation also adds staffing and coordination cost that a small operator may not be able to carry. Record the arrangement and, where duties are combined, the compensating controls.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Secure: Contributes protection of AI logs against unauthorized access, modification and deletion, with records held as far apart from the system they describe as practical, so that compromise of an AI system is less likely to compromise its record as well. Where storage, networks, or credentials are shared, record which. This Task does not make trustworthy a record whose content can only come from the AI system itself, a limit that Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking addresses by asking for that dependency to be documented.
  • Accountable: Contributes a digital chain of custody, with records held apart from the system they describe, so that its failure, rollback or restart does not erase them and the audit record remains a source that regulatory oversight and liability management can rely on. This Task does not cover designating who makes decisions about liability once an incident occurs, which Task 8.3: Designate Roles for SI Incident Governance covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Records protected against modification and deletion keep any personal information they hold past the point at which retention limits or deletion obligations may require its removal. Implementation 10.1.3: Forensic Integrity and Privacy-Preserving Log Partitioning limits which fields reach routine view, and Implementation 10.2.2: Protect Logs from Alteration Where They Serve as Evidence gives ways to reconcile the conflict for the records that remain.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Task covers the integrity protection that lets incident tracking and response treat AI logs as unaltered evidence, with records stored separately from the system being audited; it does not cover defining the incident response process itself. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.
  • MEASURE 2.8 (on measuring accountability and transparency): This Task covers the tamper-evident chain of custody on which documented findings about accountability for automated actions can rest; it does not cover examining the transparency and accountability risks that MEASURE 2.8 names.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Does the authority to manage logging, and to move or delete records, sit with roles separate from those that operate or administer the SI system, and if not, what compensating controls apply?
  • Which drops in log volume, gaps in sequence or configuration changes raise an alert?
  • How are protected records reconciled with retention limits and deletion obligations? (see the Privacy-Enhanced effect in Task 10.2: Establish Data Integrity and Tamper-Evidence Controls for Telemetry and Logging)
  • Who monitors the logging pipeline, and how are its alerts handled outside working hours?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control AU-9, Protection of Audit Information, protects audit information and logging tools from unauthorized access, modification and deletion and alerts on such events. Enhancement AU-9(2) stores audit records on a system separate from the one being audited, so that compromise of an AI system does not also compromise its records, and AU-9(3) protects their integrity cryptographically. Enhancement AU-9(4) limits management of audit logging to a subset of privileged users, noting that those who are also the subject of an audit may affect the reliability of its records. Control AU-5 has defined roles alerted when audit logging fails, including failures of capture mechanisms and exhaustion of storage. Their control AC-5, Separation of Duties, includes in its discussion ensuring that security personnel who administer access control functions do not also administer audit functions, and SP 800-53B selects AC-5 and AU-9(4) in its moderate and high baselines. These bear on separating log administration from SI system administration and on monitoring the logging pipeline.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-86, Guide to Integrating Forensic Techniques into Incident Response, August 2006, from NIST. Section 3.1.2, Acquiring the Data, describes a chain of custody as a log of each person who had physical custody of the evidence and the actions each performed and when, with the evidence stored securely when not in use. It describes integrity verification by computing message digests of the original and copied data and comparing them. Written for digital forensics generally, it sets out the practice behind the “digital chain of custody” that this Task protects.
