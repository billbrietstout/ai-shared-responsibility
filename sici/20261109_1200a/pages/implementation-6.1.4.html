Implementation 6.1.4: Manufacturer Accountability and SI Resilient-by-Design

Specify AI-specific “Resilient-by-Design” principles, that build upon conventional “Secure-by-Design” guidance during procurement. Vendors should be required to deliver pre-tested model boundary constraints and standardized behavioral validation datasets (test vectors) tailored to the operational context.

Because probabilistic machine learning models cannot be made entirely deterministic or immune to unexpected inputs, policies can require manufacturers to document explicitly how their systems behave under stress, such as failure envelopes for contextual drift, adversarial prompting, or data schema variance. Vendors should provide clear instructions on how the model safely degrades or transitions to backup logic when these boundaries are exceeded, shifting the burden of initial behavioral profiling upstream to the manufacturer. Where the vendor supplies a machine-readable bill of materials, it can also carry the model’s intended use cases, including its operational design domain, and its known technical limitations, such as the kinds of data on which the model is not expected to perform well. Procurement terms can also ask the manufacturer to state the conditions under which the operator can disable or bypass SI features without disrupting the underlying control function, and whether the product can perform its essential functions on premises or without continuous access to the vendor’s cloud. Where loss of a hosted service would be unacceptable for the application, that answer bears on whether the product is fit for it. The manufacturer can also be asked to notify the operator when it finds an indication that the SI system can provide improper advice or take inappropriate action, in addition to its usual vulnerability disclosure. Where a manufacturer cannot meet these expectations, record the gap and the compensating controls relied on, as Implementation 6.1.2: Require SIBOM for Mission-Critical Applications describes for gaps in an AIBOM.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Where a manufacturer cannot say how SI features can be disabled, or whether the product works without its cloud service, is the product still fit for the application?

References:

  • Guidance Guidelines for Secure AI System Development, 2023, published by the United Kingdom National Cyber Security Centre and the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency with twenty-one further national agencies. Organizes secure-by-design practice across four stages of the AI system lifecycle. It urges providers to follow the secure by design principles of CISA, the NCSC and international partners, which prioritize taking ownership of security outcomes for customers and embracing radical transparency and accountability. Section 1, Secure design, covers integrating the most secure settings into a system by default and applying restrictions to the actions an AI component may trigger, including non-AI fail-safes. Section 2, Secure development, asks that documentation cover intended scope and limitations, guardrails, cryptographic hashes or signatures, and potential failure modes. Section 3, Secure deployment, covers computing and sharing hashes or signatures of model files and datasets so that consuming systems can validate them, and being clear to users about known limitations and potential failure modes on release.
  • Guidance ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024, Artificial intelligence: functional safety and AI systems, a Technical Report published jointly by ISO and IEC. Addresses the use of AI within functions that carry a safety role, which is the question an operator faces when a vendor’s probabilistic component sits alongside deterministic protection.
  • Guidance Authoritative Guide to AI/ML-BOM: Drive Transparency, Compliance, and Security Across the AI Supply Chain, First Edition (Revision 1), 10 June 2026, from the CycloneDX AI/ML Working Group of the OWASP Foundation. The Model Design Considerations section describes fields for the model’s intended use cases, including its operational design domain, and for its technical limitations. These include the kinds of data on which the model should not be expected to perform well and the factors that might degrade its performance. This bears on documenting failure envelopes in a form a consuming system can read.
  • Guidance Implementing AI Bill of Materials (AI BOM) with SPDX 3.0: A Comprehensive Guide to Creating AI and Dataset Bill of Materials, October 2024, by K. Bennet, G. K. Rajbahadur, A. Suriyawongkul and K. Stewart, from Linux Foundation Research. Among the optional fields of the SPDX 3.0 AI Profile (Table 9 and Specific AIPackage Field Details), domain describes where the AI model can be expected to operate successfully, and limitation captures limitations of the AI package. These are the SPDX counterparts of the CycloneDX fields above for recording a failure envelope in machine-readable form.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 2.3, Understanding the Role of OT Vendors in AI Integration, asks operators to find out whether vendors can operate on premises or without continuous access to the internet or vendor cloud, and to specify conditions under which AI features can be disabled or enabled, with control given to the operator. It adds that vendors should disclose indications that the AI can provide improper advice or take inappropriate action. Section 4.2 asks that how to bypass or replace an AI system be written into functional safety processes. This bears on the manufacturer commitments asked for here.
