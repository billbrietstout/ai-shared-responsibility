Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning

Establish a strategic framework for mission continuity to manage immediate-term alternative operating modes, and the longer-term recovery and restoration of essential functions following significant SI-related failures.

This Practice helps an organization keep essential functions running through an SI-related failure, and highlights the need to pre-allocate recovery resources and deliberately prioritize service restoration. This Practice focuses on the “Plan B” when an incident occurs. Organizations with established continuity and disaster recovery plans can extend them to the ways SI systems fail, and those without such plans can start with the essential functions in Task 11.1: Identify and Prioritize SI-Dependent Essential Functions. Significant SI-related failures include an SI system that keeps running but whose outputs can no longer be trusted, for example after drift, data poisoning, or compromise, as well as an SI system that unexpectedly stops operating for any reason.

Without a way to keep essential functions running while an SI system is switched off, the authority to override or shut it down under Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness may be impractical to use, because using it would cause an unacceptable loss of service. Alternative operating modes that rely as little as practical on the SI system (Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity) help make that authority usable in practice.

This Practice dovetails with Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness, which focuses on technical aspects that support this Practice, and Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures, which focuses on governance obligations. For graceful degradation while the SI system is still running, see also Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations. For functions that continue after an SI system is retired, see also Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Resilient: Contributes the ability to run essential functions and fulfill obligations without the SI system for the outage durations in the organization’s design basis, and to return to automated operation afterward: restoration priorities, manual procedures, standby resources set aside in advance, and rehearsal. This Practice does not cover graceful degradation of the SI-enabled function while it is still running, which Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations covers, or the moment of human intervention, which Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness covers.
  • Safe: Contributes a defined set of checks (Implementation 11.4.1: Define a Checklist for Validating System Safety Before Re-Engaging SI Control) before control is handed back to the SI system, so that turning it back on is decided against evidence of system health rather than on the restoration of service alone. This Practice does not cover setting the test regime those checks draw on, which Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Fair: Setting restoration priorities means that, in an outage, the people served by lower-ranked functions wait longer for service to return. This Practice suggests ranking by consequence to life safety and service delivery. Task 11.1: Identify and Prioritize SI-Dependent Essential Functions leaves to the organization whether the ranking also weighs who bears the longer wait, and asks that the criteria be recorded.
  • Secure: The dependency map, restoration priorities, manual procedures, and exercise findings this Practice produces show which essential functions depend on which SI components, how the organization operates without them, and where its continuity arrangements are weakest. An attacker could use them to choose targets, to time an attack for when the organization is running on manual procedures, or to force a switch to a fallback that exercises showed to be fragile. Protecting this material is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice, balanced against the need for manual procedures to be at hand during an outage (Implementation 11.2.1: Document Basic Manual Fallback Procedures for SI-Dependent Control Points).

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Resilient: Where standby personnel, tools, and time are sufficient, continuing essential functions in manual mode serves resilience and safety together. Where they are not, pressing on with an under-resourced fallback risks crossing safety boundaries or failing to meet service obligations. Stopping completely ends the service and, if abrupt, can itself create secondary hazards, such as thermal or pressure excursions for SI that controls an industrial process. One way to balance this is a properly developed, documented, practiced, and controlled shutdown process for the cases in which the fallback cannot be sustained (Implementation 11.2.4: Unplanned Shutdown and Controlled Isolation Protocols); others include pre-allocating enough resources to sustain the fallback through the outages judged realistic (Task 11.3: Pre-Allocate Operational Resources for Alternative Service Delivery).

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 2.1 (on allocating resources): This Practice discusses viable non-SI alternatives for essential functions, and the personnel, tools and reserves set aside to run them; it does not cover the resources needed to investigate an SI incident. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.
  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding SI systems): This Practice covers what follows disengagement, in sustained operation without the SI system, and the checks required before it is re-engaged; it does not cover the in-the-moment override and intervention mechanisms. For that, see for example Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness.
  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Practice covers the recovery part, from restoring essential functions in priority order to returning the SI system to service; it does not cover communicating incidents to regulators and the public. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • For which outages is a practiced, controlled shutdown planned instead of a sustained fallback? (see the Safe versus Resilient tension)
  • Who owns continuity planning for each SI-dependent essential function, and what budget and staff time keep the plans, standby resources, and exercises current?
  • When the preferred fallback is unavailable, for example because standby personnel cannot reach the site, what is the next option, and who decides to use it?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-34r1, Contingency Planning Guide for Federal Information Systems, May 2010, from NIST. Section 2.2 separates continuity planning, which concerns continuing critical functions during and after an emergency, from contingency planning, which recovers information systems; Section 1.2 places the guide on the system side. Section 3.2 sets out the business impact analysis: maximum tolerable downtime, resource requirements and a recovery priority hierarchy. Section 4.4, Reconstitution, runs a recovered system concurrently until there is assurance it operates correctly and securely, a counterpart to the shadow-mode re-validation in Task 11.4.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-184, Guide for Cybersecurity Event Recovery, December 2016, from NIST. Section 2.1 holds that recovery is often not a binary activity, and asks organizations to plan how to operate in a diminished capacity or restore services over time by relative priority, mapping dependencies that include manual steps. Section 2.3.1 lists approved operational workarounds for when a system cannot be restored within its recovery time objective. Section 3.1 uses exercises and tests to confirm or refute planning assumptions. The guide is written for IT and notes it may apply to OT.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 3.3.9 asks that recovery prioritize human and environmental safety before an impaired OT operation is restarted. Section 6.2.4.5 asks that business continuity plans cover long-term outages as well as short-term ones, notes that recovery objectives may justify investment in spare inventory, suggests ordering recovery by start-up sequence, and offers bench tests or other offline testing where testing recovery procedures for OT is constrained by operational and safety requirements.
  • Guidance Mitigating Artificial Intelligence (AI) Risk: Safety and Security Guidelines for Critical Infrastructure Owners and Operators, April 2024, from the U.S. Department of Homeland Security. Map guideline f asks critical infrastructure owners and operators to maintain awareness of new failure states and alternate process redundancy when SI is incorporated into safety and incident response processes. Appendix A lists building operational resiliency, through backup systems including manual and non-AI systems, continuity of operations plans and crisis exercises, among mitigations applicable to all three of its SI risk categories.
