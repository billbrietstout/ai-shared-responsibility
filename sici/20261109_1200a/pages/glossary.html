This glossary defines terms as they are used in this profile. Particularly in fast-moving fields, there is rarely a single definition that serves every use; the NIST Computer Security Resource Center glossary often lists several definitions of the same term, drawn from different NIST publications and catering to different contexts and audiences.

This profile is written for a wide range of readers across the critical infrastructure ecosystem, including those with backgrounds in OT and automation; governance, regulation, and policy; electrical, electronic, mechanical, structural, and civil engineering; and the breadth of machine learning, SI, and computer science. Familiarity with definitions will range from formal to colloquial.

The definitions chosen in this profile aim to be as accurate and understandable as possible across this wide audience. As a result, many of these will not exactly match those used in particular sectors or in other documents, including other NIST documents, that were written for different contexts and audiences. For most terms, the glossary therefore also gives a brief terminology crosswalk, relating the definition to other wordings and to definitions used elsewhere.

Readers of this profile are welcome to adopt these definitions, or to note where their own usage differs, as appropriate for their own contexts and audiences.

Within each entry, the terminology crosswalk appears under one or more of the following headings:

  • Also called lists other wordings the reader may meet in this and related documents.
  • Collides with notes where a sector, field or framework uses the term differently and a misreading would change what someone does.
  • References lists selected NIST publications and other sources with similar or related definitions, and notes any differences.

Entries may also include:

  • Forms include, which lists specific kinds of the thing the term names.
  • Contrast, which names related terms that are easily confused with this one.
  • See, which points to where the term is used in this profile, and See also, which points to related entries.

Action-level authorization

The decision, made at the moment of execution, whether a specific proposed action by an identified entity is permitted, given its parameters, the entity’s current task scope and the operational state. It is distinct from access authorization, which decides whether an entity may interact with a system at all, and from authentication, which establishes which entity is acting. An entity that is authenticated and has access to a system is not thereby authorized for each action it can request, and an authorized action is not thereby safe to execute.

Also called: action authorization, per-action authorization, per-request authorization.

Contrast: access authorization; authentication; safety wrapper.

Collides with: “authorization” in general access control usage, where it usually means the permissions granted to a user or other principal; and an authorization to operate, a management decision about a whole system.

See: Task 5.1, Implementation 5.2.2, Task 7.4, Task 3.2, Implementation 3.2.3; zero trust.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-207, Zero Trust Architecture, from NIST. Section 2.1, Tenets of Zero Trust, includes that authentication and authorization to one resource will not automatically grant access to a different resource; this entry applies the same principle to individual actions within an authenticated session.

Agentic AI system

A system that consists of, or incorporates, one or more AI agents. A single agent is the simplest case. Systems of several agents that co-ordinate with one another, break a goal down into tasks and delegate them, and operate over extended periods are what the term most often refers to, and they add risks beyond those of each agent: errors and compromised context can pass from one agent to another, delegation chains and dependencies can form that no one documented, and one agent’s resilience can mask another’s failure.

Also called: agentic AI, agentic system; multi-agent system (for the several-agent case).

See: Practice 3, Practice 5, Task 3.3, Implementation 3.8.3, Implementation 4.4.2, Implementation 8.1.5, Implementation 13.2.2.

See also: AI agent.

References:

  • Research The Agentic AI Landscape and Its Conceptual Foundations, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 56, February 2026, from the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation. Section 3.2 describes agentic AI as generally involving multiple co-ordinated agents, task decomposition and delegation, and extended operation; this profile also counts a system with a single agent, so the OECD’s agentic AI is the main case of the term here rather than the whole of it.

AI agent

An AI system that is given a goal and some authority to act, and that pursues the goal by deciding on and carrying out a series of steps itself, rather than only producing an output for a person to act on. Communicating on the organization’s behalf counts as acting where no person reviews the output first, for example disclosing information to, or making commitments to, a customer. Those steps may include gathering information, using software tools or other systems, and changing data, configurations or physical equipment, without a person approving each one. How much it can do on its own depends on the permissions and limits it is given. Many current AI agents are built on large language models, but a planner or reinforcement-learning system that decides which steps to take toward a goal is an AI agent in the same sense. A controller that applies a fixed, pre-engineered response to its inputs is automated rather than agentic; the profile treats such logic as non-AI when it serves as a fallback or interlock (Task 2.2).

Also called: agent (where the context is clear).

Contrast: agentic AI system, which may consist of one AI agent or many.

Collides with: “agent” in endpoint-security and IT-management usage, meaning installed monitoring or management software, “threat agent”, meaning an attacker, and “conversational agent”, a common name for a chatbot, which may converse with a user without acting for the organization. Some definitions for AI agent also count any AI system whose output drives actions without human intervention as an agent, even where its sequence of steps is fixed in advance. This profile’s emphasis on OT and ICS in critical infrastructure requires careful deconfliction with the term “automation.” Therefore, this profile keeps the term AI agent for systems that decide their own steps, at the level of abstraction being considered, to reduce confusion with concepts that the OT community would consider “automation,” such as fixed, pre-engineered control logic that OT relies on as an independent fallback or interlock. Its controls on authority and access, such as those in Practice 5, apply equally to fixed-sequence systems whose actions are driven by an AI system’s output, and to conversational systems whose responses reach people outside the organization without review.

See: Practice 3, Practice 5, Task 5.3, Implementation 5.3.3, Implementation 5.3.2.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “agent”, broadly consistent with this entry; this profile also stresses the authority and permissions the agent is given.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group AI Lexicon (the Shared Artificial Intelligence (AI) Lexicon), February 2026, from the Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group, a public-private partnership formed by the U.S. Department of the Treasury with the FBIIC and FSSCC. The entries “Agentic AI” and “AI Agent” define AI systems that make decisions and take actions to achieve goals without direct human intervention, consistent with this entry; neither mentions the permissions or limits that this profile treats as setting how much an agent can do.
  • Standard ISO/IEC 22989:2022, Information technology: Artificial intelligence: Artificial intelligence concepts and terminology, from ISO and IEC. Clause 3.1.1 defines “AI agent” as an automated entity that senses and responds to its environment and takes actions to achieve its goals; like this entry, it is not limited to language-model agents, but it says nothing about delegated authority.
  • Research The Agentic AI Landscape and Its Conceptual Foundations, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 56, February 2026, from the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation, a paper that describes itself as preliminary results and research in progress. Section 3.1 surveys definitions of AI agents and finds that most share objectives, actions and a degree of autonomy, which this entry follows.

Alarm flood

More alarms, alerts or other notifications arriving than the people responsible can recognize, assess and act on, so that important signals are missed, delayed or cancelled without being understood. The profile treats AI as a new source of floods: in domains with no alarm-management experience, by breaking the assumptions behind established alarm management (for example, one fault reported in many slightly different ways), and through a correlated surge, when one external event makes many AI systems request attention at once.

Also called: alert flood, alert/alarm flood.

See also: alert fatigue (also alarm fatigue), the desensitization that repeated floods produce; oversight capacity.

Collides with: process-industry alarm management (ISA-18.2, EEMUA 191), where an alarm is specifically an indication to the operator of an abnormal condition requiring a response, and floods are judged against recommended alarm-rate targets, such as those cited in Task 4.4. The profile uses “alarm”, “alert” and “notification” interchangeably.

See: Task 4.4; Implementation 4.4.1; Implementation 4.4.2; Implementation 4.4.3; Implementation 4.4.4; Task 3.3; Implementation 3.3.1; Implementation 3.2.4.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Appendix B defines “alarm”, but not alarm flood.
  • Guidance Health and Safety Executive (2000), Better alarm handling, Chemicals Sheet No 6, from Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. States long-term average alarm-rate targets for process plant.
  • Guidance EEMUA Publication 191, Alarm Systems: A Guide to Design, Management and Procurement, from the Engineering Equipment and Materials Users’ Association.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Table 21, under “Spoofed reporting message”, names alarm floods as an adversary tactic to distract defenders and operators from the actual source of a problem; this profile treats floods mainly as an effect of AI, and the NIST passage adds the adversarial case.
  • Guidance The Joint Commission (2013) Sentinel Event Alert 50: Medical device alarm safety in hospitals, 8 April 2013, from the US non-profit that accredits healthcare organizations. Describes “alarm fatigue”: alarm signals reaching several hundred per patient per day, most needing no clinical intervention, leading clinicians to become desensitized and to silence or reset alarms; a healthcare counterpart to process-industry alarm floods.
  • Commentary Understanding and Applying the ANSI/ISA 18.2 Alarm Management Standard, 2010, from PAS, a commercial supplier of alarm management software and services. Summarizes ANSI/ISA-18.2-2009, quoting its definition of an alarm as an indication of an abnormal condition requiring a response, and its recommended alarm-rate and alarm-flood metrics, which it notes are approximate and not on their own an indicator of acceptability.

Artificial intelligence (AI)

To the Reviewer: This entry is a placeholder pending more detailed guidance regarding compatibility with EO 14434.

In this profile, AI and SI (Super Intelligence) refer to the same systems: a machine-based system that can, for a given set of human-defined objectives, make predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments, adapted from 15 U.S.C. § 9401. This deliberately includes reasoning, planning, optimization and statistical systems, deep learning neural networks, large language models and hybrid systems, and so covers automated and agentic systems, not only machine learning or chatbots.

The body text says “SI” following Executive Order 14434, while cited works, proper names, quotations, identifiers, terms of art and keyword lists keep “AI”. Where the profile calls a fallback, verifier or interlock “non-AI”, it means manual or legacy procedures, or deterministic logic whose behavior is fully specified and verified in advance, even if that logic would otherwise fall within this definition.

Also called: SI, Super Intelligence.

Collides with: everyday and executive usage, where AI means generative AI or chatbots, and OT usage, where rule-based or model-predictive control is called “advanced control”, not AI.

See: Section 1 (Introduction), Section 1.2 (Scope), footnote 1.

References:

  • Standard ISO/IEC 22989:2022, Information technology: Artificial intelligence: Artificial intelligence concepts and terminology, from ISO and IEC. Clause 3.1.4 defines “AI system” as an engineered system that generates outputs such as content, forecasts, recommendations or decisions for a given set of human-defined objectives, close to 15 U.S.C. § 9401; clause 3.1.3 reserves “artificial intelligence” for the research discipline, unlike this profile.
  • Regulation Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, 13 June 2024, from the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union. Article 3(1) defines “AI system” as a machine-based system that infers from its input how to generate outputs for explicit or implicit objectives; Recital 12 explains that the definition does not cover systems based on rules defined solely by natural persons to execute operations automatically, a narrower scope than this profile’s.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group AI Lexicon (the Shared Artificial Intelligence (AI) Lexicon), February 2026, from the Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group, a public-private partnership formed by the U.S. Department of the Treasury with the FBIIC and FSSCC. Its “Artificial intelligence (AI)” entry quotes 15 U.S.C. § 9401, the source this profile adapts; its separate “AI system” entry, from 40 U.S.C. chapter 113, excludes common commercial products with embedded AI, which this profile’s scope deliberately includes.
  • Guidance NIST AI 100-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), from NIST. The Executive Summary defines an AI system as an engineered or machine-based system that can, for a given set of objectives, generate outputs such as predictions, recommendations or decisions influencing real or virtual environments, adapted from the OECD and ISO/IEC 22989; unlike 15 U.S.C. § 9401, it omits “human-defined”.

As practical

Used in this profile, as in “as independent as practical” or “shares as little as practical”, for measures that are a matter of degree. How far to go is set by the consequence of a shared failure, weighed against the resources available, and whatever dependency or gap remains is a decision that someone with the authority to accept that level of risk accepts and records.

Also called: as independent as practical, shares as little as practical, as far as practical.

See: How Far to Go; Implementation 3.9.6.

Attack surface

The set of points where an attacker can try to enter, affect or extract data from a system, a component or its environment. For an AI system this includes the routes by which adversarial input can reach inference logic, such as prompts, retrieved content, training and fine-tuning data, tools and APIs, and the model itself. It is part of the risk surface. The profile names it separately because security practitioners know the term, and because adversarial and non-adversarial causes call for different responses. It is mapped in Implementation 3.8.1. The added monitors, fallbacks and watchdogs that this profile recommends can enlarge it.

See: Task 3.8, Implementation 3.8.1, Practice 5, Task 8.1, Implementation 8.1.6.

See also: Risk surface.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Appendix A defines attack surface as the points on a boundary where an attacker can try to enter, cause an effect on, or extract data.
  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Sections 2 and 3 catalog attacks on predictive and generative AI systems by attacker goal and capability.

Automation bias

The tendency of people to trust an automated system’s output more than it warrants: accepting it without enough checking, or delaying stepping in when it is wrong. It is related to, but distinct from, automation complacency, where a system that usually works leads people to monitor it less or lets manual skills fade.

Also called: over-trust, over-reliance.

Note: Both words are also used for automation complacency, which NIST SP 1270 defines as over-reliance, so should be read in context.

Collides with: “bias” in its fairness or measurement senses. In this profile, automation bias is a human error source handled under Bias Managed.

See: Task 3.6, Implementation 3.6.3, Task 4.6, Task 9.2, Implementation 9.2.1, Task 12.2.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 1270, Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence, from NIST. The Glossary defines the related term “automation complacency”.
  • Regulation Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, 13 June 2024, from the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union. Article 14(4)(b) names “automation bias” as the possible tendency of automatically relying or over-relying on a high-risk AI system’s output, and provides that people assigned oversight be enabled to remain aware of it; close to this entry, though it does not separate out automation complacency.
  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, from NIST. Section 2.7, Human-AI Configuration, describes automation bias as excessive deference to automated systems and notes it can exacerbate confabulation risk; NIST frames it for generative AI, while this profile applies it to any automated output.

Break-glass

A pre-established emergency procedure by which authorized personnel, including external emergency responders where provided for, can quickly override AI-driven actions, modeled on the audited emergency override of access control. It is tiered rather than all-or-nothing, because simply cutting off the AI system can leave the process unsafe.

Collides with: IAM and OT “break-glass accounts”, which grant emergency access but do not by themselves stop AI actions or hand over control.

See: Task 3.9, Practice 4, Task 4.1, Implementation 4.1.1, Implementation 4.1.2.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement AC-3(10), Audited Override of Access Control Mechanisms, is the audited emergency override this entry says break-glass is modeled on; the NIST control overrides access control, not AI-driven actions.
  • Guidance Adapting Zero Trust Principles to Operational Technology, 29 April 2026, from CISA, the Department of War and the Department of Energy with partners. The ICAM for OT section calls emergency access non-negotiable and describes break-glass accounts with limited lifespans and stringent auditing; this is the IAM sense the entry lists under “Collides with”, granting access rather than stopping AI actions.

Cold start

The disorientation of people who take over from an AI system without current awareness of system state, recent automated decisions or accumulated work.

Collides with: ML “cold start” (no prior data) and power-plant cold start or grid black start.

See: Task 4.2, Implementation 11.2.3.

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 1.3, Educate Personnel on AI, warns that relying too much on AI can reduce human oversight and situational awareness and erode the manual skills needed during AI failures; it does not address handover itself, but describes conditions that make a cold start more likely.

Common-cause failure

The failure of two or more systems, components or safeguards because of a single shared cause, such as a common power or cooling supply, network path, hypervisor, software library, training dataset or operating condition. Redundancy only protects against failures that do not share a cause, so the profile asks that functionally independent systems and safeguards be checked for what they share, rather than assumed to fail independently.

Also called: common mode failure (in the SP 800-160v2r1 material the profile cites); correlated failure modes (Implementation 3.2.5); common-cause analysis (the activity of looking for shared causes).

See: Task 3.5; Implementation 3.5.2; Task 2.2; Implementation 3.2.5; diversity (in redundancy).

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Principle E.26, Redundancy, defines common mode failure as a single event that results in the same or equivalent elements failing in the same manner, and identifies diversity as a means to address it; this profile’s term also covers shared dependencies such as power, data or hosting.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. The OT Discussion for control enhancement SR-3(1), Diverse Supply Base, uses “common cause failures” in this profile’s sense and notes that limited supply options can prevent diversity.

Compensating control

A control put in place to cover a gap where a recommended measure cannot be applied, for example where a legacy device cannot hold a unique identity, a vendor cannot supply a complete AIBOM, or an AI system cannot support the root cause analysis an application calls for. The profile asks that the gap and the control covering it be documented, and in some places that the control be shown to close the gap.

Collides with: “compensating controls” in the NIST Risk Management Framework, controls “employed in lieu of the controls in the baselines described in NIST Special Publication 800-53B that provide equivalent or comparable protection for a system or organization” (NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5). Because this profile also uses the term for partial measures proportional to the gap (Implementation 6.1.2), a measure it calls a compensating control may not qualify as one in an RMF assessment.

See: Implementation 3.2.2; Implementation 5.1.1; Implementation 5.2.3; Implementation 6.1.1; Implementation 6.1.2; Implementation 8.1.1; Task 8.2; Implementation 8.2.1; Practice 13.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “compensating controls” as controls used in place of those in a baseline that provide equivalent or comparable protection; this profile also uses the term for partial measures proportional to the gap (Implementation 6.1.2).
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-37r2, Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Their Glossary gives a closely similar definition: controls “implemented in lieu of the controls in the baselines described in NIST Special Publication 800-53” that provide equivalent or comparable protection.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Section F.4, Tailoring Considerations, describes compensating controls for OT that cannot support certain baseline controls, and says organizations should document why the baseline control could not be implemented, how the compensating controls provide equivalent security capabilities, and the risk acceptance for any residual risk; close to this entry’s documentation ask.

Confidence score

A number produced by an AI system indicating how certain it is about an output. It is not necessarily a reliable probability that the output is correct. Implementation 3.2.2 recommends that safety constraints not depend on such values, and Task 4.6 that any confidence shown to personnel be derived from how the AI system reached its output.

Also called: self-reported certainty.

Note: Where shown to personnel, the profile speaks of confidence metrics, indications or bands, which Task 4.6 asks to be derived from the decision process rather than taken from the raw score.

Collides with: the statistical confidence interval or confidence level, which is a different concept.

See: Implementation 3.1.3, Implementation 3.2.2, Implementation 3.3.3, Implementation 4.2.3, Task 4.6, Implementation 4.6.2.

Critical infrastructure

Systems and assets, physical or virtual, so vital that their incapacity or destruction would have a debilitating impact on security, the economy, public health or safety, or any combination of these. This profile is framed around the 16 critical infrastructure sectors defined in National Security Memorandum 22 (NSM-22), and its Practices apply more broadly to any high-consequence application.

Collides with: “CI”, which has several established meanings among the profile’s readers: counterintelligence, in cleared industry, insider threat and security programs; configuration item, in configuration and change management (NIST SP 800-128); continuous integration, as in CI/CD pipelines, in software development; and confidence interval, in statistics. This profile spells out critical infrastructure in body text and keeps CI in some titles; the acronym SICI is a name rather than an abbreviation to be expanded.

See also: High-consequence application.

Data poisoning

Deliberately corrupting data that an AI system learns from or draws on, so that it behaves as an attacker intends. In this profile it covers training and fine-tuning data, data that an adaptive or online-learning system keeps learning from in operation, and content a system retrieves or holds in its context or memory, such as a document planted in a retrieval knowledge base.

Also called: training data poisoning, knowledge base poisoning, memory and context poisoning, context window poisoning.

See: Implementation 3.8.1; Task 7.2; Implementation 7.2.1; Implementation 7.3.3; Implementation 8.1.5.

See also: model poisoning, where the model itself is altered, for example through a compromised vendor update (Implementation 6.1.1).

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “data poisoning” as an adversary controlling part of the training data, and Section 3.4.2 treats knowledge base poisoning separately as an attack on RAG systems; this profile uses the one term for all of these.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, Version 2026, December 2025, from the Agentic Security Initiative of the OWASP Gen AI Security Project, a volunteer community project. ASI06, Memory & Context Poisoning, describes adversaries corrupting or seeding the context an agent retains, retrieves or reuses, such as summaries, embeddings and RAG stores, so that its later reasoning, planning or tool use becomes biased or unsafe; this matches the entry’s “memory and context poisoning”.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group AI Lexicon (the Shared Artificial Intelligence (AI) Lexicon), February 2026, from the Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group, a public-private partnership formed by the U.S. Department of the Treasury with the FBIIC and FSSCC. The entry “Data poisoning” limits the term to corrupting training data, as does NIST AI 100-2; financial services readers using the lexicon may read it more narrowly than this profile, which also covers retrieved content and memory.

Design basis

The conditions an item is deliberately designed to withstand, chosen with regard to their likelihood, their consequences, and the resources available, such as the severity of flood, the size of a surge, or the capability of an adversary. Conditions beyond the design basis are possible but judged too unlikely to design for; they are still examined so that the organization understands what would happen, and plans for it where it can.

Collides with: the regulatory sense in nuclear safety, where design bases are defined in 10 CFR 50.2 as “the specific functions to be performed by a structure, system, or component of a facility, and the specific values or ranges of values chosen for controlling parameters as reference bounds for design”, and where the design-basis threat has a specific regulatory meaning (10 CFR 73.1). This profile uses the term in its general engineering sense and does not invoke those requirements.

See: How Far to Go; Appendix A, A.3.1; Implementation 4.4.3; Task 11.2.

References:

  • Regulation 10 CFR 50.2, Definitions, from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Defines design bases as the specific functions a structure, system, or component must perform and the values or ranges chosen as reference bounds for design.
  • Regulation 10 CFR 73.1, Purpose and scope, from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Sets out the design basis threats against which safeguards systems are designed.
  • Guidance NRC glossary, “Beyond design-basis accidents”, from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Describes accident sequences that are possible but were not fully considered in the design process because they were judged too unlikely, and that are analyzed to understand the capability of a design.
  • Guidance NRC glossary, “Design-basis accident” and “Design-basis threat (DBT)”, from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Appendix D.3, Security Design Order of Precedence, speaks of the system design basis for loss potential, reduced to an acceptable level within the constraints of cost, schedule, and performance.
  • Guidance USGS, “The 100-Year Flood”, from the U.S. Geological Survey. Explains return periods, such as a flood with a 1 percent chance of being equaled or exceeded in any year.
  • Guidance UK National Risk Register 2023, from the UK Government. Plans against a reasonable worst-case scenario for each risk.

Digital twin

A detailed virtual replica of physical equipment or a process, kept up to date with data from the real system. The profile uses digital twins mainly as places to test AI systems, rehearse incidents and train staff where doing so on live equipment would be unsafe, and as simulated environments in which to train AI models across their full operating range. It notes that a twin cannot reproduce every real-world condition, and that twin predictions should be compared with live telemetry to detect divergence. A digital twin that uses learned or symbolic models for forecasting or “what-if” planning is also an AI application in its own right, and is in scope.

Also called: high-fidelity digital twin, twin.

See: Section 1.2 (Scope); Implementation 1.3.1; Implementation 1.3.2; Implementation 1.4.1; Implementation 2.3.3; Implementation 2.5.1; Implementation 8.1.4; Implementation 9.2.2; Task 11.5; Implementation 11.5.2; Implementation 12.3.1.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Appendix E.2.8, Digital Twins, describes their use in OT as an anomaly detection tool that compares real-time sensor inputs against a baseline model.

Diversity (in redundancy)

Building redundant paths from implementations that differ from one another, for example in model architecture, training data, supplier, hosting or technology, so that a single fault, attack or other common cause is less likely to disable every path at once. Diversity can be more apparent than real: secondary models that share a foundation model, training data or hosting provider may fail together. Diversity among AI models alone, which Implementation 2.2.1 calls heterogeneous AI redundancy, does less against a cause in the operating environment, such as distribution drift that reaches every model built on similar data; a backup that differs in technology, or a physical interlock, holds under the same drift, provided its behavior does not rest on the same training data or on other potentially correlated sources of error. Some shared design assumptions are usually unavoidable; those that remain warrant particularly careful verification, since a flaw in one would reach every path. Such a backup may itself use AI techniques, such as rule-based reasoning or planning.

Also called: heterogeneity, heterogeneous redundancy, heterogeneous AI redundancy; design diversity and supply chain diversity are forms of it.

See: Task 2.2; Implementation 2.2.1; Implementation 3.2.5; redundancy gradient; common-cause failure.

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Table D-2 defines the Diversity technique, and Table D-4 its Design Diversity and Supply Chain Diversity approaches; the NIST technique emphasizes threats that exploit common vulnerabilities, while this profile applies it equally to non-adversarial failures. Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Controls SC-29, Heterogeneity, and SA-17(9), Design Diversity.

Drift

In this profile, the difference between the data or conditions an SI system was trained or designed for and those it meets in use, arising from changes in, for example, its inputs, the operating environment, the equipment, the population served or the task. The term names the difference itself, not a trend: drift can be gradual or sudden, and it does not necessarily grow, since it can grow and then shrink again, as with seasonal factors. Drift can degrade performance while the SI system itself is unchanged, so change management alone does not reveal it; it is detected by comparing operation against baselines.

Also called: distribution drift, distribution shift, data drift, operational drift. In this profile, “model drift” and “behavioral drift” usually refer to the resulting change in a model’s performance or behavior.

Contrast: calibration drift, in which an instrument’s reading moves away from the true value (Implementation 2.3.3, Implementation 7.2.4). It is a property of the sensor rather than of the SI system, but it can be one cause of drift in the SI system’s inputs. Also configuration drift, in which a system’s running configuration departs from its approved baseline (Implementation 12.3.3). Unlike drift in this entry’s sense, it is a change to the system itself, and it is found by comparing the configuration against that baseline.

Collides with: “concept drift”, which has two incompatible senses. NIST SP 1270 uses it for “Use of a system outside the planned domain of application”, with no element of change over time, which is closer to this entry or to an out-of-distribution input. In machine learning, concept drift is a change in the relationship between a system’s inputs and the correct output, so that “the decision boundary moves” even though the input data have not changed (ISO/IEC 22989:2022). Gama and colleagues describe this second sense as “an online supervised learning scenario when the relation between the input data and the target variable changes over time”, which can happen with or without a change in the input distribution, and suddenly, gradually or in recurring patterns. Monitoring of input statistics alone may not detect concept drift in that second sense.

See: Implementation 2.3.4, Implementation 3.3.2, Task 3.4, Task 6.2, Task 10.3.

See also: Out-of-distribution input; Diversity (in redundancy); Training cutoff.

Clause 5.11.9.1, Retraining, distinguishes data drift, in which “the accuracy of the model’s predictions decays over time due to changes in the statistical characteristics of the production data”, from concept drift, in which “the decision boundary moves”; this entry’s drift corresponds to the first, without assuming that the difference only grows.

A change in the task or population served, which this entry includes, can produce either kind.

Embedded AI

An AI capability built into a larger software product, service or device, where the people operating it may not realize it is there or what kind of AI it is. AI features may also be added to existing software during routine updates.

Collides with: embedded systems engineering, where “embedded” means computing built into a device or cyber-physical system, such as a controller. This profile uses the word in both senses, so readers may miss AI features inside SaaS and enterprise software.

Fail loudly

To make a failure or degradation immediately and explicitly known to people, even if the system keeps running, perhaps in a degraded capacity. Failing loudly concerns notification and is separate from moving to a fail-safe state, although an unacknowledged loud failure may be set to force that move. How loudly to fail is weighed against the limited attention of the people receiving the alarms.

Contrast: quiet failure, where autonomous recovery or the resilience of other agents masks a degradation (Task 3.3).

Collides with: “failover” in IT continuity usage, the capability to “switch over automatically (typically without human intervention or warning) to a redundant or standby system” (NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5). A silent switch to a backup or fallback controller, such as one forced by a watchdog (Implementation 2.2.2), is a form of the quiet failure described under Contrast.

See: Task 3.3, Implementation 3.3.1, Task 4.4.

Fail-safe state

A predefined state (or states), chosen for the specific application, that the controlled process moves to when an AI output violates its constraints, an AI action is refused, the AI system becomes unavailable or degraded, or a person overrides or disengages it. It may hold last-known-safe setpoints, revert to conservative baselines or hand control to an independent deterministic controller. It does not necessarily mean shutting down, which can itself be the hazard, and where an action is refused part-way through a sequence it accounts for the steps already taken.

Despite the name, a fail-safe state is rarely free of risk. It is chosen because it is expected to be safer than continuing in the condition that triggered it. The fail-safe state carries hazards of its own, such as lost production or service, reduced capability, or more demand on the people who take over. Those hazards are best identified and planned for when the state is chosen.

Also called: safe state, known-safe state, fallback state, safe fallback state, pre-defined safe state.

Note: The same caveat applies to each. This profile uses “fallback behavior” for the wider set of responses, including for requests that do not act on the physical process.

Contrast: fallback behavior, the wider set of responses to a failure or refusal, which may also keep the SI-enabled function running in a degraded mode rather than moving the process to a defined state (Implementation 3.2.3).

Collides with: functional safety, where fail-safe can mean the de-energized state; the profile uses that sense where it refers to E-stops (Implementation 4.1.2) and to a process that can be stopped safely (Practice 4). Also security usage, where IETF RFC 4949 gives “fail-safe” two senses that it calls “opposing design alternatives”: a synonym for fail-secure, which preserves a secure state though some resource may be damaged, and “A mode of termination of system functions that prevents damage to specified system resources and system entities” when a failure occurs, though the failure might still compromise security. CNSSI 4009 (2015) adopts only the second. Both senses describe ways of terminating system functions; a fail-safe state in this profile may keep the process running.

See: Implementation 3.2.3, Task 3.2, Implementation 5.2.2, Implementation 2.2.2, Task 3.3.

Fine-tuning

Further training an existing AI model, such as a foundation model, on an organization’s own or task-specific data to adapt it to a particular task or domain. Fine-tuning changes the model itself, unlike retrieval-augmented generation, which changes what the model is given with each request. The profile asks that the source and authorization of fine-tuning data be documented, and that fine-tuned versions, such as adapters, be tracked in the AI asset list with their lineage back to the base model.

Also called: local fine-tuning; continuous local fine-tuning (when done during operation).

See: Implementation 7.1.1; Implementation 7.1.2; Task 7.2; Implementation 7.3.3.

Foundation model

A large AI model trained on broad data that can be adapted to many different tasks, for example by fine-tuning. Large language models are one kind. Many AI products are built on the same few foundation models, so one model’s flaws or retirement can affect many systems at once.

General-purpose AI assistant

An AI tool, such as a chatbot, built for a wide range of tasks and not designed or vetted for a specific critical infrastructure use. Generative AI

An AI system, or a capability of one, that produces new content, such as text, code, images, audio, configurations or plans, that is related to the data it learned from, in response to a prompt or other instruction. In this profile the term describes what a system does, not how it is built. A system described colloquially as an LLM (see Large language model) usually includes generative output, and it may combine generative components with symbolic, rule-based and hand-written ones, such as guardrails. The profile’s concerns about generated content apply to the generated output, whichever component produced it: it can be fluent and plausible yet wrong (see Hallucination), and it should be checked before it is relied on or acted upon.

Contrast: Predictive or discriminative AI, which classifies inputs or forecasts values rather than producing new content. Many critical infrastructure systems combine the two, for example a predictive anomaly detector with a generative assistant that summarizes its alerts.

Collides with: Everyday usage in which “AI” means generative AI, especially chatbots. In this profile, AI (and SI) covers predictive, generative and control systems alike (see AI).

See: Section 1.2 (Scope); Appendix B (B.5).

Guardrail

A loose term for a control that constrains what an SI system can do. Guardrails can sit within the SI component, such as instructions it is given or behaviors trained into it, or around it, such as filters on its inputs and outputs or limits on the rate at which it acts, and they may or may not be independent of it or themselves AI-based. This profile uses the term across that range: for the process-informed limits of Implementation 2.1.2, for the guardrails built into SI products or set by their vendors (Implementation 5.3.3, Implementation 6.1.2, Implementation 6.2.2), and for the independent, deterministic checks of Task 3.2, which it calls safety wrappers. Where independence matters, the profile says “safety wrapper” or “independent guardrail”.

Collides with: “guardrails” in AI security and vendor usage, which are often assumed to provide protection whatever their independence; a guardrail that is not independent of the SI system does not by itself meet Task 3.2.

Hallucination

Output from an AI system that sounds plausible and confident but is false or not supported by its inputs or sources. The profile is most concerned with the “plausible hallucination”, which reads as correct to a non-expert, for example a maintenance step that looks valid but would damage equipment. NIST AI 600-1 calls this risk confabulation.

Also called: confabulation, plausible hallucination.

Also called: industrial automation and control system (IACS).

Collides with: in emergency management and public safety, ICS more commonly means the Incident Command System, the standardized incident management structure within the National Incident Management System (NIMS). This profile does not use ICS in that sense.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Appendix B defines “industrial control system” and “operational technology”.

Inference receipt

A record created each time an AI system providing decision support or diagnostics from structured data produces an output, capturing the data elements it used, the thresholds in effect and the versioned configuration. Where the system applies encoded rules or is self-interpretable, the receipt can also record the rules or branches applied. It lets the output be traced and checked later.

Contrast: audit trail, “A chronological record that reconstructs and examines the sequence of activities surrounding or leading to a specific operation, procedure, or event in a security-relevant transaction from inception to result” (NIST SP 800-53 Rev. 5). An audit trail records what was done and in what order; an inference receipt records what a single output was based on, so existing audit logging does not necessarily provide it.

See: Implementation 4.6.3, Task 10.1, Implementation 10.1.2.

References:

  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Appendix E’s logging schema lists what logs should capture for each significant agent action, including the decision and reason, scopes used, policy version and attestation information; it logs authorization decisions rather than the data and thresholds behind an output.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 4.1, in its example of monitoring a predictive maintenance system, recommends an audit trail logging all AI system operating data, including timestamps, inputs and outputs, for analysis of AI behavior; an inference receipt adds the thresholds, versioned configuration and rules applied, so the output can be checked as well as traced.

Intercept point

A place in the path between an AI system’s output and its effect where that output can be monitored, checked, blocked or overridden by something other than the AI system. One path may have several: in the profile’s example of an AI system recommending setpoints, they include the setpoint interface, the local controller and existing emergency shutoff mechanisms. Intercept points are where safety wrappers and override procedures can act. A wrapper may check or block outputs at some of them, and at others act through an existing mechanism, for example by triggering an emergency shutoff where that is practical and permitted by policy and regulation. It should not gate commands from protective functions such as emergency stops (Implementation 3.2.2), except where a simple stop would itself be unsafe, as in a dynamically unstable process; there, a stop request may go to logic that carries out a safe shutdown sequence.

Also called: AI output intercept point, operational intercept point.

See: Implementation 3.2.1; Task 3.2; Implementation 4.1.1; Appendix C.1.

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 4.1, under Human-in-the-Loop Decision-Making, recommends that where AI is actively updating control logic, safety thresholds, alternative sensor output or state changes be used to add human-in-the-loop intervention points; intercept points in this profile also include automated checks, not only human ones.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Principle E.19, Mediated Access, states that all access to and operations on system elements are mediated, and separates the access mediation decision from its enforcement; an intercept point is a place where such mediation can be applied to an AI system’s output before it takes effect.

Interlock

A mechanism that blocks an action, or forces a protective action, unless defined conditions are met. In this profile it mostly means a physical or hardware safety interlock at the process boundary that holds regardless of what an AI system commands, at the high-consequence end of the redundancy gradient. The profile also uses it for logic conditions on a trigger, such as the “state-aware interlocks” that stop a watchdog from forcing a failover during a sensitive process transition, and for automated “policy interlocks” in non-physical processes.

Also called: safety interlock, physical interlock, hardware interlock.

See also: safety instrumented system (SIS), the engineered system that takes a process to a safe state, which an AI system should not be able to affect (Implementation 3.5.3).

See: Task 2.2; Implementation 2.2.1; Implementation 2.2.2; Implementation 2.3.4; Implementation 3.1.1; Implementation 3.2.1; Implementation 3.2.2; Task 4.5; Implementation 11.2.3.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, from NIST. Appendix B defines “safety instrumented system”, giving “safety interlock system” as another name; it does not define “interlock” on its own. Section 2.3.7, Safety Systems, describes an SIS as often independent of other control systems, so that a failure of the basic process control system will not impair it, and notes integrated control and safety systems that do communicate with the control system; relevant to the entry’s point that interlocks hold regardless of what an AI system commands.

Known-safe

Describes a state, setting, mode of operation or controller whose behavior has been established in advance, through analysis, testing or operating history, to be acceptably safe under the conditions it was assessed for. “Known” refers to that prior assessment, not to a guarantee: a known-safe state is safe only as far as the assessment holds, and may not be if conditions have moved outside what was assessed. Last-known-safe setpoints are the most recent values assessed or observed to be safe. They are a sensible default when an AI system’s output is blocked or lost, but they reflect conditions at the time they were recorded, which may since have changed.

Contrast: known-good, which this profile uses for a configuration, procedure or artifact version that has been verified to work and is kept for rollback (Task 12.3, Implementation 12.3.2). A known-good configuration restores function; a known-safe state limits harm. One need not be the other.

See also: fail-safe state.

See: Practice 2, Task 3.2, Implementation 3.2.3, Implementation 4.1.2.

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control SC-24, Fail in Known State, has organizations define the known state a system fails to and the state information it preserves.

Appendix B, Glossary, defines “safety” as “Expectation that a system does not, under defined conditions, lead to a state in which human life, health, property, or the environment is endangered”; like this entry, it ties safety to defined conditions.

Appendix B, Glossary, defines “Fail to Known State” as failing to a predetermined state, and says that “Failure in a known safe state helps to prevent systems from failing to a state that may cause injury to individuals or destruction to property.” As in this entry, the state is established in advance.

Large language model (LLM)

In this profile, an LLM is the whole system that the general public colloquially refers to (e.g., “Which LLM are you using to summarize your emails?”), not only the model at its core. Such a system typically combines one or more large statistical language models with other components: additional models, symbolic and rule-based components, retrieval of external data, tool and agent interfaces, hand-written instructions and code, and guardrails that filter inputs and outputs. Its behavior comes from all of these together, and these components may be changed by the provider without notice. An LLM-based assistant, chatbot, or coding agent is such a system, with associated additional tools and functionality, used to help a person with the associated tasks.

In computer science, “large language model” refers more narrowly to the model alone. In current practice, this is usually a large statistical model of language, implemented as a deep learning neural network, though not every definition requires it. The model is not limited to generating text, and a system built around it need not be generative. Where this profile means the model alone, it says so.

Also called: LLM-based assistant, LLM-based system.

Collides with: the computer-science sense described above, in which “the LLM” is only the trained model. Statements that hold for the model alone, about its training data, size or architecture, do not necessarily hold for the system, and controls that act on the model do not by themselves govern the rest of the system.

See also: foundation model, general-purpose AI assistant, safety wrapper.

See: Section 1.2 (Scope), Section 1.4, Implementation 1.4.2, Implementation 3.7.1, Appendix E.

References:

  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group AI Lexicon (the Shared Artificial Intelligence (AI) Lexicon), February 2026, from the Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group, a public-private partnership formed by the U.S. Department of the Treasury with the FBIIC and FSSCC. The entry “Large language model” describes machine learning trained on large amounts of data through self-supervised learning to recognize patterns and respond to user requests in natural language, and the “Foundation models” entry calls LLMs a common subset of foundation models. This is the model-centered sense; this profile uses the term for the wider system.

Least agency

The principle that an AI system’s ability to initiate actions or invoke tools should be limited to what its defined function strictly requires, and that it should not use access unnecessarily even where access exists. It extends to what an AI system carries forward between steps, such as context and memory, as well as what it can initiate. It differs from least privilege, which limits the resources and authorizations the system is granted; least agency also limits how much the system decides and does on its own within them.

Contrast: excessive agency.

See: Task 5.3, Implementation 5.3.1, Implementation 5.3.3, Implementation 5.3.4, Practice 5.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Appendix A defines the related principle “least privilege”, which limits the access and authorizations granted; least agency limits what the system initiates.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, Version 2026, December 2025, from the Agentic Security Initiative of the OWASP Gen AI Security Project, a volunteer community project. The opening letter describes “Least-Agency” as extending least privilege and excessive agency, advising organizations to avoid unnecessary autonomy, and ASI02 mitigation 1 pairs least agency with least privilege for tools; consistent with this entry.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Principle E.14, Least Functionality, states that each system element has the capability to accomplish its required functions but no more; least agency applies this to what an AI system can initiate.

Operating envelope

The defined range of conditions, such as input and output ranges, setpoint limits, latency limits, physical tolerances and dependencies, within which an AI system is specified and validated to operate.

Also called: operating window, boundary envelope.

Note: Practice 3 and Task 3.2 also speak of the process’s “safe operating envelope”: the limits the physical process must stay within, which guardrails enforce regardless of the AI system. That is the process-safety sense below, and where both are meant the document specifies so.

Collides with: aviation flight envelope; process-safety safe operating limits.

See: Implementation 1.1.2, Implementation 1.3.1, Implementation 2.5.1; for the process sense, Practice 3 and Task 3.2; Implementation 3.2.2 combines both senses as “safe operating limits.”

Operational enforceability

The demonstrated ability to turn a governance decision about an SI-enabled capability, such as restricting, suspending, rolling back or reducing its delegated authority, into an actual change in what the system does. Implementation 3.9.5 tests whether that change is effective, timely relative to consequence, verifiable without relying solely on the capability’s own report, durable and sufficient in scope.

See: Task 3.9, Implementation 3.9.4, Implementation 3.9.5.

Organizational readiness

A determination, made for one SI-enabled capability in its operating context, that the accountability, authority, escalation paths, operational capability and enforceability needed to govern it are actually in place and can be exercised, so that a governance decision, once made, takes effect in the running system. It is made before deployment and again after any material change, and it is not a general organizational maturity assessment.

Collides with: “workforce readiness” (Task 9.3), which is the training competence of personnel.

See: Task 3.9, Implementation 3.9.2, Implementation 3.9.5.

See also: operational enforceability.

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), from NIST. Out-of-band

Operating through a separate, independent path from the system being monitored or controlled, so that a failure or compromise of that system does not reach it.

Collides with: IT out-of-band management and authentication, which is the same idea in a narrower sense.

See: Task 4.3, Implementation 2.2.2, Implementation 3.2.2, Implementation 4.3.2, Task 7.3.

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Out-of-distribution input

An input or operating condition unlike those an AI system was trained or designed for, where its behavior is less predictable.

Also called: novel input, unhistorical conditions.

Note: Related to drift (see Drift), the overall difference between the data a system was trained or designed for and the data it meets in use; out-of-distribution describes the individual input or condition.

Collides with: “concept drift” in NIST SP 1270, defined as “Use of a system outside the planned domain of application”, which is close to this entry and has no element of change over time. In machine learning the same term means something different (see Drift).

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “out-of-distribution” data in terms of how it was collected relative to the training data; this profile also covers conditions a non-learned system was not designed for.
  • Standard ISO/IEC 22989:2022, Information technology: Artificial intelligence: Artificial intelligence concepts and terminology, from ISO and IEC. Clause 5.15.2, AI robustness, describes robustness as comparable performance on atypical data or on inputs dissimilar to those the system was trained on; it names the property that out-of-distribution inputs test, without defining the inputs themselves.

Post-hoc explanation

An explanation of an AI output generated afterward, typically by a separate tool that probes how outputs change with inputs (for example SHAP or LIME). Its output is a hypothesis about why the model may have behaved as it did, not a record of what it did.

Also called: post-hoc attribution, post-hoc statistical explanation.

Contrast: introspective explanation, drawn from the process that produced the output (Task 4.6). A surrogate model distilled from a learned model is a post-hoc explanation when it is used to explain that model, and an introspective one only when it is deployed in its place (Implementation 4.6.4).

See: Task 1.4, Implementation 4.6.2, Implementation 8.1.2, Implementation 10.1.2, Task 12.1, Implementation 12.1.2.

References:

  • Guidance NIST IR 8312, Four Principles of Explainable Artificial Intelligence, from NIST. Section 6 defines post-hoc explanations as explanations, often generated by other software tools, that describe, explain or model an algorithm to give an idea of how it works, contrasting them with self-interpretable models; Section 6.2 divides them into local and global explanations.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8367, Psychological Foundations of Explainability and Interpretability in Artificial Intelligence, from NIST. Section 3.2.1 says LIME replaces a causal description of a model with a simpler model whose results are only correlated with it, that SHAP shares its strengths and weaknesses, and that both are prone to unknown error outside the local neighborhood of a prediction; this supports the entry’s point that the output is a hypothesis.

Prompt compromise

Loss of integrity or confidentiality of the instructions an AI system works from, such as its system prompt, standing instructions or the context it is given. Integrity is lost when the instructions are altered, whether by prompt injection, a changed system prompt, an insider or a compromised update. Confidentiality is lost when instructions that are legitimate but sensitive are leaked. System prompts can contain internal procedures, the conditions under which the system refuses, or credentials, and a leak can help an attacker get around controls.

Forms include: prompt injection; system prompt leakage, also called prompt extraction when it is done deliberately.

See: Implementation 5.1.1, Implementation 5.3.3, Task 7.3.

See also: Prompt injection.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, March 2025, from NIST. Section 3.1.1 describes system prompts and notes that, because they may be security-relevant, a prompt extraction attack may try to steal them; the Glossary defines prompt extraction.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025, from the OWASP GenAI Security Project. LLM07:2025, System Prompt Leakage, describes system prompts that contain sensitive information not intended to be discovered, and advises against relying on the system prompt to keep secrets or enforce controls.

Prompt injection

An attack in which instructions placed in an AI system’s input, either typed directly or hidden in content the system reads (indirect prompt injection), cause it to ignore its intended instructions or take unintended actions.

See also: Prompt compromise, the broader term, which also covers prompts altered by other routes and sensitive prompts that are leaked.

See: Implementation 3.8.1, Implementation 3.2.2, Implementation 5.3.3.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “prompt injection”, with direct and indirect forms, consistent with this entry.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, Version 2026, August 4, 2026, from the OWASP GenAI Security Project, a volunteer community project. LLM01:2026, Prompt Injection, lists direct and indirect forms, including injection through retrieved content and low-privilege but trusted channels, and states that no reliable prevention mechanism exists today, so defense is architectural; consistent with this profile’s use of controls outside the model.

Protective AI capability

An SI system that performs a detection, monitoring, screening or filtering function, and so provides a protective function for other systems, acting as a control for them whether or not it is formally recorded as one. Retiring it removes that control, so the profile treats its retirement as a change to controls as well as an asset disposal. That includes establishing what it had learned to detect that is not documented elsewhere, and what will perform the function afterward.

Contrast: protective functions in general, which need not be implemented with AI. Those named in Implementation 3.2.2, such as protective relay trips, safety instrumented system shutdowns and emergency stops, are usually conventional, and a safety wrapper does not gate their commands. Where SI logic takes the place of a simple stop as a protective function, Implementation 3.2.2 expects it to be far less complex than the SI system it backs up, independent of it, and verified to the standard of a protective function.

See: Implementation 13.3.2; Task 13.3.

Redundancy gradient

The profile’s scheme for matching how independent a backup is to how severe the consequences of failure would be. For lower- and moderate-consequence functions where a non-AI fallback is not technically feasible, the backup may be a secondary AI model with a different architecture, training data and hosting (see diversity (in redundancy)). For high-consequence or cyber-physical functions, it escalates to include independent deterministic algorithms or out-of-band, non-AI physical safety interlocks, which may be paired with an AI backup. The degree of independence is set by the severity of consequences rather than from what is convenient to build.

Collides with: “deterministic” in its computing sense, a deterministic algorithm being “An algorithm that, given the same inputs, always produces the same outputs” (NIST SP 800-90A Rev. 1). Being deterministic does not make a component non-AI: classical planning and logical inference are often deterministic, and a trained model may or may not be, depending on how inference is run. What the redundancy gradient relies on is a fallback that shares as little as practical with the SI system, particularly its training data, and fully specified and verified in advance, including any design assumptions it shares with the SI system.

See: Implementation 2.2.1; Task 2.2; Implementation 3.1.3.

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Principle E.6, Commensurate Trustworthiness, makes the trustworthiness required of an element commensurate with the most significant adverse effect of its failure, and E.26, Redundancy, notes that redundant solutions are susceptible to common mode failure, with diversity as a means to address it; the gradient applies this scaling to how independent a backup must be.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Structural design principle D.5.2.6, Maintain Redundancy, says Redundancy for cyber resiliency should be applied in conjunction with Diversity and considered at multiple layers, and warns that trends to convergence can undermine it; the gradient adds a rule tying the degree of independence to consequence.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)

An AI system design in which, for each request, the model is given information retrieved from a separate store, such as a document collection, database or vector store, and uses it to produce its output. The system’s behavior therefore depends on what is in that store as well as on the model, and changing the store changes the behavior without any change to the model.

Also called: retrieval augmentation, retrieval-augmented system; the store is called a knowledge base or retrieval-augmented data repository.

Collides with: “knowledge base” in symbolic AI (Implementation 4.6.3), where it means the encoded rules a system reasons with, not documents it retrieves.

See: Practice 6; Implementation 6.1.2; Implementation 7.1.2; Task 7.2; Implementation 7.4.1.

References:

  • Guidance NIST AI 100-2e2025, Adversarial Machine Learning: A Taxonomy and Terminology of Attacks and Mitigations, from NIST. Appendix A defines “retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)” in the same sense.

Risk surface

The full set of ways in which an AI system can cause physical, operational or administrative harm, across all the trustworthiness characteristics, whether an attacker is involved or not. It includes the attack surface. The profile often names both, as in “attack and risk surface”, for emphasis and for readers who know only one of the terms. It can grow or shrink over time as the environment, the data or the degree of autonomy changes, which Implementation 3.8.3 tracks. Implementation 3.8.2 maps its non-adversarial part, and Implementation 8.1.6 triages incidents between the adversarial and non-adversarial parts.

Collides with: Usage that treats the two as separate, with “risk surface” meaning only harm that involves no attacker.

Root cause analysis

In this profile, establishing why a particular incident, failure or SI output occurred, so that its cause can be corrected and the correction shown. For an SI system this usually rests on reconstructing what the system received and produced, including its inputs, versions and settings; a post-hoc explanation offers a hypothesis about the cause, not a record of it (Implementation 8.1.2). Task 8.1 identifies where root cause analysis must rest on deterministic reconstruction, and Task 8.2 the additional controls that may be needed where that is not possible.

Collides with: “root cause analysis” in risk management, a “systems approach for the identification of underlying causes associated with a particular set of risks” (NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1), which looks for the causes of a set of risks rather than reconstructing a specific incident.

Safety wrapper

A hard-coded, deterministic control, as independent of the AI system as practical and not itself AI-based, that checks AI outputs or commands against defined constraints and limits or blocks them, engages fallback controllers, notifies operators and logs the event when a constraint would be violated. Where it guards a physical process, it applies whichever upstream source issued the command, such as an SI recommendation, an automated agent, a remote operator session or another upstream system, except for commands from protective functions, which the wrapper does not gate, and authorized overrides; for SI systems that generate information rather than commands, the constraint may instead be one of completeness or fidelity (Implementation 3.2.2). In either case the constraints do not depend on the AI system’s own confidence values. It can only bound the hazards that were identified and written as constraints.

Also called: independent guardrail, boundary logic, safety/policy wrapper. “Validation control” is used in Implementation 3.4.3 for safety wrappers and watchdog controllers together.

Forms include: command validation proxy, guarded proxy (Implementation 5.3.3).

Contrast: “enforcement layer”, which this profile uses for whatever enforces a check at the point of action: a safety wrapper, the action-level authorization of Implementation 5.2.2, or a guarded proxy (Implementation 5.3.3), as in Task 10.1, Implementation 10.1.2 and Appendix C.1. Records of what was permitted should come from it rather than from the SI system (Implementation 10.1.2).

Collides with: “guardrails” in AI security writing, which can be checks at any layer of the AI system, including ones that judge prompts or generated content; these are not necessarily independent of the AI system. The profile itself also uses “guardrail” for the process-informed rate limits of Implementation 2.1.2, which may be deterministic and may be internal to the AI component rather than independent of it.

See: Task 3.2, Implementation 3.2.2, Implementation 3.2.3, Implementation 3.4.3, Implementation 4.5.2, Implementation 5.3.3, Implementation 6.1.1, Task 7.3, Task 7.5.

Appendix A defines “shadow model” as a model that imitates the behavior of the target model, used to train attack models in privacy attacks; this is the contrasting sense the entry’s Collides with note mentions, not a testing technique.

Shadow SI

SI tools, models or services used within an organization’s operations without having gone through its procurement, approval or inventory processes. This includes personal LLM-based assistants and other tools brought in by staff or contractors, and models downloaded and run locally without approval. Because shadow SI sits outside credential directories and asset lists, it may bypass identity and other risk management controls; identity and access controls can detect and deny it when it tries to authenticate (Practice 5).

Also called: shadow AI

Collides with: shadow mode, a testing technique in this profile.

See: Task 3.7, Practice 5, Practice 7.

References:

  • Guidance AI Shared Responsibility Framework, version 1.0, Workstream 2: Preparing Defenders for a Changing Cybersecurity Landscape, 26 May 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 2.1 names shadow AI proliferation, unsanctioned AI usage that no single group monitors, as a pain point, and Appendix A.1.2.3, AI Tool Usage Governance, lists shadow AI detection and management, including discovery of ungoverned AI tool usage; consistent with this entry.
  • Research NIST AI 800-4, Challenges to the Monitoring of Deployed AI Systems, from NIST. Section 3.1.2 reports staff using AI services on personal accounts despite having authorized tools, as a monitoring visibility problem; it describes the practice without naming it shadow AI.

SI Bill of Materials (SIBOM)

A record, ideally machine-readable and, for acquired systems, supplied by the vendor for each model release, of the models an AI system uses, its main training data sources, the data it retrieves at runtime and the software it depends on at training and run time; the profile also uses it to carry vendor attestations, such as representation of similar environments in the training data. It extends the idea of a software bill of materials (SBOM), but there is no vulnerability database for model behavior equivalent to the one an SBOM is checked against. Formal specifications are still being developed.

Also called: AI Bill of Materials, AIBOM, SBOM for AI, AI-SBOM; AI BOM (SPDX), ML-BOM (CycloneDX).

See: Implementation 6.1.2, Implementation 6.1.5, Implementation 7.1.3.

References:

  • Guidance Mitigating Artificial Intelligence (AI) Risk: Safety and Security Guidelines for Critical Infrastructure Owners and Operators, April 2024, from the U.S. Department of Homeland Security. Map guideline e lists AI system bills of materials (AIBOMs), with SBOMs and data and model cards, among vendor documents for supply chain review, and footnote 26 describes an AIBOM as containing SBOM-like information for AI systems; it notes that the software security and AI communities are working toward harmonized AIBOM guidance, consistent with this entry’s note that formal specifications are still being developed.
  • Guidance G7 Cybersecurity Working Group, A Shared G7 Vision on Software Bill of Materials for AI, 2025, a food-for-thought paper. Section 3 sets out properties of an “SBOM for AI”, including machine-readable format and capture of datasets used across the lifecycle, and example minimum elements covering models, learning, datasets, safety and security characteristics, licensing and infrastructure; its element list is broader than this entry’s.
  • Guidance 2026 Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM), 29 July 2026, from CISA and co-authoring organizations. The section SBOM for AI Software Systems says AI systems may have components the minimum elements do not capture, such as model and data cards, notes ongoing discussions about SBOM for AI data fields, and does not itself add elements for AI; its footnote points to the G7 joint guidance Software Bill of Materials for AI – Minimum Elements.

Silent update

A change to an AI model or its behavior that a vendor applies without the operator being told or having reviewed it, usually also without the operator taking any action, for example a cloud-side model change. An internal change that reaches production unreviewed is the internally sourced equivalent.

Also called: unannounced update.

Contrast: cloud-side model change, which describes where a change is made rather than whether the operator is told; both a cloud-side change and an on-premises one can be announced or silent (Implementation 6.1.1).

Collides with: “update” in software security usage, defined as “A patch, upgrade, or other modification to code that corrects security and/or functionality problems in software” (NIST SP 800-213 and NIST IR 8259A). A change-management process built on that sense may not treat a change to a model or to the instructions it is given as an update, although such a change can alter behavior without any change to code.

See: Implementation 6.1.1, Implementation 6.2.2, Implementation 7.4.1.

State-at-Handoff

A summary shown to people taking control from an AI system: recent AI actions, active trends, accumulated task queues, unacknowledged alerts and open physical or policy constraints.

Also called: state-at-handoff display.

See: Task 11.2, Implementation 11.2.3.

References:

  • Guidance FAA AC 120-71B, Standard Operating Procedures and Pilot Monitoring Duties for Flight Deck Crewmembers, 10 January 2017, from the United States Federal Aviation Administration. Paragraph 6.4, items 4 and 7, says transfer of pilot flying and pilot monitoring roles should include a short brief of aircraft state, and that a pilot returning from a break should be briefed on aircraft and systems states and current ATC instructions; the aviation analogue of a state-at-handoff summary, between people rather than from an AI system.

Super Intelligence (SI)

The term this profile uses, following Executive Order 14434, for the systems defined as artificial intelligence in 15 U.S.C. § 9401. In this profile SI and AI refer to the same systems, and the definition and scope are those set out under artificial intelligence (AI). The order defines SI by reference to that statutory definition, and provides for a new definition to be proposed. Cited works, proper names, quotations, identifiers, established terms of art and keyword lists keep “AI”.

Also called: AI, artificial intelligence.

Collides with: AI research usage, where “superintelligence” means hypothetical systems whose capabilities exceed those of humans; this profile does not use the term in that sense, and covers the same everyday systems as before, including rule-based, optimization and embedded systems. Other abbreviations that read as SI or contain it: the SI family of security controls (System and Information Integrity) in NIST SP 800-53, cited in this profile as SI-6, SI-10, SI-17 and others; the International System of Units (SI); safety instrumented system (SIS) in process safety; and the identifiers ASI01 to ASI10 in the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications.

See: footnote 1; Section 1 (Introduction).

See also: artificial intelligence (AI).

Section 2 directs executive departments and agencies to use “Super Intelligence” and “SI” in place of “Artificial Intelligence” and “AI” in non-statutory documents, and does not require previously issued documents to be altered. Symbolic AI

AI that reasons with explicitly represented knowledge, such as rules, logic, constraints or a knowledge base, rather than with patterns learned from data. It is often deterministic, but need not be: planning, search and probabilistic reasoning can involve randomness or uncertainty. Separately, the profile relies on some logic as a fallback, verifier or interlock and describes it in the text as non-AI (for example Task 2.2, Implementation 2.2.2); what matters there is that its behavior is fully specified and verified in advance and independent of the SI system, not whether its technique is symbolic or deterministic. The rationale it displays can be a trace of the steps it actually applied, which shows how it reached its output but not that its rules are correct. In a hybrid system, such a trace explains only the symbolic part.

Also called: symbolic reasoning, logic-based, rule-based.

See: Task 4.6, Implementation 2.5.1, Implementation 4.6.3, Implementation 10.1.2.

References:

  • Standard ISO/IEC 22989:2022, Information technology: Artificial intelligence: Artificial intelligence concepts and terminology, from ISO and IEC. Clause 3.1.33 defines “symbolic AI”, contrasted with subsymbolic AI in 3.1.34, and Clause 5.9 notes that modern AI systems typically combine both, as hybrid AI; consistent with this entry.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group AI Lexicon (the Shared Artificial Intelligence (AI) Lexicon), February 2026, from the Artificial Intelligence Executive Oversight Group, a public-private partnership formed by the U.S. Department of the Treasury with the FBIIC and FSSCC. The entry “Traditional AI”, also called symbolic or rule-based AI, describes AI that performs preset tasks using predetermined algorithms and rules; it ties the term to narrow tasks, which this profile does not.

Task-bounded authority

Authority for an AI agent to act that is limited to a specific task, operational mode or time window, expires automatically and can be revoked quickly, instead of being held as standing privileges. When an agent passes work to downstream tools or sub-agents, their authority is scoped to that task as well. Where the authority cannot be checked in real time, for example at a partitioned or remote site, the profile recommends reverting to pre-engineered local fallback behavior rather than continuing on unverified standing authority.

Also called: task-bounded and revocable agent authority.

See: Implementation 5.3.2; Task 5.3; least agency; Appendix C.1.

See also: withdrawal of authority.

References:

  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 1.2 sets out eliminating standing privilege in favor of short-lived, task- and context-bounded entitlements, and Section 3.4 says scope should narrow at each delegation hop and revocation should cascade downstream; consistent with this entry.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Principle E.15, Least Persistence, states that system elements and other resources are available, accessible and able to fulfill their design intent only for the time for which they are needed; task-bounded authority applies this to an agent’s authority to act.

Testing, evaluation, validation and verification (TEVV)

The planned activities that produce evidence of how an AI system or one of its components performs against its requirements and previous baselines, at each stage of its lifecycle. This is the AI RMF’s umbrella term, which the AI RMF orders as test, evaluation, verification and validation. “Validation” and “verification” are used here in their general sense.

Collides with: “model validation” in financial-sector model risk management, “the set of processes and activities intended to verify that models are performing as expected, in line with their design objectives and business uses” (Federal Reserve SR 11-7, Supervisory Guidance on Model Risk Management), which spans both verification (against design objectives) and validation (against intended use) in the general senses used here.

See: Task 1.3 (the anchor, which sets criteria for every lifecycle stage), Implementation 1.3.1, Implementation 1.3.2, Implementation 1.3.4, and Task 2.3, which extends TEVV to anomalous and edge-case conditions.

Appendix A describes TEVV tasks performed throughout the AI lifecycle, for design, development, deployment and operations, and says those carrying out verification and validation should ideally be distinct from those performing test and evaluation; the AI RMF term this entry adopts.

Training cutoff

The most recent date covered by the data a model was trained on. Information that changed after it, such as revised guidance, procedures, or regulations, is not reflected in the model unless supplied at run time, for example through retrieval, so the model may reproduce a superseded version.

Also called: knowledge cutoff, training data cutoff.

See: Implementation 1.1.3, Implementation 6.1.1, Practice 6, Appendix B (B.5).

Watchdog controller

An independent, non-AI monitor, implemented in simple deterministic hardware or software, that checks an AI system’s health, latency, setpoint stability and outputs, and starts a safe fallback sequence when they fall outside set limits. It judges whether the AI system as a whole is still performing as required, where a safety wrapper checks each command against defined constraints.

Also called: enforcement application.

Collides with: the embedded-systems watchdog timer, which only detects a system that has stopped responding.

See: Task 2.2, Implementation 2.2.2, Implementation 2.3.4, Implementation 3.4.3.

Withdrawal of authority

In this profile, the removal of every means by which a retired AI system can still act: its credentials, tokens and keys, scheduled and event-driven triggers and registered interfaces, authority it delegated to other entities, and the compute resources that may still hold any of these. Each removal is confirmed rather than assumed, and for any delegation that cannot be confirmed revoked, what could not be confirmed is recorded, together with the decision to complete the retirement on a recorded exception or hold it open, and any compensating control.

Collides with: rescinding an authorization to operate, a management decision that does not by itself remove a system’s credentials or triggers.

See: Practice 13, Task 13.2, Implementation 13.2.1, Implementation 13.2.2.

It does not address removing a system’s credentials or triggers, which this profile’s term covers.

Section 3.5 sets out decommissioning as revoking the agent’s identity and invalidating tokens, erasing private keys, handling sensitive data and revoking downstream delegated rights; it covers credentials and delegation, while this profile adds triggers, compute and confirmation of each removal.

Zero trust

A cybersecurity approach in which no implicit trust is granted to users, devices or services based solely on their network location, ownership or prior authorization, and access to each resource is authenticated and authorized separately. This profile applies the underlying principle, that trust is verified at each boundary rather than assumed, to AI risk more generally. The extension is in what is not assumed: in cybersecurity, chiefly that a requester is who it claims to be and is permitted to act; here, also that a legitimate, uncompromised AI system is behaving as intended. An AI system’s outputs, actions and inputs therefore warrant verification where they cross into other systems, physical processes and human decisions, whether a departure from expected behavior arises from compromise, malfunction or a change in operating conditions. For example, an AI agent holding valid credentials is still authorized action by action (Implementation 5.2.2), and guardrails independent of the AI system check its outputs before they reach a physical process (Task 3.2).

See: Section 1.2 (Scope); Task 3.2; Practice 5; Implementation 5.2.2.

Appendix A, Glossary, defines “zero trust architecture”, whose zero trust security model “eliminates implicit trust in any one element, component, node, or service and instead requires continuous verification of the operational picture via real-time information from multiple sources to determine access and other system responses”; nearer this profile’s extension than SP 800-207, since what is verified includes the operational picture, not only the requester.
