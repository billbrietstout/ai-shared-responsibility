Implementation 3.6.2: Scheduled “Manual Control” Drills

Regular drills show whether people and fallback systems can actually carry the operation without the SI system, and keep the skills to do so fresh. Where appropriate, establish a routine cadence of scheduled operational drills analogous to facility “live-load generator exercising” or “blackout drills” in OT, paper-based charting and manual triage cycles in healthcare, or offline transaction reconciliation procedures in finance. During designated low-risk or off-peak windows, simulate a failure of the AI system. The failure could include a loss of availability or a combination of other failures as identified in Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs. Include failures that are harder to recognize than a loss of availability, such as an SI system that stops updating without raising an alarm or that gives confident but incorrect advice. Include a loss of the SI system during an event that is already abnormal. Exercise the return from manual operation to SI-supported operation as well. These drills should be designed to achieve two primary outcomes:

  • Technical Validation: Verify that fallback deterministic control systems and analog backups successfully assume the operational load, and that independent safety protections act as designed, without data loss or safety breaches.
  • Human Readiness: Force human operators to actively manage operations via legacy or manual methods, preserving the critical muscle memory, tactical domain skills, and situational awareness required to safely navigate a real-world, unannounced AI outage.

For applications where this is not possible within safety or regulatory constraints, they should be performed within non-production systems, see Implementation 1.3.2: Categorization of Testing Environments for further details.

Evaluate each drill against criteria set in advance, such as time to recognize the failure, time to reach stable manual control and errors made, and compare the results with the levels set under Implementation 3.6.1: Baseline Human Competency Retention. Record findings and follow-up actions in an after action report. Treat a sustained decline, or a fallback that cannot carry the load, as a finding to act on, for example by narrowing reliance on the SI system or recording the residual risk. Where safety allows, some drills can be unannounced to the operators taking part, with a controller able to restore the SI system at once. Spread participation across shifts and crews, so that readiness is not demonstrated by the same few people.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How often are drills run, on the live system or in a non-production environment?
  • Are any drills unannounced, and with what safeguards?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Table D-4 describes the Self-Challenge approach to Coordinated Protection as affecting mission or business processes or system elements adversely in a controlled manner to validate the effectiveness of protections, with examples including role-based training exercises, fault injection, testing automated incident response and tabletop exercises, which is what a scheduled drill that takes the AI system away during a low-risk window does. Its discussion calls for such exercises throughout the system life cycle and with different scopes.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-84, Guide to Test, Training, and Exercise Programs for IT Plans and Capabilities, September 2006, from NIST. Section 2 describes functional exercises, which allow personnel to validate their operational readiness in a simulated operational environment. Section 5.5 describes the after action report that documents findings and recommendations from a functional exercise, and the action items assigned from it. This bears on evaluating each drill and following up on what it finds.
