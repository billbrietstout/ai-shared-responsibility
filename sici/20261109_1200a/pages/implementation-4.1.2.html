Implementation 4.1.2: Establish Tiered Intervention Procedures, Training, and Authorization

Implement or utilize existing tiered training, authorization, and access control for personnel intervening in AI system operation. This may include requiring multiple personnel to be available to authorize particularly high impact interventions. Where too few qualified people are available for multi-person authorization, for example on a night shift, at a remote site, or in a small organization, record that, and what offsets it, such as a review of each use afterward (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks). Where an intervention is needed immediately to protect public or environmental safety, weigh the delay that multi-person authorization adds (see NIST SP 800-53r5). Where a protective tier, such as stepping down the SI system’s autonomy or reverting to its fail-safe state, cannot itself create a hazard, consider allowing any qualified operator to invoke it without prior approval. Returning the SI system to a higher tier or to service should require defined approval and an understanding of the cause of the intervention.

Examples of tiers could include placing the system into “emergency mode,” overriding individual actions, partial suspension of operation, and full system isolation. For each tier, document the conditions for invoking it, the state the process is expected to be in afterward, how long it can safely remain in that state, and the criteria for leaving it. Ensure that sufficient appropriately trained and authorized personnel, including contractors and vendor field staff who hold intervention roles, are available commensurate with the system’s oversight mode and operational criticality. This is especially important where real-time human intervention is explicitly required as an active risk mitigation control. How the organization treats personnel who intervene in good faith within these procedures, when the intervention proves unnecessary, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. The prospect of personal cost can deter a timely intervention, while the organization also needs accountability for interventions that disrupt operations. Record the arrangement and the reason for it; Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses raises the equivalent question for reporting.

Consider an “emergency mode” protocol for external emergency response personnel who may need to safely isolate or override the AI system in an emergency situation. This may be important for reasons that include:

  • Organizational personnel may be unable to safely approach or communicate with the AI system during an emergency.
  • AI systems may behave in ways that responding personnel may not expect and may not have time or resources to address.
  • Emergencies and other incidents may generate inputs into the AI system that it does not expect and for which it may not have been tested, resulting in greater probability of erroneous and inappropriate outputs.

For example, during a fire or chemical emergency, or in a widespread disaster situation, organizational personnel may be unable to safely approach or communicate with a remote facility. Responders may need out-of-band emergency interfaces, like secured external emergency stop buttons (E-stops) (subject to applicable functional-safety certification requirements, such as IEC 60204-1, independent of any AI-specific considerations), to stop SI-enabled systems safely. For high-consequence systems, consider whether decision-making authority should be pre-established for scenarios where the underlying cause of an anomaly isn’t yet clear, so that incident response teams and emergency responders aren’t delayed during a machine-speed event, adversarial or otherwise. Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability covers the prior question of whether the organization can enforce such a decision once it is made; this Implementation covers who is authorized to make it in the moment.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which protective tiers can any qualified operator invoke without prior approval, and what approval does returning to a higher tier or to service need?
  • Which interventions need multi-person authorization, and how is the delay weighed where safety calls for an immediate response?
  • What may external emergency responders do under an emergency mode, and how are they authorized?
  • How are personnel who intervene in good faith treated when the intervention proves unnecessary?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement AC-3(2), Dual Authorization, calls for the approval of two authorized individuals for defined privileged commands or actions, and its discussion cautions that organizations consider the risk of dual authorization when immediate responses are necessary to ensure public and environmental safety, which is the tension between multi-person authorization and response time this Implementation balances.
