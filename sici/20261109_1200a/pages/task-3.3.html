Task 3.3: Establish Failure Notification Requirements

Define the operational conditions under which an AI system should overtly assert a state of exception (“fail loudly”) versus when it may attempt autonomous error recovery, clearly distinguishing notification alerts from physical fail-safe states. For high-consequence applications, this Task supports the situational awareness operators need to make timely, informed intervention decisions, and helps reveal degradation that “quiet” AI failures would otherwise hide. This is particularly important in multi-system and agentic workflows, where failures in one agent may be unintentionally masked by the resilience of other agents, silently reducing safety margins, degrading performance, or causing preventable damage. A failure can also compound across handoffs within a multi-agent system while every agent passes its own local checks, for example where one agent’s missing or uncertain result reaches the next as a clean input and a later agent reports more confidence than the chain supports. Carrying uncertainty indications across handoffs, and checking the end-to-end output as well as each agent’s, can help reveal this.

The need to “fail loudly” should be considered carefully against the limited oversight capacity of the people triaging the resulting alarms, and the throughput of any automated and/or SI triage that serves them, to avoid “alarm flood” situations, where a large number of “high priority” alarms reduce the ability to address the underlying problem, especially when unusual failures happen as discussed in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation. Track how these notifications perform once in service, for example how often they are false, how often a failure occurred without a timely notification, and how long operators took to respond. A notification that operators routinely acknowledge without taking action should be reviewed as a defect in the notification design. Periodically test that each notification path still reaches the people who should act on it. Organizations with an alarm management program can apply it to these notifications rather than running a separate one.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes the requirement that a departure announces itself at the time: which conditions oblige the AI system to assert an exception, and the thresholds at which degradation becomes a reportable event rather than a later finding. This Task does not cover capturing what the AI system did in the ordinary case, which Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies covers.
  • Valid: Contributes a quantitative limit on how far the operating environment, inputs, task and context may move from the conditions the AI system was established for before a mitigation is required, reviewed at a defined interval and on significant environmental change. This Task does not cover establishing fit in the first place, which Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Safe: Each condition that obliges the AI system to speak adds to what the same operators have to read, and this Task’s own text notes that a large number of high-priority alarms reduces the ability to address the underlying problem. Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation governs notification volume as a question of capacity.
  • Secure: The drift thresholds and notification conditions set here show how far an SI system’s behavior and inputs can move before anyone is told. An adversary who knows them could shift inputs slowly enough to stay under each threshold. The comparison with the original baseline in Implementation 3.3.2: Establish Maximum Drift Thresholds limits this. Protecting the thresholds is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Resilient versus Transparent: Where autonomous recovery restores an administrative function without touching a physical process or a safety margin, a retry that goes unannounced costs little. Where the same recovery masks a degradation that is consuming a safety margin, or one agent’s resilience absorbs another agent’s failure, the recovery removes the signal that anything moved. One way to balance this is the per-application assessment in Implementation 3.3.1: Context-Specific Failure Modes, which sets the notification requirement from the consequence rather than from recovery being available; others include announcing the recovery at a lower priority, or counting it against a drift threshold so that repetition becomes visible.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Task defines when an AI system should assert a failure overtly rather than recover quietly, and the pre-authorized pathway by which each class of output reaches the person who should act on it; it does not address what happens when notification volume exceeds what operators can triage. For that, see for example Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation.
  • MEASURE 2.5 (on measuring validity and reliability): This Task converts the documented limits of a model’s generalizability into quantitative drift thresholds, each with a defined response when the operating environment moves beyond it; it does not cover establishing those limits. For the testing that establishes them, see Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV).
  • GOVERN 4.3 (on establishing AI incident response and information-sharing processes): This Task makes an AI failure identifiable at the moment it occurs, by treating silent recovery as a choice to be justified for each application rather than as the default; it does not cover the analysis that follows once a failure has been identified. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who tracks how notifications perform once in service, and who decides when one is redesigned or removed?
  • Who periodically tests the notification paths, and how often?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 6.3.1, Anomalies and Events, recommends basing OT alerting thresholds on baselines of normal behavior and setting them with the expected response time in mind. In Appendix F.7.18, the OT discussion of SI-6 notes that shutting down and restarting OT may not always be feasible when an anomaly is identified.
  • Standard ANSI/ISA-62443-3-3, from the American National Standards Institute and the International Society of Automation. Sets system-level security requirements and security levels for industrial automation and control systems.
  • Standard ISA-18.2, Management of Alarm Systems for the Process Industries, from the International Society of Automation. Covers alarm prioritization and what makes an alarm actionable, which is the established practice a requirement to fail loudly has to sit inside if the resulting alarms are to stay actionable.
  • Standard IEC 60601-1-8:2006, Medical electrical equipment, Part 1-8: General requirements for basic safety and essential performance, Collateral Standard: General requirements, tests and guidance for alarm systems in medical electrical equipment and medical electrical systems, 2006, from the International Electrotechnical Commission. As its title states, it sets general requirements, tests and guidance for alarm systems in medical electrical equipment and systems. It is a healthcare counterpart to ISA-18.2 for making a failure known in a form that stays actionable.
  • Research Bransby, M. L., and Jenkinson, J., The management of alarm systems, Contract Research Report 166/1998, funded by the Health and Safety Executive, Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. Section 2.3 notes that where automatic protection guards against hazards to people or the environment, pre-alarms are almost always installed as well, giving the operator a chance to intervene before the protection operates. This is a process-industry precedent for failing loudly as a step separate from the fail-safe action.
  • Guidance Health and Safety Executive (2000) Better alarm handling, Chemicals Sheet No 6, from Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. Step 1 asks whether alarms are necessary and require operator action, and whether large numbers of alarms are acknowledged in quick succession. Step 3 repeats those measurements to see whether alarm performance is now reasonable. These bear on tracking notification performance and on notifications acknowledged without action.

To the Reviewer: >This concept is one that is familiar to the OT community and, for that matter, the electronic and mechanical engineering disciplines. For example, in many cases the purpose of an electrical or mechanical fuse is to cause a piece of equipment to “fail loudly” even if it could have kept going for a little bit longer (but then may have caused more damage when it finally failed). A fuse also stops the equipment; in this profile, failing loudly refers only to making the failure known, and stopping is the separate choice of a fail-safe state.
