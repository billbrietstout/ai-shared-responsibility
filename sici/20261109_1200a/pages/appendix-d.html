Appendix D: Crosswalks

To the Reviewer:

While SICI prioritizes operational workflows and entry points for critical infrastructure acquirers, owners, and operators, we recognize that systems engineering teams, software developers, and compliance auditors often organize risk management activities within formal lifecycle models.

This appendix begins with D.1, which maps common control areas to the assumptions SI systems can break and to where this Guidebook can help. Crosswalks mapping SICI’s 13 Practices to established systems engineering standards (ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288, NIST SP 800-160) and AI management frameworks (ISO/IEC 42001, NIST AI RMF) remain under development. We welcome feedback on D.1, and on which specific lifecycle phases, decision gates, or sub-processes within these standards offer the greatest utility for your organization’s compliance and engineering workflows, and additional general resources that we should prioritize for crosswalking.

We also welcome suggestions for sector-specific crosswalks that we can pass on to sector-specific organizations for their consideration.

D.1 Existing Control Areas and the Assumptions SI Systems Can Break

Organizations, auditors, and assessors can build on the controls they already apply to IT and OT systems when they approach SI systems. Those controls remain relevant, and for many of them what SI systems call for is an adjustment rather than a new control. However, many were designed around assumptions that SI systems do not always meet, so a control can be followed to the letter and still miss what it was meant to catch. The table below lists common control areas, the assumption each typically makes, how an SI system can break it, and where this profile suggests ways forward. It is illustrative rather than exhaustive, describes technical issues rather than interpreting any requirement, and is offered as voluntary considerations for those deciding how to apply their existing controls to SI systems.

Change management Behavior changes only through changes the organization approves Behavior shifts through drift, changes in inputs, vendor model updates, or runtime adaptation, without any approved change Task 7.5, Implementation 7.4.1, Implementation 6.2.2, Implementation 7.3.3, Implementation 2.3.4

D.1.1 Worked Example: Access Reviews

A periodic user access review typically checks that each account belongs to a current person or system, and that its permissions still match that role. The review assumes that access is held by an identifiable account, that its permissions are stable between reviews, and that reviewing the account reviews what can be done through it.

An AI agent can break each of these assumptions. It may act through a service account shared with other functions, or through credentials issued for a single task. It may compose requests to several downstream services, delegate work to sub-agents or tools that hold their own permissions, and use different permissions depending on the task it is given. A review of the agent’s account can then show permissions that look appropriate, while not showing what the agent actually did with them, or what it could reach through the systems it called.

The control’s intent, that access matches need and is reviewed, still applies. This profile suggests ways of meeting it for AI agents: giving each agent its own identity (Implementation 5.1.2: Establish Unique Identities for SI Service Accounts and Agents), bounding its authority to a task and making it revocable (Implementation 5.3.2: Task-Bounded and Revocable Agent Authority), authorizing individual actions at the time they are taken (Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies), and reviewing what agents actually did as well as what they were permitted to do (Implementation 5.4.1, Implementation 5.4.2). Which of these an organization needs depends on what its agents can reach and the consequences of misuse.

D.1.2 Using the table

The table is a starting point for conversations between those who own existing controls and those who build and operate SI systems. For a given control area, it can help identify whether the control’s assumption holds for a particular SI system and, where it does not, which items in this profile may help meet the control’s intent. Where an organization concludes that an item does not apply, recording the reasoning supports later review. Where an existing control or supervisory obligation already meets an item’s intent, recording that mapping, and any remaining difference, can avoid standing up a second governance process for the same system.
