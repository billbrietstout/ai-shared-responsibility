Task 5.1: Establish Identities and Authentication for SI Entities

A distinct identity for each SI entity helps an organization trace each action to the entity that took it, and revoke one misbehaving entity’s access without stopping the rest. Inventory SI entities and define authentication requirements that uniquely identify each SI entity, including those that are ephemeral, and ensure those identities are distinct across system boundaries, operational roles, and multi-agent deployment. In this task, an SI entity refers to an invocation of an SI agent or tool that performs atomic tasks, at a suitable level of granularity, and whose behavior should be meaningfully traced and disambiguated from the behavior of other invocations of the same or similar SI agents and tools. If the SI entity is compromised or otherwise found to be behaving in an undesired manner, the level of granularity should be sufficient to identify and revoke access while minimizing the effect on other SI entities.

An identity establishes which entity is acting, not what it is permitted to do. Whether that entity may reach a system is determined in Task 5.2: Determine and Implement Specific Access Requirements, whether a specific action is authorized at the moment of execution in Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies, and whether the action is safe to execute in Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs. These should be specified and audited as separate layers, so that a strong identity control is not taken as evidence of the others. Where an SI entity acts on behalf of a person or another SI entity, the credentials it presents and the records they produce should carry both its own identity and that of its principal, so that no hop in a chain presents itself as the originator.

The end of an identity’s lifecycle, where it is withdrawn permanently rather than revoked in response to a problem, is in Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result. The appropriate level of granularity will vary depending on variables that include:

  • Available resources (finance, time, expertise).
  • Expected threat level.
  • Expected impact.
  • Other mitigating controls.
  • Scale.
  • Vendor co-operation.
  • Application complexity.

Organizations should implement this task flexibly, to allow for changes in granularity as these variables change. Where cryptographic authentication is not possible, such as with legacy or compute-constrained OT devices, alternative controls may be necessary and enforced at an alternative point in the system.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Accountable: Contributes attribution at the granularity at which behavior needs to be disambiguated: a distinct identity for each AI entity, including ephemeral invocations, so an action traces to one invocation rather than to a shared service account. This Task does not cover recording what that entity then did, which Task 5.4: Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations logs at the access layer and Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities at the decision layer.
  • Secure: Contributes mutual, cryptographically verifiable authentication between an AI entity and the system it calls, so a spoofed component is kept from presenting itself as a trusted agent. This Task does not cover determining what an authenticated entity may reach, which Task 5.2: Determine and Implement Specific Access Requirements covers.
  • Resilient and Safe: Contributes selective containment: an entity that is compromised, drifting or erroring can be cut off by revoking its credentials, which stops what it is doing without withdrawing the credentials the rest of the deployment depends on. This Task does not cover deciding when an entity should be cut off, which Task 5.4 and Task 3.4 inform.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Where identities link each SI entity’s actions to the person on whose behalf it acted, the resulting records also describe that person’s activity in detail. How long these records are kept and who may use them, and for what, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations, including labor agreements. Their value for attribution and incident investigation pulls toward keeping them, and what they reveal about individual people pulls toward limiting their retention and use. Record the decision and the reason for it. Implementation 10.1.3: Forensic Integrity and Privacy-Preserving Log Partitioning addresses separating sensitive content within logs.
  • Secure: The credential audit in Implementation 5.1.1: Audit for and Replace Shared Credentials, Service Accounts, Secrets, Passwords, and API Keys records where each SI credential is stored, what it can reach, and which shared credentials remain in place with their compensating controls. An attacker could use this record to locate secrets worth stealing, and to find the shared credentials that reach OT or safety-relevant functions. Protecting it is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Accountable versus Reliable: Where AI entities are software invocations on capable hosts, splitting identity finely improves attribution at modest cost. Where the entity runs on legacy or compute-constrained equipment, per-invocation cryptographic credentials may exceed what the device can carry, and issuing them can degrade the function the device performs. Options this Task names include enforcing the identity at an alternative point in the system and accepting coarser attribution at the device. That coarser attribution may be the right end point for some devices, or for agents that serve several workflows, or an interim step, with the granularity revisited as resources, scale or expected threat change, which this Task asks organizations to plan for.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 1.6 (on building the AI inventory): This Task inventories AI entities at the granularity of a single agent or tool invocation, which is the unit at which an action can be attributed; it does not cover the system-level register of deployed AI. For that, see for example Task 7.1: Maintain an Internal Registry of SI Deployments.
  • MANAGE 2.4 (on decommissioning or superseding AI systems): This Task sets the identity granularity that lets one compromised entity be revoked while the others keep running; it does not cover bounding how long an authority lasts when nothing has gone wrong. For that, see for example Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Where a device cannot carry its own cryptographic identity, where is identity enforced instead, and how much coarser attribution is accepted there? (see the Accountable versus Reliable tension)
  • How long are records linking SI entity actions to people kept, and who may use them, and for what?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-63-4, Digital Identity Guidelines, July 2025, from NIST. Covers identity proofing, authentication and federation of users who interact with government information systems over networks. Section 1.1 states that the guidelines do not explicitly address machine-to-machine authentication, interconnected devices, or access to APIs on behalf of subjects. Appendix B defines an identifier as associated with a single, unique entity within a given context and never assigned to any other entity within that context. This bears on the uniqueness this Task asks of SI entity identities; the authentication requirements of the guidelines are written for users rather than for SI entities.
  • Guidance Agentic Identity and Access Management, Workstream 4: Secure Design Patterns for Agentic Systems, version 1.0, 20 March 2026, from the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 3.4 distinguishes an agent’s own rights from the rights it exercises on behalf of another principal, and holds that delegation should preserve both identities, with tokens carrying explicit actor and subject claims so that delegation chains remain visible. This bears on carrying both identities when an SI entity acts for a principal.
