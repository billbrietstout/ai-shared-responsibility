Task 6.3: Identify and Control Dataflows Across the SI Supply Chain

Knowing and controlling the data that flows between SI systems and external services helps an organization keep sensitive and operational information within agreed handling terms, and notice flows it did not expect. Define and maintain visibility over the dataflows between AI systems and external vendors, platforms, and services they connect to. Ensure that data exchanged with these external dependencies, including telemetry, inference requests (a trained AI model producing an output for a given input), model updates, and any operational or sensitive information transmitted outside the organization is authorized, monitored, and bound by data handling policies. These dependencies include the foundation model providers and subprocessors behind the immediate supplier, with which the organization may have no contract. For each flow, consider whether it is needed and whether the data can be minimized, aggregated, anonymized or delayed, and whether the vendor may use the organization’s data to train models offered to other customers. Some information already carries handling obligations, for example Critical Energy Infrastructure Information in the electric sector, and these should be checked before a flow is permitted. Integrating an SI system should not open paths between operational networks and external services that existing segmentation deliberately closed: flows can instead pass through the existing demilitarized zone, or through push-based or brokered arrangements that do not grant persistent inbound access.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Privacy-Enhanced: Contributes containment of what leaves continuously rather than as a one-off transfer: inference requests and operational telemetry, cataloged in Implementation 6.3.1: Catalog Data Egress Points and Third-Party Processing Destinations and routed through restricted enclaves in Implementation 6.3.2: Implement Egress Monitoring and Isolated, Access-Restricted Enclaves for SI Training and Telemetry. This Task does not address privacy risk arising from the AI system’s own outputs, which Implementation 7.5.3: Integrate Privacy Impact Assessments (PIA) into Update Cycles addresses in part, through privacy impact assessment at each significant update.
  • Secure: Contributes exfiltration detection at the AI egress path, watching cataloged destinations for unexpected volume or destination change (Implementation 6.3.2: Implement Egress Monitoring and Isolated, Access-Restricted Enclaves for SI Training and Telemetry), which is how loss of a model, training data or telemetry through a vendor endpoint becomes visible, whether through attack or misconfiguration. A destination change can also reveal a vendor endpoint or sub-processor change that was not announced, which Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management tracks. This Task does not cover authenticating the requesting AI entities, which Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools does.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The egress catalog in Implementation 6.3.1: Catalog Data Egress Points and Third-Party Processing Destinations and the per-tool-call records in Implementation 6.3.2: Implement Egress Monitoring and Isolated, Access-Restricted Enclaves for SI Training and Telemetry show what sensitive data leaves, by which paths, and which third parties hold it. An attacker could use them to pick the vendor or destination where the organization’s most sensitive data accumulates, or a path that monitoring does not cover. Protecting these records is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Privacy-Enhanced and Secure versus Reliable: Reliability may depend on third party remote monitoring, such as by a vendor with specialized knowledge. Restricting or inspecting what leaves for third parties may impair system reliability as the vendor may be unable to respond to degradation appropriately, and may limit the vendor services and updates the SI system can draw on. One way to balance this is to agree with the vendor on a reviewed, minimized extract that supports its monitoring, as in Implementation 6.3.2: Implement Egress Monitoring and Isolated, Access-Restricted Enclaves for SI Training and Telemetry. Others include vendor monitoring on premises under the organization’s access controls, or accepting less vendor support for less critical functions.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MANAGE 3.1 (on monitoring and responding to third-party risks): This Task monitors what leaves the organization to reach third-party AI services and applies egress controls; it does not cover monitoring the third party’s own behavior or performance. For that, see for example Task 6.2: Align SI and Existing CI Asset Lifecycle Management.
  • MEASURE 2.10 (on measuring privacy): This Task covers the data-exposure inventory that a privacy-risk examination depends on; it does not cover performing that examination or assess privacy risk arising from the system’s own outputs.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which dataflows to third parties are permitted, and may a vendor use the organization’s data to train models offered to others?
  • How much telemetry is shared with vendors for remote monitoring? (see the Privacy-Enhanced and Secure versus Reliable tension)

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-47 Revision 1, Managing the Security of Information Exchanges, from NIST. Section 3.1.5, Step 5: Document Appropriate Agreements, with Table 1, Potential Agreements Matrix, maps the method of exchange and the impact level of the information to the agreements that may be needed, from an entry in a tracking system for low-impact exchanges to information exchange agreements, memoranda of understanding, non-disclosure and service-level agreements for higher-impact exchanges via database- or web-based services. Sending inference requests or telemetry to an external AI service is an exchange via a web-based service, and the matrix gives a starting point for the handling terms this Task asks to be in place.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 2.2 suggests not sharing sensitive data with AI models hosted in environments controlled by external parties, and Section 2.3 notes that operators may not want vendors to train AI on operational data. Section 2.4 asks that new connection paths work through the existing demilitarized zone or jump host infrastructure, and Section 4.1 prefers push-based or brokered architectures that do not grant persistent inbound access. This bears on the dataflow controls this Task asks for.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). In Chapter 3, under Informational Leakage, it reports significant concern about the security of Critical Energy Infrastructure Information sent to or returned by generative AI systems. Energy sector only. This bears on checking the handling obligations that already attach to information before it flows to an SI vendor.
