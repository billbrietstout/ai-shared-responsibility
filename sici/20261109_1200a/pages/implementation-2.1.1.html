Implementation 2.1.1: Implement Baseline Rate Limiting

Rate limit the various resources and actions that an AI system can take, including consuming AI generation resources (often measured in tokens), making configuration change requests, taking control actions, and requesting attention from humans and other systems. This reduces the likelihood that automated activity will saturate critical networks or cause instability in other systems, particularly legacy systems that may not be designed for the rapid changes in resource usage or control that can result from some AI systems. It may also highlight signs of malfunction, such as a runaway loop or an SI system whose behavior has drifted, as well as adversarial action or interference.

Limits on AI generation resources also bound cost, since a runaway agent loop or retry storm can exhaust a usage budget while it loads other systems, and a budget exhausted part-way through operation can itself withdraw a capability that operations rely on. For agentic systems, such limits can include step, recursion-depth, time and per-run cost ceilings on each execution. A limit that halts use when it is exceeded can be more dependable than an alerting threshold, since a fast-accumulating workload can outpace an alert. Where several AI workloads compete for the same constrained resource, which of them yield when the resource is short is for the organization to decide in advance, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Giving priority to workloads that support the essential mission protects core service delivery, and the workloads that yield bear the delay, which may fall on obligations of their own. Record the priority and the reason for it. Allocating the resource by priority rather than only by quota is one way to enforce the priority. Retries can multiply load as well. Where several layers of a system each retry a failed request, one action can become many attempts on the system underneath. A retry budget that fails requests once it is used up can help contain this.

Enforce rate limits at a point outside the AI system that it cannot reconfigure, such as a gateway, a broker or the receiving system, and avoid giving the AI system credentials that would let it reach the resource without passing that point. Limits can apply to each AI agent identity as well as in aggregate, so that several agents together stay within what a downstream system tolerates and a limit event can be attributed to the agent that caused it (see Implementation 5.1.2: Establish Unique Identities for SI Service Accounts and Agents).

Ensure that when rate limits are hit, the system’s overall behavior falls back to its fail-safe state and that operators are notified via non-disruptive alerts where appropriate (Implementation 3.2.3 and Task 4.4). For requests that do not act on the physical process, such as generation or configuration change requests, rejecting the request with a clear error, queuing it or degrading the service can be the fallback behavior instead, as with the retry budget above. This may be particularly important where AI is used to improve efficiency in normal times, resulting in either more systems utilizing the same allocated resources or the same systems being allocated fewer resources. Such rate limits act as a first line of defense against overload, particularly in unusual or unforeseen situations where the efficiency gains may not hold.

To safeguard operator cognitive capacity and minimize alert fatigue, notification delivery metrics should follow exception batching, aggregation, and soft-throttling strategies, some of which are discussed in Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation. Rate limits should account for legitimate increases in command frequency during upset or abnormal operating conditions. An AI system responding correctly to a fast-moving abnormal condition, such as a rapid pressure excursion or a cascading equipment fault, may need to issue a higher volume of valid commands than during steady-state operation, and a rate limit tuned only to normal conditions could suppress legitimate corrective actions at the moment they matter most. Rate limit thresholds should therefore be informed by Implementation 2.1.2: Develop Process-Informed Guardrails, rather than applied as a single fixed ceiling across all operating conditions.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which workloads yield when a shared resource is short, and why? Where is that priority recorded?
  • What retry budget applies at each layer?

References:

  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, Version 2026, from the OWASP GenAI Security Project, a volunteer community project of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. LLM06:2026, Unbounded Consumption, names denial of wallet and recursive agent tool-calling loops among its examples. Its mitigations include limits on tokens and estimated cost rather than on requests alone, hard spending caps that halt inference rather than alert, and step, recursion-depth, time and per-run cost limits on agent executions, which bear on the cost and agentic limits in this Implementation.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control SC-6, Resource Availability, allocates defined resources by priority or quota. Its discussion describes priority protection as keeping lower-priority processes from delaying or interfering with higher-priority ones, which bears on deciding which AI workloads yield a shared resource to those that support the essential mission.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 2.4, Comparing OT and IT System Security, notes that OT and their real-time operating systems are often resource-constrained, that legacy systems often lack resources common on modern IT systems, and that indiscriminate use of IT practices in OT may cause availability and timing disruptions, which bears on this Implementation’s concern for legacy systems not designed for rapid changes in load.
  • Commentary “Addressing Cascading Failures,” Chapter 22 of Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems, written by M. Ulrich, from Google, published by O’Reilly Media. Its section on retries shows how retries issued at several layers multiply the attempts that reach the lowest layer. It suggests limiting retries per request and setting a server-wide retry budget beyond which requests fail rather than retry. This bears on the retry storms that this Implementation’s limits bound.
