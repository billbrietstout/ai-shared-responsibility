Practice 7: Manage Internal SI Supply Chain and Data Provenance

Establish operational oversight for SI assets that are under direct organizational control, including data and inference capabilities, once they have been acquired or developed.

Keeping track of the SI systems an organization controls directly, the data that shapes their learned behavior, and the changes made to them helps the organization oversee, update, and retire each system deliberately. This Practice focuses on the mechanics of deployed systems internal to the organization, including:

  • local training, transfer learning, and fine-tuning,
  • retrieval-augmented data repositories,
  • version control for safety wrappers,
  • local data provenance and flow,
  • localized SI model and system configurations, and
  • locally hosted inference.

Keeping earlier versions of models, prompts, and retrieval sources after a change can also keep past decisions interpretable (see Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities) although this may carry substantial overhead for systems that update their behavior on a regular basis. In operational settings, these changes can go through the organization’s existing change management process, such as for process safety, or through a separate process for SI. An existing process brings established safety review and familiarity to operations staff, but may lack reviewers able to judge changes to models, data or prompts.

This Practice also describes the visibility needed to mitigate “Shadow SI” risks and helps keep internal modifications to automated logic traceable and mission-aligned. In addition, it includes measures that support Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems, and informs the need to update the SI attack and risk surface as discussed in Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface.

Practice 6 and Practice 7 are two sides of the same SI supply chain. This Practice covers what the organization manages directly once a component is in its hands, whether acquired or developed. Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management covers what can only be obtained from vendors and third parties, from before acquisition through the life of the contract. Where an internal SI system calls an external service, both apply: Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management for what the vendor discloses and commits to, and Implementation 7.4.2: Implement Regular Integrity Checks for Systems that Interface with External SI Services for what the organization checks for itself.

Note on terminology: In Practices 6 and 7, “SI supply chain” means the supply chain of the SI system itself: its models, data, software and services. SI systems used to manage an organization’s physical supply chain, such as logistics, routing or procurement optimization, are within scope as SI systems and are covered by the Practices that apply to any SI system, rather than by Practices 6 and 7.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes a single internal account of what SI is running, at which version, built from which models, drawing on which data, and where that data came from, so that oversight rests on a maintained inventory rather than on what departments happen to report. This Practice does not cover components obtained from vendors before acquisition, which Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes a record, for each change to a model, its data, or the safety mechanisms around it, of who authorized the change and on what evidence, so that internal modifications to automated logic stay traceable. This Practice does not cover capturing what the SI system did in operation, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.
  • Secure: Contributes protection against unauthorized change to what shapes SI behavior from the inside: the data it learns from or retrieves, its system prompts and safety filters, and the parameters of systems that adapt while running. This Practice does not cover deciding whether a specific action is permitted at the moment it is carried out, which Practice 3 and Practice 5 address.
  • Any characteristic a change could degrade: Contributes review of authorized changes to the same data, prompts, safety filters and adaptive parameters before they take effect (Task 7.5: Conduct Impact Assessments and Risk Management for SI System and Model Updates), so that a well-intended change does not quietly lose behavior that was already correct, loosen a safety boundary, or otherwise degrade another characteristic by an unacceptable amount. This Practice does not cover setting the standard a change is judged against, which Practice 1 and Practice 2 cover.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The asset list, data inventory, and SI bills of materials this Practice builds together describe what SI is running, where, on what data, and behind which controls. Together this information could help an attacker choose and plan an attack. Task 7.3: Manage Version Control and Logging for Internal SI Safety Mechanisms already treats sensitive system prompts as sensitive information; how the inventory itself is classified and handled is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations, as with other descriptions of where its systems are exposed.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Any characteristic a change could degrade versus any characteristic it would fix: Where a change to a model, its data, its prompts or its safety mechanisms can wait, full change control costs little and guards against a change that quietly degrades safety, security, reliability or another characteristic. Where the change is itself an urgent fix, the same review leaves the fault in place while it runs. If the normal review cannot wait, options include suspending the SI system under Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness until the reviewed change is ready, where the organization has prepared a way to carry on the function without it, or using an emergency change path, if the organization has chosen to establish one (Implementation 7.4.1: Define and Implement Requirements for Approvals and Logging of Updates and Changes to SI Systems). Suspension carries its own considerations, such as the loss of the function and the extra load on personnel who take it over (see Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning). Changes made through an emergency path are reviewed and recorded through the normal process as soon as practical afterward. Until then, temporary measures such as closer monitoring or tighter guardrails can limit the risk of a change that has not yet been reviewed.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 1.6 (on building the SI inventory): This Practice covers the inventory of SI systems, models and data under direct organizational control, including legacy systems and those found by network scanning; it does not cover setting the resourcing of that inventory that GOVERN 1.6 also calls for.
  • MAP 4.2 (on establishing risk controls for SI system components): This Practice covers internal controls for the components it governs: data provenance, version control for safety mechanisms, authorization of changes, and assessment of updates; it does not cover controls on third-party technologies before acquisition. For those, see for example Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management.
  • GOVERN 1.7 (on establishing decommissioning processes): This Practice covers visibility that enables the retirement of internally developed models, datasets, and pipelines without breaking what depends on them or leaving unmonitored attack surfaces; it does not cover withdrawing the authority of an SI system that is still operating or ensuring that critical capability and information is preserved. For that, see for example Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Do changes to SI systems go through the existing management of change process, a separate process, or both, and who reviews changes to models, data or prompts?
  • Who resources the inventory, data certification, and change review this Practice calls for, and what is deferred, with what remaining risk, when resources fall short?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-128, Guide for Security-Focused Configuration Management of Information Systems, August 2011, with updates as of October 2019, from NIST. Section 2.2 organizes configuration management into planning, identifying and implementing configurations, controlling configuration changes and monitoring, and notes that monitoring identifies undiscovered or undocumented components and unauthorized changes. Section 2.3.4 treats a consolidated component inventory as the basis for visibility and control, and Section 2.3.7 keeps older approved baselines available for review or rollback. Section 3.3.2 asks that unscheduled changes, made when time does not allow the established process, still be managed and be reviewed as soon as practical afterward. Written for information systems, it is the established discipline this Practice extends to models, data, prompts and safety wrappers.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-218A, Secure Software Development Practices for Generative AI and Dual-Use Foundation Models: An SSDF Community Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2 notes that model source code is amenable to practices for versioning, lineage and reproducibility, but final weights are defined only after training and fine-tuning, where tracking collection, processing and training becomes limited, and asks organizations to document the parts those practices do not cover. Task PS.3.2 recommendation R1 asks that the provenance of a model and its components and derivatives be tracked. Both bear on this Practice’s lineage from base model to locally fine-tuned adapter.
  • Guidance Establish Risks and Controls for the AI Supply Chain, version 1.0, 12 June 2025, from Workstream 1, Software Supply Chain Security for AI Systems, of the Coalition for Secure AI, an OASIS Open Project. Section 3.2.2, under Internal Model Security Considerations, calls the assumption that internally developed and stored models are inherently secure a common misconception: they remain open to data poisoning, and privileged insiders could modify them leaving few forensic traces. Section 4.1 asks executives to implement discovery processes identifying SI use within internal systems. Both bear on this Practice’s internal scope and its visibility of Shadow SI.
