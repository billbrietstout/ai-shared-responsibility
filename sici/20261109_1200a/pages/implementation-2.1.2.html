Implementation 2.1.2: Develop Process-Informed Guardrails

Process-informed guardrails let an SI system use more resources or act more freely when the process can absorb it, and hold it tighter when it cannot. They dynamically determine the appropriate rate at which AI systems should be utilizing resources or taking actions. For example:

  • An AI-based optimization system may use more tokens during quiet periods.
  • Rapid changes to power grid load distribution should be avoided when the grid is running close to capacity.
  • The number of units or feeders that an AI system may adjust at once, and the minimum interval between adjustments to the same control loop, may be set tighter during startup and shutdown than in steady operation.

These guardrails should be deterministic and based on the application’s needs. Such guardrails give the AI system flexibility to use additional resources or take additional actions when the application requires it, such as during quieter periods, while maintaining tight control during rapid change or heightened activity. Complex guardrails can cause problems of their own, such as tripping into a restrictive mode when it is not wanted or entering feedback cycles. Keeping the rules simple, with a way to quickly disable or retune them, can help. Disabling or retuning a guardrail removes or weakens a protection, so who may do it, and under what conditions, is for the organization to decide in advance. The tighter limits apply only in uncommon conditions and so are seldom exercised, and a path that is rarely used may not work when it is needed. Exercising it regularly can help.

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 4.1 cautions that with active AI systems directly influencing control, problems can escalate before operators become aware of them. Where AI is actively updating control logic, it asks for safety thresholds, alternative sensor output, or state changes that add human-in-the-loop intervention points, which bears on tightening control of AI action as process conditions change.
  • Commentary “Addressing Cascading Failures,” Chapter 22 of Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems, written by M. Ulrich, from Google, published by O’Reilly Media. Its section on load shedding and graceful degradation notes that a degraded mode is seldom exercised in steady-state operation, and that the code path never used often does not work. It adds that complex degradation logic can trip when not desired or enter feedback cycles, and suggests a way to quickly turn such logic off or retune it. This bears on keeping process-informed guardrails simple and exercised.
