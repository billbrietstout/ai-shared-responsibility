Implementation 1.2.2: Develop High-Fidelity Operational Baselines Including Edge-Case Performance Under Stress

Extend the baseline in Implementation 1.2.1: Establish Manual or Legacy Process Performance and Safety Baselines to include performance under stressed or edge-case conditions, not just normal operation, so that any brittleness introduced by the AI system can be identified by comparing like-for-like stress performance rather than only normal-condition performance.

For example, in process control, the number of alarms presented to operators can be counted during normal operation and during a plant upset, and the same counts repeated after a change to show whether performance has improved. Counts after an upset can be recorded for fixed periods, such as the first minute and the next ten minutes, together with the type of upset. Stressed conditions to baseline can be drawn from the adverse conditions enumerated in Implementation 1.1.2: Structured Requirements Mapping.

Where records of stressed conditions are few, operator training simulator sessions and exercises that introduce, for example, the loss of a sensor, a communications outage or an equipment trip can supply the stressed-condition baseline, measuring detection, response time, errors and recovery.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which stressed and edge-case conditions are baselined, and how is each reproduced?

References:

  • Guidance Health and Safety Executive (2000), Better alarm handling, Chemicals Sheet No 6, from Great Britain’s workplace health and safety regulator. Step 1 asks how many alarms occur during normal operation and how many during a plant upset, and gives a separate rate target for each. Step 3 repeats the original measurements to measure progress. It is a worked example, from process plant, of a baseline taken under normal and stressed conditions and measured again after a change.
  • Research Bransby, M. L. and Jenkinson, J. (1998), The management of alarm systems: a review of current practice in the procurement, design and management of alarm systems in the chemical and power industries, HSE Contract Research Report 166, research for Great Britain’s Health and Safety Executive drawing on visits to 15 chemical and power plants. Volume 1, Section 4.6, Number of alarms during a major plant upset, reports that operators find alarm systems significantly less helpful after an upset than in normal operation. It suggests recording counts for set periods after an upset, with the type of upset. This bears on measuring the stressed-condition baseline.
