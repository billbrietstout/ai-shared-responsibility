Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations

Establish expectations of, and safeguards to maintain, essential service delivery and functional safety when SI system performance deviates from expected levels, regardless of the cause.

Because an SI system can depart from expected performance for reasons that are hard to foresee, a layered set of safeguards helps an organization keep essential functions running: some that act on the effect whatever its cause, and others that depend on recognizing a particular cause, each where it fits. Such safeguards should be considered at multiple points in the system to reduce the likelihood of cascading failures. These include usage thresholds and rate controls (Task 2.1: Establish SI Usage Thresholds and Rate Control Mechanisms), redundant paths, and watchdog controllers (Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions).

Where an SI system is hosted by a third party or reached over external networks, its availability and latency also depend on networks and providers outside the organization’s control. The safeguards should then also cover a slowed or lost external service. Related Practices include Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior, which defines the deterministic boundaries and fail-safe states these safeguards fall back to, and Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness, which covers operator intervention when operators are called on. Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning covers sustained operation when the SI system is unavailable for an extended period.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Resilient: Contributes the layered safeguards that keep essential service delivery and functional safety in place while SI performance is away from expected levels, through fallback to reduced capability or to a known-safe mode of operation. This Practice does not cover sustained operation during, or recovery from, a prolonged SI system outage, which Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning covers.
  • Robust: Contributes the expectation itself: the range of conditions and uses across which SI performance should hold, and the testing that shows where it stops holding. This Practice does not cover establishing the level of performance required in the first place, which Practice 1: Establish Requirements for Success covers.
  • Reliable: Contributes expectations for repeated re-checking against a maintained reference (Implementation 2.3.1: Baseline SI System Performance), so that gradual departure from expected performance is found on a schedule, before it becomes deficient. This Practice does not cover the moment-to-moment detection of sudden departures, which Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure and Safe: Redundant paths, independent monitors, and fallback routes each add a further way for an instruction to reach the process, whether that instruction is malicious or simply wrong. Examples include a switchover forced by a faulty sensor or a fallback that has fallen out of step with the system it backs up. Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface covers the scoping of the attack and risk surface that grows as they are added.
  • Fair: Rate limits, throttling and fallback to reduced capability decide which requests are served when capacity is short, and those served by lower-priority functions bear the delay. Where such priorities are set, they should be explicit and consistent with the organization’s service obligations.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Robust versus Resilient: Where the SI system meets a departure it was designed to absorb, holding performance and degrading gracefully point the same way. Where safeguards are set to keep the SI-enabled function performing across a wider range of conditions, the boundary at which the fallback should engage becomes harder to observe, and the fallback, when it does engage, starts from further outside familiar territory and potentially with less available time, power, control overhead, or other resources to degrade gracefully. One way to balance this is to fix the point at which the fallback engages from the consequence of being wrong rather than from how far the SI system can be pushed. Others include treating the wider range as a documented allowance that prompts review rather than as normal operation.
  • Accurate versus Robust: Where operation stays close to the conditions the SI system was tested in, closer agreement with the reference and steadiness across conditions move together. Where the expectation is set to hold across a wide range of conditions, an SI system tuned for its best average agreement may become more fragile where conditions differ significantly from the average. One way to balance this is to state the required performance separately for normal and for stressed conditions, so that the two are procured, tested and accepted as distinct expectations, with Task 1.2’s normal and stressed baselines for the existing process as a point of comparison. Others include accepting a narrower range of conditions of use and carrying the remainder on the fallback.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • What minimum level of service is kept for each essential function while the SI system is degraded, within any regulatory or contractual obligations?
  • When SI performance departs from expected levels, who decides whether to keep operating, throttle the system, or move to the fallback, and do they have the information and authority to act in time?
  • Where an SI system depends on a service or network outside the organization’s control, what happens to service delivery when that external service is slow or unavailable?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 4.1.1, Framing OT Risk, notes that OT may have strict availability or recovery requirements and that failures in one system or process can easily cascade to others. Its Table 3 gives example impact definitions, from outages across sites to public confidence, people and the environment.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v1r1, Engineering Trustworthy Secure Systems, from NIST. Its Appendix E states the design principles for trustworthy secure design. Protective Failure (E.23) holds that the failure of one system element should be contained, so that it does not cause unacceptable loss or trigger a further loss scenario, which is the cascading failure this Practice asks a layered approach to reduce; Loss Margins (E.18), Defense in Depth (E.9) and Protective Recovery (E.24) carry the rest of the layering. Section 3.5 reasons in terms of loss, and its Table 2 names limited capacity, being diminished delivery of function, service or capability, as one of the dimensions along which loss can be limited.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Table 3 defines the Continue objective as maximizing the duration and viability of essential mission or business functions during adversity, and Table D-1 breaks it into minimizing degradation of service delivery, minimizing interruptions in service delivery and ensuring that ongoing functioning is correct, which corresponds to the service delivery floor this Practice sets. The discussion of the goals in Table 2 counts structural failures and unexpectedly high performance loads as adversity alongside attacks, so the objective is not limited to adversary action. In Table D-4, the Consistency Analysis approach to Coordinated Protection is defined as determining whether and how protections can be applied in a coordinated, consistent way that minimizes interference, potential cascading failures or coverage gaps, which bears on this Practice’s call for safeguards at multiple points that reduce the likelihood of cascading failures.
  • Standard ANSI/ISA-62443-3-3-2013, Security for industrial automation and control systems, Part 3-3: System security requirements and security levels, from the International Society of Automation, a technical society that develops automation standards, approved as an American National Standard. Clause 11, Resource Availability, is the industrial control counterpart of the service delivery floor this Practice sets.
  • Guidance Adapting Zero Trust Principles to Operational Technology, published 29 April 2026 by the Zero Trust Operational Technologies Security Working Group, a joint initiative of the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency, the Department of War and the Department of Energy, with the Department of State, the Federal Bureau of Investigation and NIST contributing. Pages 12 and 13, on network and microsegmentation, hold that isolation prevents cascading failures across interconnected systems and that segmentation limits the blast radius of a cyber or operational incident, so the reasoning reaches a malfunctioning SI path and not only a compromised one. The guide’s Table 1 states what OT segmentation is for in contrast to IT segmentation: system availability and system safety rather than data confidentiality, with an extremely low tolerance for downtime because disruptions can have physical consequences.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by the United States Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency. Section 2.3 notes that some OT devices come with built-in SI that may require internet connectivity to function, and suggests asking vendors whether they can operate on premises or without continuous access to the internet or vendor cloud. Section 2.4 adds that cloud integration may cause data transmission latency. This bears on quality-of-service expectations for externally hosted SI.
