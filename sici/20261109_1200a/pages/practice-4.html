Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness

Establish and maintain documented procedures that allow operators to intervene in, suspend, or override SI system actions that are unreliable, unexpected, or potentially harmful to critical operations.

Prepared intervention procedures help an organization step in safely when an SI system behaves in a way that is unreliable, unexpected, or potentially harmful. Organizations with established emergency procedures for equipment and automation can build on them, and those without can start from the Tasks below. Intervening in an SI system is harder than the presence of an off switch suggests. Simply disabling an SI system can leave the process it was controlling in an unsafe state, so intervention needs a defined handover rather than a cut-off (Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures). Before they can act safely, personnel need to understand what the SI system was doing, often under time pressure and after long periods of passive monitoring (Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness). They also need information and control channels that depend as little as practical on the SI system they are overriding (see How Far to Go; Task 4.3: Establish Independent (“Out-of-Band”) Communication, Monitoring, Alerting, and Control Capabilities). SI systems can also generate more notifications than people can triage, using up the attention that intervention depends on (Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation).

Where events unfold faster than people can respond, given the available personnel, time, attention, and training, real-time human oversight is not achievable. Defining the operating regime for those cases in advance helps responses keep up (Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation). Confidence indications and rationale shown to personnel can make incorrect actions, recommendations, and other outputs more persuasive. Those that accurately reflect how the SI system actually reached its output, appropriately presented, can help to identify such errors (Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output).

This Practice covers intervention in the moment. Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior defines the automated boundaries and fail-safe states that intervention can fall back on, and Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning covers sustained operation without the SI system. Other Practices also bear on intervention, for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures for the incident response that follows it and Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust for the training that intervening personnel need.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes the human intervention path: stopping, overriding or falling back when the system’s own constraints have not prevented a hazard. This Practice does not cover those constraints, which Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior covers.
  • Resilient: Contributes the human-mediated fallback: suspending, overriding or reverting the SI-enabled function so operation can continue while the SI system is not trusted. This Practice does not cover sustained manual operation, which Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning covers.
  • Explainable & Interpretable: Contributes the situation awareness an intervening person needs at the moment of acting: what the SI system is doing, and what response that calls for. This Practice does not cover the underlying telemetry, which Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure and Safe: The intervention routes this Practice creates are themselves privileged paths into the control system. Some intervention tiers, out-of-band channels and emergency modes bypass the normal control path and the checks built into it, so a route misused deliberately can undermine security, and one invoked in error can cause the hazard it exists to prevent. Tiered authorization on the personnel routes (Implementation 4.1.2: Establish Tiered Intervention Procedures, Training, and Authorization) limits both, at a cost in speed that Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures weighs. The procedures, tier definitions, and channel inventories this Practice produces also show where and how the SI system can be stopped, bypassed, or blinded. An attacker could use them to trigger a disruptive intervention, or to act where intervention would be slow. Protecting those documents is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Safe versus Resilient: Where the process can be diverted or stopped safely, a deterministic trip and graceful degradation point the same way. Where diverting or stopping is itself hazardous, such as a vehicle or industrial process, they separate: Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures notes that disabling the SI system can leave the overall system unsafe, so the tiered ladder in Implementation 4.1.2: Establish Tiered Intervention Procedures, Training, and Authorization exists to let an operator choose a point between a full isolation that halts the process and a partial suspension that keeps it running.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who holds the authority to override or suspend each SI-enabled function, and do they have the visibility, time, access, and support to use it?
  • What should personnel do when the preferred intervention is unavailable, for example when a fallback controller or manual capability has not been maintained?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-61r3, Incident Response Recommendations and Considerations for Cybersecurity Risk Management, from NIST. Section 2.1 sets out the incident response life cycle within which an override and the recovery after it sit. Section 2.3 lists what the incident response policy should settle in advance, including which roles hold the authority to confiscate, disconnect or shut down technology assets, which is the authorization question this Practice asks about SI systems.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Section C.3 discusses the relationship between cyber resiliency and safety. It notes that the unacceptable states defined by safety engineering may constrain cyber resiliency solutions or be used in trade-off analyses, and that achieving an objective such as Continue or Reconstitute may require a system to operate transiently in an unsafe (or insecure) state, depending on how stakeholders prioritize required properties. That is the trade-off behind choosing between full isolation and partial suspension of an SI system.
  • Standard IEC 61508, Functional safety of electrical/electronic/programmable electronic safety-related systems, from the International Electrotechnical Commission, an international standards body for electrical, electronic and related technologies. Defines functional safety for electrical, electronic and programmable electronic safety-related systems, meaning systems whose safety depends on their operating correctly.
  • Standard ISO 26262-1:2018 Road vehicles - Functional safety, from the International Organization for Standardization. The road vehicle sector’s functional safety series.
