Implementation 4.1.3: Regularly Practice and Evaluate Intervention Processes

Conduct tabletop exercises and, where feasible, hands-on drills to test intervention procedures from Implementation 4.1.1 and Implementation 4.1.2 under adversarial and unexpected scenarios. Where the organization already runs emergency or incident response exercises, intervention scenarios can be added to them rather than run as a separate program. To prevent unintended operational triggers during these simulations, testing should be performed on systems in accordance with Implementation 1.3.2: Categorization of Testing Environments. Use established TEVV protocols from Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle and as extended in Task 2.3: Establish Protocols for and Regularly Perform Systematic and Achievable Testing, Evaluation, Validation, and Verification (TEVV) to guide intervention procedure testing. Where feasible, at least one exercise per cycle should involve a correlated scenario in which multiple safeguards are degraded together, rather than testing single-point failures only. Evaluation criteria set before each exercise, and recorded afterward in an after action report, can help show whether the exercise met its objectives and where further exercises are needed. Measures can include the time from the first cue to detection, to decision and to the intended process state, whether the appropriate tier was chosen, and whether the override path worked, each compared with the time available under Implementation 4.5.1: Define Engineering Time-Constant Thresholds. Over successive cycles, exercises should include different shifts and any backup control locations. How hesitation or confusion about authority during an exercise is treated is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Treating it as a finding about the procedure encourages candid participation and brings unclear authority to light, while some cases may also point to individual training needs under Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust. Record how such findings are handled.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How often are exercises run, which are hands-on, and which shifts and locations do they rotate across?
  • What evaluation criteria are set before each exercise, and how are findings about hesitation or unclear authority handled?
  • Who resources exercises, including staff time away from operations and any test environment, and where a hands-on drill is not feasible, what is done instead and what risk remains?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control IR-3, Incident Response Testing, calls for the effectiveness of the incident response capability to be tested at a defined frequency, and its discussion names checklists, walk-through and tabletop exercises, and parallel or full-interrupt simulations as the forms that testing takes. Enhancement IR-3(2) coordinates that testing with contingency, continuity and critical infrastructure plans, which is where an exercise of a correlated scenario such as the one in this Implementation would be coordinated.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-84, Guide to Test, Training, and Exercise Programs for IT Plans and Capabilities, September 2006, from NIST. The Executive Summary distinguishes tabletop exercises, which are discussion-based and do not involve deploying equipment, from functional exercises, in which personnel perform their duties in a simulated operational environment. Section 4.3 describes the after action report, written against evaluation criteria developed before the exercise. These bear on the choice between tabletop exercises and hands-on drills, and on how an exercise is evaluated.
