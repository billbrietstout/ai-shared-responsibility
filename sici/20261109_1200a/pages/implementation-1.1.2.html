Implementation 1.1.2: Structured Requirements Mapping

A structured requirements matrix lets the organization see, for each requirement, where it came from, what will meet it once the SI system is in place, and how it will be verified. Conduct cross-functional requirements analysis with SMEs, including those from engineering, operations, safety, compliance, and legal departments, and front-line personnel who will supervise, rely on, or override the SI system, to translate high-level mission goals into a structured requirements matrix for the SI system, and the broader operational system within which it operates.

Requirements should explicitly define the operational task and mission scope, including the tasks that the system (AI and overall) is expected to perform, and its functional dependencies across sensors, network backbones, control software, physical actuators, and human operators in the oversight loop.

They should also define the conditions and constraints under which the system is expected to perform those tasks, including:

  • Defined Operating Envelopes such as quantified input/output ranges (e.g., deterministic setpoint limits, clinical dosage thresholds, transaction execution ceilings), latency ceilings, and physical tolerance boundaries.
  • Environmental Operating Conditions, including physical, digital, and operational boundary parameters (e.g., sensor noise thresholds, data schema variations, network latency ranges, equipment wear tolerances).
  • Adverse and Adversarial Conditions, including enumerating expected environmental stresses (e.g., weather events, process upsets, sensor degradation) and attack surfaces as mapped in Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface (e.g., prompt injection, data poisoning, compromised telemetry) and corresponding explicit behavioral requirements, such as those outlined in Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior.
  • Classification by physical consequence, such as explicit classification of requirements that directly impact physical machinery, environmental safety, or human life (see Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses), triggering elevated verification rigor (see Implementation 12.1.1: Artifact Risk Classification) and deterministic boundary constraints (see Implementation 3.2.2: Define and Independently Enforce Deterministic Operational Constraints).
  • Other non-functional requirements, such as specific auditability standards and data retention periods (see Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies).

For each requirement, record its source, how it is met today (by an engineered safeguard, a written procedure or operator judgment alone) and what will meet it once the SI system is in place, such as the SI system, a deterministic constraint, a human approval step or an existing safety function. Requirements met today by operator judgment alone, and that cannot be measured, warrant particular attention. An SI system can erode them without any indicator changing. Record how each requirement will be verified, and restate any that cannot be verified as written, such as “never unsafe under any input”, in a verifiable form, or allocate it to a layer where it can be verified.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which functions and front-line roles take part in requirements mapping for each SI system?

References:

  • Research Recent policy developments on AI in the labour market, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 63, July 2026, from the OECD Directorate for Employment, Labour and Social Affairs. Section 7, Box 7 and Table 2, reports that the G7 countries, Costa Rica and Colombia all recommend involving workers in occupational safety and health matters, and many require it, because workers often know where risks arise. This is the reason for bringing front-line personnel into requirements analysis.
  • Standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023, Systems and software engineering: system life cycle processes, published jointly by ISO, IEC and IEEE. Clause 6.4.3, the system requirements definition process, asks that requirements be analyzed for characteristics including traceable and verifiable, with conflicts identified and resolved. It keeps bidirectional traceability to verification methods and to allocated requirements, and refers to ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148 for detail on requirement characteristics. This bears on recording a verification method and an allocation for each row of the matrix.
