Implementation 2.5.1: Train Across the Full Operating Range

AI systems trained within a narrow slice of their operating envelope risk failing when conditions legitimately vary across the broader system profile. To support resilience across both digital execution layers and, where applicable, cyber-physical operating ranges: Simulation & Synthetic Parameter Cycling: When training models via simulations or digital twins, developers should intentionally randomize and cycle environmental, digital, and physical process parameters across their full allowed specification limits (e.g., maximum/minimum rated latencies, tolerances, pressure and thermal thresholds, sensor noise, equipment wear states, and ambient variations). Physical & Hardware Representative Testing: When training or validating on physical hardware, testing should be conducted across a representative cross-section of functionally equivalent assets, incorporating varying levels of component wear, calibration drift, and environmental exposure. Symbolic Background Knowledge & Constraint Encoding: For systems utilizing symbolic background knowledge or formal domain rules, system operating envelopes and component specifications should be explicitly encoded so the AI learning process avoids relying on capabilities outside authorized design boundaries. This is intended to prevent the AI from constructing execution policies that fail when a system or component operates at a different, yet fully compliant, point within its formal specification sheet. Historical operating data tends to be dominated by steady-state operation, so legitimate but less frequent regimes, such as startup, shutdown, low load, process upsets and degraded instrumentation, may need deliberate coverage in addition to the parameter cycling above. Reporting validation results separately for each operating regime, and separately for real and for simulated or synthetic data, can keep strong aggregate or simulated performance from hiding weakness at the edges of the range. This needs each training and test record to carry its source. The parameter cycling above is related to domain randomization, a technique used in robotics in which simulation parameters are set randomly during training to learn behavior that generalizes to similar real-world situations (see Geirhos et al. 2020).

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How much of the specification range is covered in training, given the installations the system will meet? (see the Robust versus Accurate tension in Task 2.5: Verify that Learned Behavior Is Robust to Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance)

References:

  • Guidance ISO/IEC TR 5469:2024, Artificial intelligence: functional safety and AI systems, a technical report from the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission. Covers risks associated with environmental complexity, data distribution against hazard analysis, and test coverage limitations.
  • Research Taguchi, G. (1986). Introduction to Quality Engineering: Designing Quality into Products and Processes. Asian Productivity Organization, an intergovernmental body headquartered in Tokyo. Establishes the engineering principle of exposing a system to deliberate parameter variation during development. Its subject is manufactured products and the processes that make them, and the parameter cycling this Implementation asks of simulation and digital twin training applies that principle to a training regime.
  • Research Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks, by R. Geirhos, J.-H. Jacobsen, C. Michaelis, R. Zemel, W. Brendel, M. Bethge and F. A. Wichmann, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2020 (preprint version held). Section 7, Beyond shortcut learning, describes domain randomization in robotics, setting certain simulation parameters randomly during training, as an approach for learning policies that generalize to similar real-world situations. This bears on the parameter cycling in this Implementation, which bounds that randomization by specification limits.
