Implementation 9.3.2: Implement Periodic Readiness Drills and Validated Skills Certifications for Critical Roles

Periodic drills and certification renewal help show whether the people an override depends on can still perform when it matters. For personnel in roles carrying the highest AI oversight responsibility, one-time training completion is partial evidence of ongoing competence. Whether to require periodic hands-on readiness drills, formal certification renewal or both, and for which roles, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Certification gives a checkable record and a fixed point for renewal, and also adds administrative cost and can become a box-ticking exercise if the assessment behind it is weak. Record which roles are covered and why. A highly reliable SI system can call for more drill investment than a less reliable one, because personnel meet fewer real interventions to learn from (see Bainbridge 1983). Drills can present low-probability events known to the trainer but not to the trainee, and should build general problem-solving strategies as well as rehearsed responses. Drill performance is better evidence of readiness than self-reported confidence. In one laboratory study, participants who had monitored an automated decision aid understood the situation less well after it failed, yet were more confident in their decisions than those who had worked manually (see Endsley and Kiris 1995). Where a sector already requires operator certification, the SI-specific elements can be added to it. Drills of operation without the SI system are covered by Implementation 3.6.2: Scheduled “Manual Control” Drills. The renewal interval is for the organization to set, within any regulatory or contractual obligations; the consequence of the role and how quickly the relevant skills fade without use both bear on it. A significant change to the SI system or its operating context can make earlier certification stale, so whether such a change also triggers renewal is a further choice to make and record.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which roles require readiness drills, certification renewal or both?
  • What renewal interval applies to each role, and does a significant change to the SI system trigger renewal?

References:

  • Standard ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288:2023, Systems and software engineering: system life cycle processes, published jointly by ISO, IEC and IEEE. Clause 6.4.12, the operation process, includes defining training and qualification requirements to sustain the operating workforce and assigning trained, qualified operators. Its Note 6 observes that some environments require certification of operators by regulation and others do not. This is the variation the certification element of this Implementation meets.
  • Research Bainbridge, L., Ironies of Automation, Automatica volume 19 number 6, 1983, pages 775 to 779. Automatica is the journal of the International Federation of Automatic Control, an international body whose members are national control engineering organizations. A seminal paper on the human factors of automated process control. Its subject is the process-control computers of 1983, and the mechanisms it identifies are relevant to SI systems. Section 2.3 notes that unknown faults cannot be simulated, so training must address general strategies, and that simulations can give experience with low-probability events known to the trainer but not to the trainee. It closes by observing that the most successful automated systems may need the greatest investment in operator training.
  • Research Endsley, M. R. and Kiris, E. O., The Out-of-the-Loop Performance Problem and Level of Control in Automation, Human Factors volume 37 number 2, 1995, pages 381 to 394, from the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society. A laboratory study of 80 undergraduate students making navigation decisions with an expert system. After the expert system failed, those who had worked under moderate or high automation were slower to decide manually and less aware of what had been happening. Those who had monitored the expert system were more confident in their decisions than those who had worked manually. It bears on this Implementation’s preference for demonstrated drill performance over self-reported confidence.
