Implementation 9.1.1: Identify Required SI Literacy and Risk Awareness Levels for Existing Operational Roles

For each operational role that interacts with, supervises, or can override an AI system, define the specific AI literacy and risk awareness that role requires, distinguishing between what a front-line operator needs to know and what an executive decision-maker needs to know. The level a role requires depends on what the role does with the SI system and the consequence of an error in doing it, not on the role’s seniority. A control room operator, for example, may need a deeper working understanding of an SI system than a senior manager does. For many operational roles, the requirement is likely to be AI literacy rather than advanced AI skills: the ability to use and interact with the SI system and to evaluate its outputs critically. One element of that literacy is knowing which kinds of problem the SI system handles well and which it handles poorly, so that personnel can judge when to rely on it and when to apply their own skills (see Long and Magerko 2020). Revisit these requirements when the SI system, its role or its operating context changes. For example, in manufacturing, a machine operator’s work can shift from placing parts by hand toward supervising robots, calibrating them and troubleshooting their errors.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Does each role need AI literacy or deeper skills, given what it does with the SI system and the consequence of an error?
  • Which changes to the SI system, the role or the operating context trigger a review of a role’s requirements?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control AT-3, Role-Based Training, determines training content from assigned roles and responsibilities and the systems to which personnel have access, lists roles ranging from senior leaders and system owners to administrators and personnel performing verification and validation, and provides for training when system changes require it, which is the structure this Implementation applies to AI literacy and risk awareness for roles that use, supervise or can override an AI system.
  • Research Skills in the AI age, OECD Artificial Intelligence Papers No. 60, 2026, from the OECD, an output of the OECD-Korea Digital Society Initiative under the Digital Policy Committee. Section 4, under Promoting AI literacy, defines AI literacy as competences to critically evaluate AI, communicate and collaborate with it and use it as a tool. It states that most workers will only need literacy, and that training for literacy is as important as training in advanced AI skills. The paper addresses labor-market policy. It bears on the level this Implementation sets for roles that use SI systems but do not build them.
  • Research Long, D. and Magerko, B., What is AI Literacy? Competencies and Design Considerations, Proceedings of the 2020 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, April 2020, from ACM. A review of 150 documents across disciplines, and the source of the definition the OECD paper uses. Its audience is learners without technical backgrounds. Competency 5, AI’s Strengths and Weaknesses, asks learners to identify problem types that AI excels at and those that are more challenging for AI, and to decide from this when to leverage human skills. It bears on the content of the literacy this Implementation defines for each role.
  • Working Document Preliminary Report, October 2026, from the National Security Commission on Robotics for Advanced Manufacturing, an initiative of the Special Competitive Studies Project. Under Finding 4, the discussion of job transformation notes that a machine operator’s daily responsibilities frequently shift from manual part placement toward robot supervision, system calibration and error troubleshooting, and that these redesigned roles demand different analytical capabilities. It bears on revisiting role requirements under this Implementation when a deployment changes the role.
  • Regulation Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, 13 June 2024, from the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union. Article 4, AI literacy, requires providers and deployers to take measures, to their best extent, toward a sufficient level of AI literacy of staff dealing with the operation and use of AI systems, taking into account their knowledge, experience, education and training and the context of use. Binding only within its scope, it bears on setting literacy by role and context here.
