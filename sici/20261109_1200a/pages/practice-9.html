Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust

Establish a systematic program for SI risk management training tailored to specific operational roles, accountability requirements, and decision-making responsibilities.

This Practice helps ensure that stakeholders across the organization possess the knowledge, matched to their role, necessary to identify SI risks and to trust SI systems neither more nor less than they warrant. The people who supervise SI systems are also the safety net when those systems fail. Over-trust leads personnel to accept outputs they should have checked. Under-trust leads them to ignore or work around systems that are performing well. Both forms of miscalibrated trust grow from inaccurate mental models of how a particular SI system behaves and fails, and both increase risk for the organization. For example, over-trust can lead personnel to act on improperly vetted and potentially incorrect SI system outputs, and impairs oversight. Similarly, under-trust can lead personnel to override correct SI actions with incorrect manual ones, and can reduce capability, particularly where the SI system was intended to contribute to overall risk management, such as anomaly and intrusion detection.

What each role needs differs: an operator who may have to take over needs practice in recognizing failure and in manual control, while leadership needs enough understanding to judge what assurances are worth and the trade-offs involved (Task 9.1: Perform Role-Based SI Risk Competency Mapping). Training covers automation bias and the other human factors of working with SI systems (Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias), and its effectiveness is checked through assessments, simulations and drills rather than assumed from attendance (Task 9.3: Establish Audit-Ready Systems for Verifying Workforce Readiness). It complements Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation, which addresses the erosion of crucial skills and attention when automated tasks that were previously performed manually still require human oversight and fallback. Organizations with established training, qualification, and exercise programs, such as for operational technology or cybersecurity, can build on them rather than start a separate program.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Safe: Contributes training of people who can recognize when an SI system’s output should not be acted on and who can carry out an override in time, so that the intervention path the system depends on works in practice and not only on paper. This Practice does not cover the override mechanisms and procedures themselves, which Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes roles whose SI-related responsibilities are matched by defined and verified competence, with records showing who has been trained and assessed for what. This Practice does not cover assigning those responsibilities or the authority to decide, which Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior sets out.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Accountable: A record that personnel were trained can inadvertently place a persistent failure on an individual when its cause is structural. Implementation 9.2.3: Recognizing Disincentives to Reporting SI Issues notes that training alone does not resolve under-reporting where disclosure is individually costly, and that persistent under-reporting is evidence of a structural rather than a training deficiency; Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses sets out the structural reporting measures.
  • Secure: Training material that explains how an SI system’s guardrails work, where they are weak, and which failures personnel are drilled to catch also shows which weaknesses to aim for and which failures are least likely to be noticed. An insider, or an outsider who obtains the material, could use it to get around the guardrails. Limiting that detail to the roles that need it can reduce the exposure. Protecting the material is otherwise a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • GOVERN 2.2 (on training for SI risk management): This Practice covers training matched to each role that uses, supervises, or can override an SI system, or creates the data it consumes, with readiness verified rather than assumed; it does not address training for the organization’s partners, which GOVERN 2.2 also names.
  • MAP 3.4 (on defining operator proficiency): This Practice covers the definition and assessment of operator proficiency with SI system behavior and limitations, including drills and certification renewal for the most critical roles; it does not cover retaining the manual skills that routine SI reliance erodes. For that, see for example Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation.
  • GOVERN 4.1 (on establishing a risk-aware culture): This Practice covers training that cultivates critical review of SI outputs and recognition of disincentives to reporting SI degradation; it does not cover establishing reporting structures that keep disclosure from being individually costly, without which trained personnel may still under-report. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How will the organization tell whether personnel trust each SI system more than it warrants (over-trust) or less than it warrants (under-trust), rather than assuming training has corrected it?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 1270, Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence, March 2022, from NIST. Section 3.3.1 calls the perception that a human, expert or otherwise, can effectively and objectively oversee algorithmic decision systems a problematic assumption, notes that experts are not necessarily trained to govern the use of an SI system they played no role in developing, and describes automation complacency. It states that without significant procedural and cultural support, optimistic expectations of human oversight are not borne out in practice, which bears on this Practice as one part of that support.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-82r3, Guide to Operational Technology (OT) Security, September 2023, from NIST. Section 6.2.2, Awareness and Training, asks organizations to identify, document and train all personnel with significant OT roles and responsibilities, with training that covers the physical process being controlled as well as the OT system and is tailored to roles ranging from senior executives and third-party providers to control engineers, operators and maintainers. This is the existing OT training structure into which this Practice adds SI-specific competence by role.
  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Table 2 lists SI dependency, in which operators miss safety-related information the SI misses and lose manual skills, among the SI risks in an OT environment alongside cybersecurity and data quality, and maps it to Section 1.3, “Educate Personnel on AI.” That section adds skill gaps, in which personnel misinterpret SI outputs, and treats training as a mitigation of these risks rather than general awareness.
