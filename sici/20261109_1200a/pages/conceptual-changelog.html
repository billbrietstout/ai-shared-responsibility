Conceptual Changelog

This section summarizes key conceptual changes between draft versions, deliberately omitting minor edits. It is not an exhaustive diff, but a targeted summary to help reviewers identify substantive updates relevant to their feedback.

November 9, 2026

This version follows the Sep 28, 2026(a) draft, the last one shared with the community of interest. It brings in the changes made since then, including those in an Oct 15 draft shared with a small group of internal reviewers. No Practice, Task or Implementation has been removed, and apart from seven new Implementations, item numbers are unchanged, so comments keyed to earlier drafts still apply. Many titles have been reworded, as described below. This entry is written for readers of the Sep 13 and Sep 28 drafts, to point to what they may want to look at again.

The profile is now SICI, published as a set of documents. The SICI Summary is a four-page overview for leadership. The SICI Profile, of about thirty pages, gives a condensed introduction, the trustworthiness characteristics, each of the 13 Practices in full with the titles of its Tasks and Implementations, and a selection of scenarios. This document, the SICI Guidebook, contains everything in the Profile and adds the text of every Task and Implementation, the references, the Glossary and the appendices; the Profile and Guidebook differ only in level of detail. The Guidebook is published as a web edition, with a page for each Practice, Task and Implementation, and as a single Markdown file for LLM-based assistants, accompanied by a knowledge graph in YAML. It is no longer published as a PDF. The Introduction now describes these documents, and Section 1.4 and Appendix E explain how to use the web edition and the Markdown, with or without an assistant.

How far to go: measures as a matter of degree. A new section before the Practices, How Far to Go, says that most measures are a matter of degree, that how far to go is set by consequence weighed against finite resources, and that whatever dependency or gap remains is a decision, made within any regulatory or contractual obligations, accepted by someone with the authority to accept that level of risk, and recorded. Alternatives such as reverting or switching to a backup are available only to the extent they have been prepared. It introduces the design basis, the conditions a system is deliberately designed to withstand, with a new Glossary entry, and applies it where the text sized for an extreme (Implementation 4.4.3, Task 4.4, Practice 11, Task 11.2, Implementation 11.2.2). Appendix A, A.3.1, relates Reliable, Robust, and Resilient to the design basis.

Two new Implementations on records. Implementation 10.1.5 asks what each operational record is for, who uses it and when, and how much to keep, including coarser or statistical recording where its limits are understood and accepted, and records that span several organizations. Implementation 3.9.6 asks for a record of design decisions and accepted risks. Many items that ask for something to be recorded now point to one of them.

Trustworthiness Effects to Consider is now Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider, in the sense of the side effects listed for a medicine: something to know before going ahead. The field description in Section 2 explains that a side effect can become a tension where the measure that would address it cannot be resourced. Handoffs (“This Task does not cover… For that, see…”) are now described as examples of where a limit is taken up, not the only place, and lists of triggers and conditions as illustrative, with a named role deciding whether other changes warrant action.

“One resolution is” is now “One way to balance this is” in the Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance fields, and some tensions now say that an option is available only where it has been prepared.

Responsibility and roles. Section 1 adds a paragraph on how SI systems challenge the way organizations divide responsibility. Appendix A, A.1.11, and several Tasks now say that whoever holds a responsibility needs the visibility, time, resources, access, support, and authority to act on it, and where separation of roles cannot be achieved, for example in a small organization or a field office, the text asks that this and how it is offset be recorded.

Every Practice now has a Decisions for the Organization field, and many items have new Decisions questions on ownership, resourcing, and what happens when the preferred option is unavailable.

Titles changed: Implementation 2.2.1 (Maintain Backup Systems Proportionate to Consequence), Implementation 8.3.2 (Form an SI Incident Governance Team), Implementation 11.2.2 (Develop a Validated “Safe Operating Mode” that Depends Minimally on SI Infrastructure), and Task 13.2 (Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result). “Table 2” is now referred to as the Trustworthiness Characteristics table.

Field labels now in title case: Decisions for the Organization, and Related AI RMF Subcategories Include, matching the other field labels.

The profile now uses “Super Intelligence” (SI) in place of “Artificial Intelligence” (AI), following Executive Order 14434. A footnote on the title explains that “SI” refers to the same systems defined as Artificial Intelligence in 15 U.S.C. § 9401, and that cited works, proper names, quotations, identifiers, established terms of art and keyword lists keep “AI”. The update is still in progress, so both terms appear. The working title is now Automated, Agentic, and Super Intelligence in Critical Infrastructure and High-Consequence Applications, and the Scope explains why automated and agentic systems are named in it. Following the change, the AI Bill of Materials is now the SI Bill of Materials (SIBOM) in Implementation 6.1.2, while AIBOM remains where a cited source uses it.

The Introduction and Scope have been rewritten. They now state that the profile is voluntary and is not a compliance checklist; that SI systems benefit from the same controls as other software, even on purpose-built hardware, while many of the assumptions behind those controls need to be adjusted; and that operational trust rests on both governance evidence, which shows that processes exist, and technical evidence, which shows whether a particular SI system can meet a function’s requirements. The Scope now notes that many organizations already use SI systems, so that an inventory is a natural first step, and that risks can compound where newer SI systems are layered on older statistical and optimization systems. Section 1.2 explains why SI in critical infrastructure needs particular attention, applies the principle behind zero trust architecture to SI risk more generally, and distinguishes the SI Attack Surface from the wider SI Risk Surface.

A new field, Decisions for the Organization, appears in 218 Practices, Tasks and Implementations. It lists the choices an item leaves to the organization, such as which events trigger re-validation and who decides the response. Each Practice and Task also now says what it does not cover and where that is addressed (“This Task does not…”), and where an item leaves a matter for the organization to decide, it notes that the decision is made within any regulatory or contractual obligations.

The trustworthiness and AI RMF fields now cover every Practice and Task. Practices 7 to 12 and their Tasks now carry the Main Trustworthiness Contributions, Trustworthiness Effects to Consider, Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance and Related AI RMF Subcategories fields in the form previously used for Practices 1 to 6 and 13.

References have been substantially expanded, and now point to specific sections. Section 2 now has about 640 reference entries across 248 of its 258 items, compared with 91 under 43 items in the Sep 28 draft. Each entry names the part of the source that applies and why, and opens with a label for the kind of document. Sources that present themselves as unfinished, such as drafts, requests for comments and concept papers, are labeled Working Document and cited deliberately, to record where practice is heading. Section 1.5 lists the sources for Section 1, and Appendix F: Consolidated References lists the 163 sources cited in Sections 1 and 2 once each, with where each is cited.

The Glossary has been rebuilt, from seven terms to 66. Each entry gives a definition and, where relevant, other names for the term, terms it should be contrasted with, and senses in other fields or sectors that could cause a misreading, together with the items where it is used and references. Entries that reviewers may wish to review include AI agent, agentic AI system, super intelligence, action-level authorization, attack surface and risk surface, drift, safety wrapper and interlock, protective AI capability, fail-safe state, known-safe, root cause analysis, and prompt compromise.

All six appendices now have content.

  • Appendix A: Trustworthiness Characteristics is new. It gives the source definition of each characteristic and where this profile departs from it, how the grouping relates to the AI RMF’s, distinctions between characteristics that readers often merge, the tensions that recur across this Guidebook, and what the characteristics do not reach.
  • Appendix B is now Scenario-to-Practice Mapping. Its 33 scenarios are illustrative composites inspired by historical incidents, not descriptions or assessments of any particular incident or organization, and each names the trustworthiness characteristics most clearly at stake.
  • Appendix C: Use Cases and Operational Scenarios sets out a common form for use cases and adds the first, C.1 Healthcare: Authorized Access versus Authorized Action, contributed by a member of the community of interest.
  • Appendix D begins with D.1, which lists common control areas, the assumption each typically makes, how SI systems can break it, and where this profile can help, with access reviews worked through as an example. Crosswalks to external frameworks remain under development.
  • Appendix E: Suggested Ways to Use this Document with an LLM-Based Assistant covers what to give the assistant, nine common uses with example prompts, including evaluating a project against the trustworthiness characteristics, the ways an assistant can misrepresent this document, and how to check an answer. It stresses that the prompts are illustrative, and that answers should be checked against this profile and the sources it cites.
  • Appendix F: Consolidated References is described above. The reviewer material that was Appendix E in the Sep 13 and Sep 28 drafts is now the Additional Information for the Reviewer section at the end of the document.

Five Implementations were added before this round. Implementation 3.6.4 suggests monitoring aggregate indicators of engagement at review and approval points. Implementation 4.6.4 describes distilling a trained opaque model into a self-interpretable model that can be verified and deployed in its place. Implementation 5.3.4 extends least agency to the context an agentic SI system carries from one step, session or delegated agent to the next. Implementation 7.2.4 treats the selection and preparation of an SI system’s inputs, at training and at runtime, as a risk control. Implementation 10.2.2 asks the organization to consider how strongly logs need to be protected from later change where they may serve as evidence.

Several additions address how separate controls on the same action fit together. Task 3.2 notes that identity, per-action authorization and decision-level records act on the same paths as its guardrails, and suggests checking these controls together for each path. Task 5.1 states that an identity establishes which entity is acting, not what it is permitted to do. Implementation 3.2.2 adds that each constraint should hold without reference to values the SI system generates about its own output, and that a safety wrapper on a physical process does not gate commands from protective functions. Implementation 10.1.2 asks that records of what action was taken, and whether it was permitted, be generated where possible by the enforcement or validation layer rather than reported by the SI system about itself.

Logging no longer assumes immutability. Task 10.2 and Implementations 10.1.2 and 10.2.2 now ask that logs be protected from alteration in proportion to how they will be used, particularly where they may serve as evidence, and Implementation 10.2.1 now asks whether existing logging facilities can absorb SI logging without crowding out other logs.

Other content changes carried from the Oct 15 draft include the SI Risk Surface now covering the SI Attack Surface (Task 3.8, Task 8.1, Implementation 8.1.6); protection for those who report near-misses (Task 8.6, Implementation 8.6.2); treating system prompts as sensitive and SIBOMs for internally developed systems (Task 7.3, Implementation 7.1.3); more consistent terms following the Glossary, such as oversight modes rather than levels of autonomy, operating envelope, and “independent deterministic” in place of “non-AI”; and a reading path for auditors, assessors and compliance functions in Section 1.4.

Titles have been put in title case and many reworded, including for the change to SI. Rewordings that change meaning include Task 5.4 (Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations), Implementation 3.6.3 (Cognitive Engagement by Design), Implementation 4.4.2 (Adapting Existing Alarm Management for Complex, Adaptive or Otherwise Unpredictable Systems), Implementation 7.5.2 (Perform Automated “Champion-Challenger” Assessment), Implementation 8.6.4 (Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning), Task 7.2 (Validate, Select, and Prepare Data for SI Systems), and the Practice 10 Task and Implementations above.

Corrections. Implementation 8.6.4 now points to Implementation 8.6.2, rather than to a cell that did not exist. Implementation 3.4.3 now says which controls it monitors. Several references to NIST publications that overstated what the source requires now say what it calls for, and a scope limitation attributed to this profile in Practice 1 now correctly belongs to SP 800-218A.

Version 20261109_1200a differs from 20261109_1200 only in minor presentation changes to the web edition and two notices in the front matter: that feedback shared with NIST will become part of the public record, and that the views expressed do not necessarily reflect those of NIST or the United States Government. The content is otherwise unchanged.

Earlier entries

Sep 28, 2026(a)

This is a minor update to the Sep 13 draft. It adds one Task, six Implementations and a section on using the profile, and otherwise clarifies and corrects existing text. No Practice, Task or Implementation has been removed.

Three new Implementations address how operating context and source data can change without any change to the AI system. Implementation 1.1.3: Establish Triggers for Re-Validating Requirements on Change of Operating Context covers assets whose applicable rules change when they relocate or their contract status changes, such as a mobile asset entering a different jurisdiction, and Task 7.5: Conduct Impact Assessments and Risk Management for SI System and Model Updates now also applies where such a change alters rules encoded in the AI system, such as in its prompts, retrieval corpus, or guardrails. Implementation 7.2.3: Assess the Conditions Under Which Source Data Is Created extends provenance upstream of ingestion to the point of capture, where records can be lost in manual transfer chains, overwritten, or degraded by migration between maintenance management systems. Implementation 9.1.3: Include Data-Originating Roles in Competency Mapping brings field engineers, maintenance technicians and others who create source data into role-based training, even where they never use an AI system directly.

A new Section 1.4, Using this Profile, suggests where different readers might start. It gathers the guidance on non-linear reading and on starting from Appendix B that was previously spread through the Introduction, adds starting points for technical staff, for those focused on a trustworthiness characteristic, for those mapping to other frameworks and for organizations with limited resources, and discusses using the document with an AI assistant, including the organizational context an assistant needs and the ways it can misrepresent the document.

The profile now states how it relates to existing assurance regimes and to zero trust. The Scope now says that existing assurance regimes, including authorization and continuous monitoring, can remain the foundation, and that this profile builds on them with attention to what should trigger reassessment and to monitoring what an AI system does, not only how it is configured. It also describes the profile as applying the zero trust principle, that trust is verified at each boundary rather than assumed, to AI risk more generally, including to a legitimate, uncompromised AI system that is not behaving as intended. This is carried into Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior, Task 3.2: Implement Independent Guardrails for SI Outputs and Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools, and zero trust is added to the Glossary.

Language incorrectly implying guarantees removed. Throughout the table, statements that an item “ensures” an outcome now say that it “helps ensure” it, and similar claims elsewhere, such as guardrails that structurally block unsafe commands even from a fully compromised AI system, have been restated as what the item can do.

Terms used in their general sense do not carry sector-specific meanings. The introduction to the table now states that where a sector, regulation or standard gives a term such as validation, verification, incident or safety function a precise meaning, this profile does not invoke that meaning, and following the profile does not by itself establish conformance with requirements stated in those terms. The Glossary adds industrial control system, noting that ICS means something different in emergency management. Table 1 now cites National Security Memorandum 22 as the source of the 16 sectors.

Explanation features are no longer treated as evidence that a recommendation is correct. A new Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output gathers the requirements on confidence and rationale shown to personnel, previously in Implementation 4.2.3: Human-Centered Interfaces for SI Uncertainty and Actionable Triggers, and adds guidance for symbolic, hybrid and self-interpretable AI systems, whose rationale can be a trace of the steps actually applied. Implementation 4.6.1: Design Rationale Displays that Give Personnel Something to Check notes research finding that showing explanations alongside recommendations can increase acceptance whether or not the recommendation is correct, and suggests testing whether a rationale display improves decisions for the personnel who will use it, including whether they still reject incorrect recommendations. Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias adds training on this effect. Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output also carries a new tension between the Accurate and Explainable & Interpretable characteristics, and Implementation 10.1.2: Runtime Telemetry and Artifact Tracking notes that the inference receipt can record the rules applied, which provides the displayed trace. Implementation 4.2.3: Human-Centered Interfaces for SI Uncertainty and Actionable Triggers, previously titled Human-Centered Interfaces for AI Uncertainty, Rationale, and Actionable Triggers, now addresses uncertainty and triage cues, and its Trust/Execute cue is renamed Proceed, which indicates that an output falls within the conditions the AI system was validated for rather than that it is correct.

A refused action part-way through a sequence needs its own fail-safe state. Implementation 3.2.3: Specify Fail-Safe States and Fallback Behavior now covers the case where an AI action is refused under Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies after earlier steps have already executed, with a worked example of a pump changeover.

Vendor hazard reachability attestations should be corroborated before they suppress alarms. Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures now says vendor self-attestation should not be treated as sufficient to suppress a warning, and Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation applies this to alarm suppression. Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures also notes that the existing VEX justification for inline mitigations covers only protections built into the product, so controls that an operator deploys around an AI system would need to be stated as a separate justification.

Retiring a protective AI capability is a change to controls as well as an asset disposal. Implementation 13.3.2: Treat the Retirement of a Protective SI Capability as a Change to Controls and Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System previously framed these as alternatives.

Some statements now cite their source in the text. Where a sentence rests on a specific study or document, the source is now named in the form “(see Author Year)” or “(see ID Year)”, for example in Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation, Implementation 4.5.1: Define Engineering Time-Constant Thresholds, Implementation 4.6.1: Design Rationale Displays that Give Personnel Something to Check, Implementation 6.1.2: Require SIBOM for Mission-Critical Applications and Implementation 6.1.5: Operational SI Hazard Reachability Disclosures.

Corrections. The example in Implementation 2.1.2: Develop Process-Informed Guardrails had the operating conditions reversed; guardrails now allow more headroom during quieter periods and tighter control during rapid change or heightened activity. Several pointers to where an adjacent concern is handled now point to a more specific item, most notably from Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability and Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools to Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result rather than Task 7.6: Establish Processes for Internal SI Component Deprecation and Decommissioning, and from Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority to Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations and Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers rather than Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations.

Other clarifications. Table 2 relates the Reliable, Robust and Resilient characteristics to the terms of a typical service-level agreement, and revises the Bias Managed and Fair entries. Implementation 2.2.2: Deploy “Watchdog” Controllers notes that a watchdog may itself be part of the attack surface. The References field description notes that citation descriptions are meant to help locate successors to revised or superseded documents. Search Keywords were added in Practices 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations, Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior, Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness, Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools and Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures, and reviewer notes were added on the crosswalks, the glossary, and record retention for retired systems.

Sep 13, 2026

This entry is organized by what changed rather than by where, because most of what follows was answered in more than one place. Feedback on the August draft was often combined with related input and addressed somewhere other than where it was raised, sometimes at a different level of the table, and sometimes through several smaller changes that only add up when read together. If you are looking for whether something you raised was acted on, you may find it more quickly by scanning the themes below than by returning to the cell you commented on.

We have indicated where changes apply substantially to only part of the document. No Practice, Task or Implementation has been removed; everything present in the August draft is still there.

A thirteenth Practice covers retiring AI systems. Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority treats the permanent removal of an AI capability as its own problem rather than as an extreme form of updating one: an update transfers trust from one version to the next, while retirement withdraws authority, removes a capability other things have come to rely on, and destroys operational knowledge that may exist nowhere else. Task 13.1: Prepare for Retirement While the System Is Still Running covers what has to be prepared while the system still runs, because retirement can be imposed by a withdrawn vendor or a deprecated model and can arrive after the system has stopped working. Task 13.2: Withdraw All Authority and Confirm the Result covers withdrawing authority completely and confirming it. Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System covers preserving evidence that teardown would destroy and recording what happens to everything that depended on the system. The existing treatment of internal AI artifacts, in Task 7.6: Establish Processes for Internal SI Component Deprecation and Decommissioning, is unchanged; Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority addresses the operating capability and its authority rather than the models, datasets and pipelines themselves.

Placeholder Implementations are now written, most heavily in Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools and Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning. Thirty-two Implementations that carried a single line of intent in the August draft now carry actual guidance. Nine of them are in Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools, covering AI agent identity, access mapping, action-level authorization, permission scope, and logging and anomaly detection for agent activity. Nine more are in Practice 11: Maintain SI-Aware Mission Continuity and Disaster Recovery Planning, covering process ranking, dependency mapping, manual fallback, safe operating mode, standby personnel and infrastructure, re-engagement validation, and fail-to-manual exercises. If you passed over either Practice in August because there was little to review, they are worth a second look.

Organizational readiness is now something to assess and record before adopting an AI capability. New Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability, with Implementation 3.9.1: Establish Accountable Ownership, Implementation 3.9.2: Define Decision Authority and Reassessment Triggers, Implementation 3.9.3: Establish Escalation to Empowered Authority, Implementation 3.9.4: Assess Operational Capability, and Implementation 3.9.5: Demonstrate Operational Enforceability covering accountable ownership, decision authority and reassessment triggers, escalation to an empowered authority, operational capability, and demonstrated enforceability. Related material sits in Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation on maintaining human capability and in Implementation 9.2.1: Include Role-Specific Training on SI Failure Modes and Implementation 9.2.2: Implement Realistic Incident Simulation Training on role-specific training.

Near-misses can now be reported without the reporter bearing the cost. New Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses, with Implementation 8.6.1: Separate Benefit-Claiming from Risk-Reporting on separating benefit-claiming from risk-reporting, Implementation 8.6.2: Protect Reporter Identity Through an Independent Reporting Route on protecting reporter identity through an independent route, Implementation 8.6.3: Ensure Supplier Terms Do Not Penalize Degradation Disclosure on supplier terms that penalize disclosure, and Implementation 8.6.4: Share De-Identified Incident Records for Collective Learning on sharing de-identified records. Implementation 9.2.3: Recognizing Disincentives to Reporting SI Issues addresses recognizing the disincentives themselves.

External AI components are now tracked from procurement through end of life. Implementation 6.1.1: Add SI Disclosure to Existing Procurement Policies on AI disclosure in procurement policy and Implementation 6.1.2: Require SIBOM for Mission-Critical Applications on requiring an AI Bill of Materials, and Implementation 6.2.1: Register Externally Sourced SI Components in Asset Management Systems, Implementation 6.2.2: Track Vendor Updates and Third-Party Entity Changes Through Change Management Process, and Implementation 6.2.3: Manage End-of-Life Transitions for Externally Sourced SI Components on registering externally sourced components, tracking vendor updates and third-party changes through change management, and managing end-of-life transitions.

An AI entity’s authority can be bounded, delegated and withdrawn. Implementation 5.3.2: Task-Bounded and Revocable Agent Authority became Task-Bounded and Revocable Agent Authority, Implementation 5.2.2: Action-Level, Context-Aware Authorization Policies became Action-level, context-aware authorization policies, and new Implementation 5.3.3: Separate Privilege via Encapsulated Downstream Agents and Tools covers separating privilege through encapsulated downstream agents and tools.

Constraints on AI behavior are now required to be enforced independently of the AI system. Implementation 3.2.2: Define and Independently Enforce Deterministic Operational Constraints became “Define and independently enforce deterministic operational constraints”, and Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle became “Independently verify AI-generated operational logic and artifacts across their lifecycle”. Both titles changed to carry that point rather than imply it.

In Practices 1 to 6, each Practice and Task now states what it does not cover and where the adjacent concern is handled. This is the combined effect of three rebuilt fields: the AI RMF mappings now say what an item contributes and what it does not; the trustworthiness entries state the limits of each contribution; and reference descriptions name the part of a source that applies and where it stops. Practices 7 to 12 have not yet had this work and carry markers instead; Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority was written on the new scheme from the start.

The AI RMF mapping field was replaced rather than renamed. “Primary AI RMF Mappings” is now “Related AI RMF subcategories”, and the change is one of meaning: listing a subcategory indicates that an item can contribute toward that outcome, not that following it satisfies the subcategory. If you commented on the previous field, the basis of that comment has changed.

References now say which part of a source applies and why it is cited here. Each entry opens with a label for the kind of document, and the description names the clause, section or control that bears on the item. This has been done for Practices 1 to 6 and 13; Practices 7 to 12 retain their earlier reference lists.

Cross-references now carry the title of what they point to. Every internal reference in the table is a link showing the name of the target Practice, Task or Implementation, rather than an item number alone.

Human oversight has a defined vocabulary. The Glossary and Terminology Crosswalk now defines human oversight together with oversight mode, oversight capacity, oversight threshold and oversight authority, and these terms are used consistently through the table.

An appendix is reserved for crosswalks to external frameworks. Appendix D is now Crosswalks, and is a placeholder under development. The reviewer material that was Appendix D in the August draft became Appendix E (it is now in its own separate section at the end of the document). If you have notes referring to “Appendix D”, check which one you meant.

The profile states that it covers high-consequence applications beyond critical infrastructure. This was implicit in the August draft and is now stated in the title and carried through the Introduction and Scope.

Search Keywords updated across the document. These have been chosen to both assist readers (and their AI assistants) in locating relevant Practices and Tasks in this document, as well as to provide candidate keywords for readers to use when searching other resources.

3. Key Conceptual Revisions to Existing Controls

  • TEVV Production Guidance (Implementation 1.3.1: Define TEVV Scope and Requirements per SI System & Implementation 1.3.2: Categorization of Testing Environments): Advises that high-risk testing, fuzzing, or red-teaming should be performed on isolated staging, HIL test rigs, digital twins, or during scheduled downtime windows, rather than on live systems.
  • Process-Informed Rate Limiting (Implementation 2.1.1: Implement Baseline Rate Limiting): Clarified that rate limits should be dynamic and process-informed (Implementation 2.1.2: Develop Process-Informed Guardrails) rather than fixed ceilings, so that valid high-volume commands are less likely to be suppressed during abnormal process upsets.
  • Time-to-Divergence Cadence (Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations): Introduced quantitative Time-to-Divergence baselining during staging to dictate model risk evaluation schedules as a fraction of estimated drift velocity.
  • Consequence-Driven Risk Thresholds (Implementation 3.1.3: Define Acceptable Risk Thresholds and Associated Control Responses): Warns against over-indexing on CVSS or raw confidence scores, directing organizations to consequence-driven frameworks like SSVC.
  • Non-Human Identity (NHI) Enforcement (Implementation 5.1.1: Audit for and Replace Shared Credentials, Service Accounts, Secrets, Passwords, and API Keys): Specifies distinct cryptographic identities per sub-agent/tool invocation and warns against sharing enterprise API keys or master service accounts across agent nodes.
  • Compensating Controls for Silent Updates (Implementation 6.1.1: Add SI Disclosure to Existing Procurement Policies & Implementation 6.1.2: Require SIBOM for Mission-Critical Applications): Added alternative controls (perimeter staging, egress gates, safety wrapper tightening) when suspending automated vendor updates is unfeasible, and reframed AIBOM compliance as a flexible gradient.
  • Logical Policy Locks (Implementation 7.3.3: Freeze Adaptation of Online-Learning SI Systems During Operational Windows): Specifies automated “Policy Locks” to programmatically freeze model weights, hyperparameters, and boundary logic for adaptive/online-learning models during operational windows.
  • Substrate Substitution Triage (Implementation 7.5.4: Audit Interoperability for Hardware or Data Substrate Substitution): Expanded component replacement change-management logic into a 3-tier triage approach (unrelated vs. non-critical vs. safety-critical paths requiring full regression).
  • Forensic Interpretability Gap (Implementation 8.1.2: Identify SI Systems that May Not Satisfy Root Cause Analysis Requirements): Added new citations and warnings emphasizing that post-hoc explanations (SHAP/LIME) are ungrounded “reconstructed narratives” and should not satisfy forensic requirements.
