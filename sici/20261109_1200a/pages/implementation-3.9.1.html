Implementation 3.9.1: Establish Accountable Ownership

A named owner and deputy help make sure that someone can decide about an SI-enabled capability when a decision is needed. Identify an accountable owner for the SI-enabled capability and for the governance decisions about it. Document who is responsible for these decisions across the SI lifecycle. This includes deciding whether the capability may be deployed, may continue operating, or may operate under modified conditions. Transfer ownership explicitly when the owner changes role, or when the capability passes from a project team to operations. The owner needs the visibility, time, resources, access, support, and authority to make these decisions, not only the title. Implementation 3.9.3 and Implementation 3.9.4 test whether these are in place.

Name a deputy who can act for the owner when the owner is unavailable. Some frameworks and obligations, such as SP 800-37, limit which actions can be delegated. Decide in advance which actions the deputy may take. Review this when the system’s environment or tasks change, especially before a period of higher risk. Stopping or restricting the capability may reduce some risks while raising others (see Practice 4: Define Procedures for Emergency Avoidance Override, Recovery, and Situational Awareness). Record the actions that are delegated, in which circumstances, and why.

A capability might lose its owner, for example when the owner leaves and no successor has been named. The organization decides whether the capability may keep operating during such a gap, and under what interim arrangement, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Continued operation avoids disrupting the function the capability supports. Operation without an owner leaves no one to make the decisions this Implementation assigns. Record the interim arrangement and how long it may last.

Where responsibilities span several organizational functions, identify the role accountable for resolving conflicting recommendations or decisions. Where one person holds several roles, check that this creates no conflict of interest. Some frameworks and obligations, such as SP 800-37, limit which roles one person may hold. One example is whether the person who approves continued operation of the capability may also own the system. Where few qualified people are available, for example in a small organization, a field office, or a niche application, one person may be the only one able to hold both roles. In that case, record this and how the conflict is offset, such as by an independent review (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks).

Where a vendor supplies the capability, the accountable owner is still within the organization. The organization decides where the leader of the operational function that the capability serves sits in this structure, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. That leader bears the operational consequences and often knows the process best. That leader also feels the production pressure to keep the capability running. Record where the leader sits and why.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Who is the accountable owner, and who is the deputy?
  • Which actions may the deputy take, and in which circumstances?
  • Where does the leader of the operational function the capability serves sit in this structure?
  • Where one person holds roles that conflict, how is the conflict offset?
  • Can the capability keep operating during a gap in ownership, and for how long?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-37r2, Risk Management Framework for Information Systems and Organizations: A System Life Cycle Approach for Security and Privacy, from NIST. Appendix D, Roles and Responsibilities, describes the authorizing official as a senior official with the authority to formally assume responsibility and accountability for operating a system, and as the only official who can accept the associated security and privacy risk. The authorizing official is the closest RMF counterpart to the accountable owner in this Implementation; the RMF system owner is a different role. Appendix D also limits what the authorizing official may delegate to a designated representative. Task P-1, Risk Management Roles, addresses conflicts of interest when one individual holds several roles, including whether an authorizing official may also own the system being authorized.
  • Guidance Model risk management principles for banks, Supervisory Statement SS1/23, updated 16 April 2026, from the Prudential Regulation Authority of the Bank of England. It is written for UK banks that use internal models for regulatory capital. Principle 2.4, Roles and responsibilities, asks firms to document roles for each stage of the model lifecycle, with the organizational independence and seniority each requires, and to document model owners for all models. Principle 2.6 keeps senior management responsible for model risk when a model comes from a third party. These bear on documenting ownership across the lifecycle, including for supplied capabilities.
