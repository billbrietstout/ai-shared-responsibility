Appendix A: Trustworthiness Characteristics

This appendix helps readers apply the Trustworthiness Characteristics consistently, and recognize where they pull against each other. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table introduces the 13 Trustworthiness Characteristics that this profile works with, and why each matters in critical infrastructure. This appendix adds the source definition of each characteristic and where this profile departs from it (A.1), how the grouping relates to the AI RMF’s own (A.2), distinctions between characteristics that readers often merge (A.3), the tensions between characteristics that recur across this Guidebook (A.4), and what the characteristics do not reach (A.5).

The terms and their definitions are drawn from the NIST AI RMF (1.0), which in turn draws several of them from ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022. The grouping, the emphasis placed on each characteristic, and the glosses in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table are this profile’s. Quotations below keep the source’s wording, including “AI”.

To the Reviewer:
This appendix is new in this draft. We would particularly welcome comment on the departures from the source definitions in A.1, on whether the distinctions in A.3 help or add confusion, and on tensions you have had to balance in practice that A.4 does not describe.

A.1 Source Definitions

Each entry below gives the definition the characteristic starts from and, where this profile uses the term more narrowly, more widely or with a different emphasis, what changes and why. Unless otherwise noted, the source is Section 3 of the AI RMF.

A.1.1 Valid

Source definition: Validation is the “confirmation, through the provision of objective evidence, that the requirements for a specific intended use or application have been fulfilled” (Section 3.1, citing ISO 9000:2015).

How this profile uses it: As in the source. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table emphasizes that validity is established for a particular deployment, so evidence from one system, place or time does not carry over to another without checking.

A.1.2 Accurate

Source definition: “closeness of results of observations, computations, or estimates to the true values or the values accepted as being true” (Section 3.1, citing ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022).

How this profile uses it: As in the source. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table adds that the reference itself has to be chosen, and that accuracy is rarely captured by one number (A.3.4).

A.1.3 Bias Managed

Source definition: “NIST has identified three major categories of AI bias to be considered and managed: systemic, computational and statistical, and human-cognitive. Each of these can occur in the absence of prejudice, partiality, or discriminatory intent” (Section 3.7, drawing on NIST SP 1270).

How this profile uses it: Separated from Fair, and without “harmful”: the AI RMF characteristic is “Fair – with Harmful Bias Managed”. This profile covers systematic error that degrades performance without harming anyone, which is common in critical infrastructure. The AI RMF itself notes that “bias is not always a negative phenomenon”. Managed means deliberately handled, not eliminated: a bias accepted for stated reasons is managed.

A.1.4 Reliable

Source definition: “ability of an item to perform as required, without failure, for a given time interval, under given conditions” (Section 3.1, citing ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022). The AI RMF adds that reliability is a goal for correctness “under the conditions of expected use and over a given period of time”.

How this profile uses it: The Trustworthiness Characteristics table says “in expected conditions and use”, so that use, and not only conditions, sets the boundary. For an SI system, and above all a general-purpose assistant, a common departure is being used for something it was not meant for, in conditions that are otherwise normal.

A.1.5 Robust

Source definition: “ability of a system to maintain its level of performance under a variety of circumstances” (Section 3.1, citing ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022). The AI RMF adds that the system “should perform in ways that minimize potential harms to people if it is operating in an unexpected setting”.

How this profile uses it: As in the source, including the AI RMF’s addition, which the Trustworthiness Characteristics table carries as “limits harm” without restricting it to harm to people.

A.1.6 Resilient

Source definition: Systems “may be said to be resilient if they can withstand unexpected adverse events or unexpected changes in their environment or use – or if they can maintain their functions and structure in the face of internal and external change and degrade safely and gracefully when this is necessary” (Section 3.3, adapted from ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022). The AI RMF also describes resilience as “the ability to return to normal function after an unexpected adverse event”.

How this profile uses it: As in the source. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table emphasizes graceful degradation and the ability to resume operation, because for many SI systems the point at which a fallback is needed is not obvious in advance.

A.1.7 Safe

Source definition: Systems should “not under defined conditions, lead to a state in which human life, health, property, or the environment is endangered” (Section 3.2, citing ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022).

How this profile uses it: As in the source, including “under defined conditions” (A.3.3). The Trustworthiness Characteristics table adds that harm can reach the process through a person acting on an SI system’s recommendation, not only through the SI system acting itself.

A.1.8 Secure

Source definition: “AI systems that can maintain confidentiality, integrity, and availability through protection mechanisms that prevent unauthorized access and use may be said to be secure” (Section 3.3).

How this profile uses it: The gloss in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table, “withstands attack”, is closer to ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022, which defines security as “resistance to intentional, unauthorized act(s) designed to cause harm or damage to a system”. An attack can change what a system does through its inputs without any unauthorized access. SI systems can be especially exposed, because inputs that look ordinary, such as subtly altered images or instructions hidden in a document, can redirect them, so a definition centered on access control alone would miss such attacks.

A.1.9 Transparent

Source definition: “Transparency reflects the extent to which information about an AI system and its outputs is available to individuals interacting with such a system” (Section 3.4).

How this profile uses it: Separated from Accountable (A.2). The gloss in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table, “the information exists and can be obtained”, stresses that the information has to have been captured in the first place.

A.1.10 Explainable & Interpretable

Source definition: “Explainability refers to a representation of the mechanisms underlying AI systems’ operation, whereas interpretability refers to the meaning of AI systems’ output in the context of their designed functional purposes” (Section 3.5).

How this profile uses it: As in the source. The AI RMF points to NIST IR 8312 and NIST IR 8367 for more. IR 8312’s principle of explanation accuracy, that “An explanation correctly reflects the reason for generating the output and/or accurately reflects the system’s process”, underlies Task 4.6: Ensure that Confidence and Rationale Shown to Personnel Reflect How the SI System Reached Its Output, which adds that an explanation constructed after the fact may not reflect how the output was actually produced.

A.1.11 Accountable

Source definition: The AI RMF does not define accountability on its own; it states that “Accountability presupposes transparency” (Section 3.4). ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022 defines accountable as “answerable for actions, decisions, and performance”, and notes that “Accountability relates to an allocated responsibility”.

How this profile uses it: Separated from Transparent (A.2). The gloss in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table, “responsibility is named and can be enforced”, is consistent with the ISO note. Allocating responsibility in advance is good practice for any system (see Nissenbaum 1996). For SI systems it matters more, because behavior is learned from data, models, and instructions that many parties contribute across the lifecycle, so even where an output can be explained, each party’s part in it can be hard to separate after the event (see Cooper 2022). As the Trustworthiness Characteristics table notes, those responsible should also be appropriately empowered and identified in advance: they need the visibility, time, resources, access, support, and authority to act. Implementations 3.9.3 to 3.9.5 test whether that holds for a particular capability.

A.1.12 Privacy-Enhanced

Source definition: “Privacy refers generally to the norms and practices that help to safeguard human autonomy, identity, and dignity” (Section 3.6). The AI RMF lists “anonymity, confidentiality, and control” among privacy values, and notes that “AI systems can also present new risks to privacy by allowing inference to identify individuals or previously private information about individuals”.

How this profile uses it: Extended to inferences about an organization’s operations, such as staffing or production, and to sensitive operational and proprietary data. The AI RMF’s inclusion of confidentiality among privacy values partly supports this extension. Inferences about staffing fall within the NIST Privacy Framework, which defines an individual as “A single person or a group of persons, including at a societal level”. Operational and proprietary data go further. The Privacy Framework treats privacy risk as problems that individuals experience, and places loss of confidentiality under cybersecurity risk. The ISO/IEC TS 5723:2022 definition of privacy likewise concerns the private life or affairs of an individual. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table treats them together because the same mechanism, inference from records that are each harmless, can expose both.

A.1.13 Fair

Source definition: “Fairness in AI includes concerns for equality and equity by addressing issues such as harmful bias and discrimination” (Section 3.7).

How this profile uses it: Separated from Bias Managed. The Trustworthiness Characteristics table extends it to uneven performance across equipment, conditions, locations and customers as well as populations, since such variation can breach service obligations an operator has. What level of variation is acceptable is a determination for the operator.

A.2 Grouping, and Crosswalk to the AI RMF

The AI RMF presents its characteristics in seven groups, and describes Valid and Reliable as “a necessary condition of trustworthiness” and Accountable and Transparent as relating “to all other characteristics” (Figure 4). This profile keeps those relationships. It separates some of the AI RMF’s pairs into single characteristics, and arranges the 13 under five questions:

  • Does it do the correct thing? (Valid, Accurate, Bias Managed)
  • Does it keep doing the correct thing, particularly as conditions change? (Reliable, Robust, Resilient)
  • Does it avoid unintentional or deliberate harm? (Safe, Secure)
  • Does it allow its behavior to be observed, understood, and accounted for? (Transparent, Explainable & Interpretable, Accountable)
  • Does it meet its obligations to those who depend on it? (Privacy-Enhanced, Fair)

The questions are a reading aid. They do not claim that characteristics under the same question never pull against each other; several of the tensions in A.4 are between members of one group.

The pairs are separated for one main reason: evidence for one member is not evidence for the other. A validation report for normal operation says little about behavior outside it, and a record that an SI system’s actions were logged does not show who is responsible for them. The AI RMF draws several of these distinctions itself. It states that “Systems in which harmful biases are mitigated are not necessarily fair”, that security and resilience “are related but distinct characteristics”, and that transparency, explainability and interpretability “are distinct characteristics that support each other”.

Transparent, Explainable & Interpretable, and Accountable are arranged as a chain. Transparency asks whether the information exists and can be obtained. Explainability and interpretability ask whether it carries meaning: in the AI RMF’s words, “how” a decision was made and “why”. Accountability asks whether responsibility attaches to it. Each depends on the one before it, as the AI RMF notes for the first and last (“Accountability presupposes transparency”), but each can fail on its own.

A.3 Distinctions Between Characteristics

A.3.1 Valid, and the conditions it has to hold across

Valid is the overall claim that an SI system is the right one for this use. Reliable, Robust and Resilient describe the conditions across which that claim has to hold:

  • Reliable concerns performance over time at the conditions and use that were specified. It is measured against the specification, so it is a question of conformance.
  • Robust concerns performance across a variety of conditions and uses, including ones not anticipated. It is measured against the system’s own performance in normal operation, so it is a question of whether that performance holds as conditions vary.
  • Resilient concerns what happens when performance cannot hold: whether the system keeps its functions and structure, degrades gracefully, and can return to normal operation.

One way to see the three together is through the design basis: the conditions a system is deliberately designed to withstand, chosen with regard to their likelihood, their consequences, and the resources available (see How Far to Go). Reliable concerns performance as specified in the expected conditions and use. Robust concerns how far that performance holds as conditions depart from what was expected. Resilient concerns what the system does when that performance cannot hold: maintaining at least a minimally acceptable level of its essential functions, and recovering afterward (see NIST SP 800-160v2r1). The design basis sets how far out the system is designed to do so. Some fields draw these lines explicitly: for nuclear power plants, for example, ground motion above an operating basis earthquake requires the plant to shut down and be inspected, while structures needed for safe shutdown are designed to remain functional through a larger safe-shutdown earthquake (see 10 CFR Part 50, Appendix S). Conditions beyond the design basis are not designed for; they are still examined, so that the organization understands what would happen and plans for it where it can. For an SI system, these boundaries are often harder to locate than for engineered equipment, because its behavior was learned from examples rather than specified, and the conditions it was trained on may not match those it meets in use.

Defining validity fixes what counts as expected, and so decides whether a given departure is a question of reliability, robustness or resilience. The three are still listed separately because each needs its own evidence. A system can also be stable, well characterized and reliable, and still not good enough for the task. Stability is not the same as adequacy, and in critical infrastructure a stable but inadequate system is a familiar problem.

What Valid adds beyond the other three is the question of transfer: whether evidence gathered for one deployment applies to another. An SI system validated at one plant, for one patient population or under one set of organizational conventions may be wrong at the next, even if nothing about the SI system has changed.

A.3.2 Accurate and Reliable

Accuracy is a property of outputs: how closely they agree with a reference. Reliability is the property of continuing to perform as required. Accuracy is often one of the things required, and reliability is that requirement continuing to be met. The words “as required” in the definition of reliability matter. Without them, a system that was consistently wrong would count as reliable.

A.3.3 Safe, and why Secure is not scoped to conditions

Safe is defined “under defined conditions”, because a hazard analysis has a scope: it identifies the conditions it considered. Secure carries no such qualifier in this profile. Moving a system outside its expected conditions is often what an adversary is trying to do, so scoping a security claim to expected conditions would assume away the threat. For SI systems the cheapest way to do this is often through the inputs: a sensor reading, a maintenance record or a document an assistant is asked to summarize, rather than a change to the plant.

A.3.4 Bias Managed, Fair, and the choice of reference

Bias concerns where systematic error comes from. Fairness concerns who is affected by the outcome. The two are independent in both directions. A model whose errors lean toward summer conditions has a bias with no fairness dimension, and a model with no detectable bias can still feed an allocation policy whose outcome is uneven across those it serves.

In critical infrastructure, bias can enter through the history of how data was collected. Records may be dominated by normal operation, with few abnormal events, or by the best-instrumented assets, so that legacy or poorly instrumented equipment is under-represented. Bias can also enter through background and domain knowledge built into an SI system, such as rules, physics models or engineered features, which may be wrong, incomplete or out of date, for example a plant model from commissioning or features designed for equipment since replaced.

Some bias is designed into the method. In machine learning, bias also has a second, technical sense, often called inductive bias: “any basis for choosing one generalization over another, other than strict consistency with the observed training instances” (see Mitchell 1980). Mitchell showed that a learner without such bias is no better than one that stores its training examples and looks them up, so inductive bias is what makes generalization possible. Examples include an assumption that what happens next resembles what came before, a preference for simpler rules among those that fit the data equally well (see Implementation 4.6.4: Distill Learned Models into Interpretable Models that Can Be Verified), and knowledge of the domain or of how the result will be used. Inductive bias is not a defect. Managing it means choosing it deliberately and checking it against the process, in line with Mitchell’s recommendation to make biases “just as explicit” as the observations a system learns from. Engineers know the same idea from a correlation used outside the range it was fitted for. The difference for many learned models is that the range is not stated anywhere: the assumptions sit in the method, not on a datasheet. A related failure is shortcut learning, where a model relies on a feature that happens to predict the answer in the training data but is not the one that matters (see Geirhos 2020).

The choice of reference brings a similar risk for Accurate. SI systems are often used where a task is too complex to specify simply, and the same complexity makes a faithful reference hard to construct. NIST SP 1270 notes that development teams often use proxies for what cannot be measured directly, and that proxies “can create many risks”. A system tuned to whichever reference was easiest to obtain can improve on the measure while getting worse at the task. This is often called Goodhart’s law, after the observation that “any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes” (see Manheim 2019). Two of the variants Manheim and Garrabrant distinguish are close to concerns elsewhere in this appendix: optimizing a measure can push the system into conditions where the old relationship between measure and goal no longer holds, which is a question of robustness, and an adversary can exploit the gap between them, which is a question of security. Examples include a failure predictor graded against work orders, which record what was noticed rather than what failed, and a summarizer graded on whether it reads well rather than on whether it kept what mattered. A related tension arises where one technical metric is used to rank risks whose consequences differ (Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs).

A.4 Tensions Between Characteristics

The AI RMF notes that “tradeoffs are usually involved” in addressing trustworthiness characteristics, and that analysis can show where tradeoffs exist but does “not answer questions about how to navigate the tradeoff”, which depends on “the values at play in the relevant context”. It names some itself: between accuracy and robustness (Section 3.1), between interpretability and privacy, between predictive accuracy and interpretability, and, under conditions such as data sparsity, between privacy-enhancing techniques and accuracy (Section 3). Section 3.6 adds that “Privacy-related risks may influence security, bias, and transparency and come with tradeoffs with these other characteristics”. The AI RMF Playbook asks organizations to articulate and analyze tradeoffs across trustworthiness characteristics for the context of use, and to document them (MAP 1.5), and to document the basis for decisions made on them (MANAGE 4.2). NIST AI 100-2e2025, Section 4.1.1, describes further trade-offs, including between explainability and adversarial robustness.

In this Guidebook, tensions are recorded in the Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance field of individual Practices and Tasks, most with the conditions under which the characteristics move together and those under which they pull apart, and many with one or more possible ways to balance them. How far to go on a measure that is a matter of degree, and how available resources enter these choices, is discussed in How Far to Go. A tension stated without its conditions is a half-truth: the same two characteristics often move together in one setting and against each other in another. This section describes the patterns that recur across those entries and points to examples. It is not a complete list, and the entries themselves remain the place to look for a specific item.

A characteristic against itself. The most common tension in this Guidebook is between a characteristic and itself, most often Safe against Safe: the same measure that guards against one hazard delays or suppresses the response to another. A fixed ceiling on automated commands can suppress correct action during a fast-moving abnormal condition (Task 2.1: Establish SI Usage Thresholds and Rate Control Mechanisms). A lower detection threshold catches more events but, for rare conditions, multiplies false alarms (Task 3.1: Identify Risk Trade-offs). Bounding an SI system’s authority can slow a protective action (Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities), refusing an action part-way through a sequence can leave the process in a hazardous state (Task 5.2: Determine and Implement Specific Access Requirements), and paced handover protects against taking control without context while delaying the takeover (Task 11.2: Define Manual and Alternative Operating Procedures for Long-Term Mission Continuity). Other entries with Safe on both sides concern verification steps that become routine (Task 3.6: Mitigate Automation Complacency and Skills Degradation), hazards whose containment can lapse as autonomy widens (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface), safety margins, where wide margins can give up the benefits the SI system was deployed for, which may include safety (Task 8.2: Identify Additional Controls that May Be Required in Scenarios Where Deterministic Root Cause Analysis Is Not Possible), training that can overshoot into under-trust (Task 9.2: Deliver Specialized Training on Human-SI Interaction and Automation Bias), and overlap between a retiring system and its successor (Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System). The same form appears for Accurate, where tuning to a fixed measure improves the measure and not the task (Task 2.4: Perform Linguistic and Domain-Context Robustness Testing) and an aggregate gain conceals a loss in a high-consequence class (Task 7.5: Conduct Impact Assessments and Risk Management for SI System and Model Updates), and for Resilient, where heterogeneous redundancy does less against drift that affects every model built on similar data (Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions).

Holding a change against the fix it carries. Change control, review and approval guard against a change that quietly degrades a characteristic. Where the change is itself an urgent fix, the same review keeps the fault in service (Practice 7, Implementation 6.1.1, Task 7.4). Holding back a newer version to allow exact replay after an incident is a variant (Task 8.1: Identify Scenarios Requiring Deterministic Root Cause Analysis and Incident Reconstruction).

Stopping against continuing. Where a process can be stopped safely, a trip and graceful degradation point the same way. Where stopping is itself hazardous, or the fallback is under-resourced, Safe and Resilient separate (Practice 4, Task 2.2, Practice 11). Pressure to return an SI system to service before re-commissioning evidence is complete, because manual operation is consuming resources, is a related form (Task 11.4: Establish System Integrity Re-Validation and Re-Commissioning Protocols). Waiting for full verification of a new artifact during urgent recovery is a form of the same tension (Practice 12: Independently Verify SI-Generated Operational Logic and Artifacts Across Their Lifecycle).

Scope against coverage. Drawing requirements or boundaries tightly makes acceptance and evidence clear, and can leave behavior outside them uncharacterized or block the behavior that made the SI system worth adopting (Practice 1, Practice 3, Task 3.2). Filtering anomalous records improves the data an SI system learns from, and can remove the abnormal conditions it most needs to recognize (Task 7.2: Validate, Select, and Prepare Data for SI Systems). Tuning for the best average can make a system more fragile at the edges (Task 1.2, Practice 2), while training across a whole specification range can spend learning capacity on conditions a given installation will not meet (Task 2.5: Verify that Learned Behavior Is Robust to Component, Hardware, and Data Schema Variance), and safeguards that keep a function performing across a wider range can make the point at which the fallback should engage harder to see (Practice 2: Define SI Robustness, Resilience, and Quality of Service Expectations). Evidence from the live system is the evidence that applies to it, but gathering it may disrupt a process that cannot tolerate disruption (Task 1.3, Task 2.3). Freezing a system that adapts at runtime holds it to conditions that may have moved, while letting it adapt exposes it to unrepresentative or adversarial data (Task 7.3: Manage Version Control and Logging for Internal SI Safety Mechanisms).

Keeping a record against protecting it. The record needed to reconstruct a decision can be the protected data itself (Practice 10, Task 10.1, Practice 13), can add latency to a real-time control path (Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities), or can be lost when restoring service or destroying a retired model (Task 8.3, Task 7.6, Task 13.2). Documenting boundary logic for operators and auditors can also tell an adversary where its thresholds sit (Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior), and autonomous recovery that goes unannounced can remove the signal that a safety margin is being consumed (Task 3.3: Establish Failure Notification Requirements). After an incident, capturing volatile state takes time while a system suspected of causing harm is still running (Task 13.3: Preserve What Is Needed and Hand Over What Depended on the System). Protecting those who report near-misses encourages reports while limiting how far individual responsibility can be enforced on what they reveal (Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses).

People in the loop. Keeping a person in the loop contributes to both Accountable and Safe only if the person can act in time (Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation). More rationale can help a person who works with an SI system daily and overload an emergency responder under time pressure (Task 4.2: Ensure that Personnel Are Able to Gain and Maintain Situational Awareness), and consolidating alerts reduces load while risking delay to a single urgent one (Task 4.4: Attention Governance and Alert/Alarm Flood Mitigation).

Accuracy against explanation. Whether accuracy and explanation pull apart depends on whether the knowledge a decision rests on can be stated as rules, constraints or a self-interpretable model (Task 1.4, Task 4.6).

Confinement against need. Confining an SI agent to its role bounds an attack or an error, and stops legitimate work, including recovery, when a need arises outside what was anticipated (Practice 5: Implement Identity and Access Management (IdAM) for SI Agents, Systems, and Tools). Related tensions arise for break-glass intervention (Task 4.1: Establish “Break-Glass” Intervention Procedures), authority on a partitioned site (Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities), automatic suspension on detection (Task 5.4: Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations), and fine-grained identity on constrained devices (Task 5.1: Establish Identities and Authentication for SI Entities). They also arise for restrictions on unvetted tools that move use out of view (Task 3.7: Govern and Mitigate Risks from Unauthorized, Transient, and “Shadow SI” Systems), limits on what leaves for a vendor who monitors the system (Task 6.3: Identify and Control Dataflows Across the SI Supply Chain), hard limits on a shared resource during an upset (Task 3.5: Define and Control Risks to Adjacent and Related Systems), and restrictions that cannot be lifted quickly when the restricted capability is keeping a service running (Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability). The reverse also occurs: an independent fallback channel that carries control keeps operation possible and adds a route into the process, for an intruder and for a mistaken command alike (Task 4.3: Establish Independent (“Out-of-Band”) Communication, Monitoring, Alerting, and Control Capabilities).

Requirements against choice. Requiring full supplier disclosure costs little when several suppliers can meet it. When few can, it can narrow the choice to a small number of large vendors, or rule out a less transparent system that would improve on the one in service in other respects (Practice 6: Integrate Visibility of the External Supply Chain of SI into Vendor and Third-Party Risk Management).

Drift against interference. Where one observation could be drift or interference, the reliability response of recalibrating and the security response of preserving state and investigating point in opposite directions (Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior).

The tensions the AI RMF names are not all represented in these entries. Accurate against Robust appears in several (Task 1.2, Practice 2, Task 2.5), and accuracy against interpretability in two (Task 1.4, Task 4.6). Privacy against accuracy appears only as part of a wider tension (Task 5.3: Implement Principles of Least Agency and Least Privilege for SI Entities), and privacy against interpretability does not appear as such. Privacy against transparency, which Section 3.6 names, appears in three entries (Practice 10, Task 10.1, Practice 13).

A.5 What These Characteristics Do Not Reach

The characteristics are framed around the SI system in its deployment. Several Practices depend just as much on people and on the organization. Whether an operator can re-engage in time to act (Task 4.5: Define Operational Regimes for Human-on-the-Loop and Human-Out-of-the-Loop Operation), whether training leaves trust matched to what the system warrants (Practice 9: Provide Role-Based SI Risk Training that Builds Well-Founded Trust), and whether the governance and oversight planned for a specific SI-enabled capability can actually be carried out (Task 3.9: Assess and Document Organizational Readiness for Each SI-Enabled Capability) are questions about people, plant and organization that no characteristic in the Trustworthiness Characteristics table fully covers. Human factors research has long described how people misuse automation through overreliance, disuse it, often because of false alarms, and how designers and managers can abuse it by automating functions “without due regard for the consequences for human performance” (see Parasuraman 1997). Accountable, which concerns who is responsible, and Valid, which concerns the deployment as a whole, come closest. This profile records these questions in the relevant Practices and Tasks rather than adding characteristics for them.

To the Reviewer:

We would welcome views on whether the characteristics should reach further into human and organizational performance, or whether the Practices are the right place for it.

Section 4.1.1 documents trade-offs among the attributes of trustworthy SI, including between explainability and adversarial robustness, between privacy and fairness, and between accuracy and adversarial robustness.
  • Research Shortcut Learning in Deep Neural Networks, by R. Geirhos, J.-H. Jacobsen, C. Michaelis, R. Zemel, W. Brendel, M. Bethge and F. A. Wichmann, Nature Machine Intelligence, 2020. Section 3 defines shortcuts as decision rules that perform well on test data like the training data but fail on out-of-distribution tests.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8312, Four Principles of Explainable Artificial Intelligence, September 2021, from NIST. Section 2.3, Explanation Accuracy, states the principle that an explanation correctly reflects the reason for generating the output or the system’s process, as distinct from the accuracy of the decision itself. The AI RMF points to it for explainability.
  • Guidance NIST IR 8367, Psychological Foundations of Explainability and Interpretability in Artificial Intelligence, from NIST. Section 3.2.4 distinguishes techniques that help a developer infer how an algorithm works from interpretability, which it describes as meaning in context for the end user. The AI RMF points to it for explainability and interpretability.
  • Guidance NIST Privacy Framework: A Tool for Improving Privacy through Enterprise Risk Management, Version 1.0, January 2020, from NIST. Section 1.2.1 and Figure 2 distinguish privacy risks, arising from data processing, from cybersecurity risks, arising from loss of confidentiality, integrity or availability, and Appendix B defines privacy risk in terms of problems individuals experience and an individual as a single person or a group of persons. The AI RMF points to it for privacy.
  • Research Mitchell, T. M., “The Need for Biases in Learning Generalizations”, Rutgers University technical report CBM-TR-117, May 1980. Defines bias in learning as any basis for choosing one generalization over another other than consistency with the training instances, shows in Section 3 that an unbiased learner can do no better than rote lookup, and in Section 4 classifies useful biases, including knowledge of the domain, of the intended use and of the source of the training data, and a bias toward simplicity.
  • Research Manheim, D. and Garrabrant, S., “Categorizing Variants of Goodhart’s Law”, arXiv:1803.04585, 2019. Describes failures that occur when optimization on a proxy measure causes it to stop tracking the intended goal, and distinguishes regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial variants.
  • Research Parasuraman, R. and Riley, V., “Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse”, Human Factors, 39(2), 1997, pages 230 to 253. Defines misuse as overreliance on automation, disuse as its neglect or underutilization, often caused by false alarms, and abuse as automating functions without due regard for the consequences for human performance.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Its glossary defines survivability, citing Richards (2009), as including “the satisfaction of a minimally acceptable level of value delivery during and after a disturbance”.
  • Regulation 10 CFR Part 50, Appendix S, Earthquake Engineering Criteria for Nuclear Power Plants, from the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission. Defines operating basis earthquake ground motion, above which the plant must shut down and be inspected, and safe-shutdown earthquake ground motion, through which structures, systems, and components needed for safe shutdown must be designed to remain functional.
  • Research Nissenbaum, H., “Accountability in a Computerized Society”, Science and Engineering Ethics, 2(1), 1996, pages 25 to 42. Identifies four barriers to accountability in computing, the first being the problem of many hands, which it notes “is not unique to computing” but to which computing is “particularly vulnerable”.
  • Research Cooper, A. F., Moss, E., Laufer, B. and Nissenbaum, H., “Accountability in an Algorithmic Society: Relationality, Responsibility, and Robustness in Machine Learning”, FAccT ’22, 2022. Section 3.1 extends the many-hands barrier to the machine learning pipeline, where harms can originate in dataset choice, task framing, or model selection, which it describes as a transparency issue beyond model interpretability.
