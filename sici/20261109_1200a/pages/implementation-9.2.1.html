Implementation 9.2.1: Include Role-Specific Training on SI Failure Modes

Role-specific training on how SI systems fail helps personnel recognize when to override an SI system and how to do it safely. Whether this training is folded into existing regular technical or cybersecurity training or delivered separately, and how often, is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. Folding it in reuses an established schedule and record, and also risks diluting SI-specific content or leaving it out of date between cycles when the SI system changes. Record the arrangement and the interval chosen. The training should include identifying applications and situations in which personnel in that role may use, interact with, or supervise AI systems, and the potential mission impact of AI system failures. It should cover situations in which AI systems should be overridden, the procedure for safely doing so, and how to obtain the necessary situational awareness. It should also cover communication, reporting, and documentation requirements. It should also cover how to assign an SI system output to the correct response pathway in Implementation 3.3.3: Actionable Operator Decision & Escalation Pathways, particularly at the boundary between “Trust & Execute” and “Spot-Check & Investigate”. For example, a load forecast that repeats the previous day’s pattern despite a known change in weather may indicate frozen or stale inputs rather than a correct prediction. Similarly, a general-purpose assistant may give information that was current when its model was trained but is no longer correct.

Training should also address how operating conditions that have implications for human cognitive fatigue (such as extended shift lengths, remote or isolated duty stations, and non-native-language communication) can reduce an individual’s capacity to catch an AI-generated error. This includes both OT and IT applications, such as those from a general-purpose assistant summarizing or drafting operationally relevant material. Role-specific guidance should be provided for compensating for these conditions. This Implementation helps reduce the risk that an operator accepts an incorrect AI recommendation without independent verification, because repeated correct outputs have conditioned them to treat AI recommendations as authoritative. This failure mode is long documented for conventional automation (see DOE 2009), but its forms with SI-driven automation and assistants, including self-updating systems and generated text, are emerging and are not yet well addressed by traditional security frameworks and guidance. It applies wherever personnel supervise, interact with, or can override AI systems in critical infrastructure operational environments.

Because established frameworks offer limited pre-built guidance, organizations should proactively extend existing TEVV protocols (Task 1.3: Implement Evaluation and Test Plans, Including TEVV, Across the SI Lifecycle) to probe for this specific failure mode, use qualified engineering and operational judgment to anticipate how automation bias might manifest in their particular application before an incident forces the question, and incorporate the results into ongoing training. Near-misses and incidents reported under Task 8.6: Establish Non-Punitive Reporting for SI-Related Near-Misses are another source of training content specific to the organization’s own SI systems.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Is this training folded into existing regular technical or cybersecurity training or delivered separately, and how often does it run?

References:

  • Guidance Principles for the Secure Integration of Artificial Intelligence in Operational Technology, December 2025, joint guidance from nine national cybersecurity agencies across seven countries, led by CISA. Section 1.3, Educate Personnel on AI, warns that reliance on AI may erode manual skills and that personnel may misinterpret AI outputs, and asks that OT teams be trained to interpret and validate AI outputs and to know what to do when they are invalid. Section 4.1 notes that human oversight also maintains human training, which is critical in an emergency. Section 2.4 asks for failsafe mechanisms that revert to traditional automation or manual operation.
  • Guidance Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning in Real-Time System Operations, White Paper Revision 1, November 2024, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC), with ResilientGrid, the NERC Energy Management System Working Group and the Real-Time Operating Subcommittee. Chapter 3, Human Factors in AI/ML, recommends training for system operators that covers how far to trust a tool and when to verify it, the fact that a self-updating system’s past reliability may differ from its current reliability, and, for generative tools, critical review of content and checking for hallucinations and confidential information. It also recommends high-fidelity simulation as the most effective way to build these skills, and wide-area knowledge so operators can judge AI recommendations. Its discussion of automaticity notes that operators who verify every recommendation at first can drift toward accepting them by habit, and its discussion of implications for the workforce notes that increased cognitive load reduces the time available to review AI outputs.
  • Guidance DOE-HDBK-1028-2009, Human Performance Improvement Handbook, Volume 1: Concepts and Principles, June 2009, from the U.S. Department of Energy. Chapter 2, Reducing Error, under Equipment Dependencies, notes that people who believe equipment is reliable may reduce their vigilance, and that monitoring by a computer can lead to complacency. Among the ways to counter this dependency, it lists training people on the failure modes of automatic systems and how those failures are detected. The handbook draws on commercial nuclear power practice, and its subject is conventional automation. It bears on the failure-mode training this Implementation extends to SI systems.
  • Research Schiller, C. A., The Human Factor in Detecting Errors of Large Language Models: A Systematic Literature Review and Future Research Directions, preprint arXiv:2403.09743v1, March 2024, from the University of Duisburg-Essen. A review of 28 papers on human detection of errors in large language model outputs. Section 7, Discussion, finds that the human factors behind this ability are not a focus of current research: studies rely on a few senior domain experts, and seniority and domain expertise are the only personal attributes considered. It bears on this Implementation’s statement that the failure mode is not yet well addressed, including for general-purpose assistants.
