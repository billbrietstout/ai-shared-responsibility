Implementation 5.4.2: Deploy Real-Time Behavioral Anomaly Detection for SI Agent Activity

Automated alerting on unusual SI agent activity gives the organization a chance to act while a problem is still developing. Extend the periodic review in Implementation 5.4.1: Establish Logging and Periodic Review of SI Agent Authentication and Access Events with automated, real-time alerting when an AI agent’s access pattern deviates from its established baseline, such as an unusual login time, an unfamiliar source location, or a spike in request volume. For agents, the baseline can also cover the tools and assets each one normally uses, so that first use of a tool, an unusual sequence of tool calls, an unexpected delegation chain or a run of denied requests can raise an alert. This matters most in machine-speed environments where a human reviewing logs after the fact may be too late to prevent harm. In isolated or semi-isolated OT segments, where SI entities may run on devices that produce little or no host-level logging, this detection may need to rely on network-layer telemetry, such as passive monitoring of the traffic between the SI system and the process, and the monitoring should depend as little as practical on the SI system it watches, recording any dependence that remains, consistent with Task 4.3: Establish Independent (“Out-of-Band”) Communication, Monitoring, Alerting, and Control Capabilities. Alerting can also cover the inputs reaching the SI system, since high-frequency or repetitive inputs can be signs of an automated attempt to compromise it. Where the organization has prepared a means to block a suspect entity’s access, or to disconnect inbound connections to the SI system in a major incident, it supports a response at the speed the detection allows. Blocking or disconnecting carries its own considerations, such as leaving a process part-way through a sequence (see the Secure versus Safe tension in Task 5.4: Implement Continuous Monitoring of SI Entities Against Access and Action Boundaries and Expectations). Which function receives and acts on these alerts is for the organization to decide, within any regulatory or contractual obligations. A security operations function brings threat context, while operations staff can better tell unusual but legitimate activity from misuse. Record where the alerts go and why. Whichever function receives them needs the access, time, and authority to act on them, including outside normal working hours.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Which function receives and acts on the alerts?
  • What prepared means exist to block an entity’s access or disconnect inbound connections, and who may use them?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control enhancement AC-2(12), Account Monitoring for Atypical Usage, monitors system accounts for defined atypical usage and reports it, with access at unusual times or from unusual locations as its examples, which are the same deviations this Implementation alerts on for AI agent accounts.
  • Guidance Deploying AI Systems Securely: Best Practices for Deploying Secure and Resilient AI Systems, Cybersecurity Information Sheet, version 1.0, April 2024, from the NSA Artificial Intelligence Security Center with CISA, the FBI and the national cybersecurity centers of Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United Kingdom. Written for organizations deploying externally developed SI systems on premises or in private cloud. It asks for alerts on anomalies and compromise attempts, for monitoring that watches for high-frequency or repetitive inputs, and for a means to immediately block suspect users or disconnect inbound connections to the AI models. This bears on what the SOC alerting watches and what it can trigger.
  • Guidance OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, Version 2026, December 2025, from the Agentic Security Initiative of the OWASP Gen AI Security Project, a volunteer community project of the Open Worldwide Application Security Project. Entry ASI02, Tool Misuse and Exploitation, includes among its mitigations immutable logs of tool invocations and parameter changes and continuously monitoring for anomalous execution rates, unusual tool-chaining patterns, such as a database read followed by an external transfer, and policy violations. This bears on extending the baseline from login-style account anomalies to the tools an agent uses.
