Practice 10: Implement Multi-Tiered SI System Logging and Audit Capabilities

Establish a framework for the systematic capture and preservation of operational telemetry across the SI lifecycle to help ensure decisions remain transparent, inspectable, and verifiable, and provide necessary insight.

Keeping a record of what an SI system did, and on what basis, helps an organization reconstruct a decision after the fact, learn from it, and show others what happened. This Practice supports the evidentiary foundation required to audit actions across the entire SI Risk Surface (Task 3.8: Scope and Map the Expanded SI Attack Surface and Risk Surface). SI-aware logging can build on an organization’s existing logging, which often centers on network and system events that cover part of the Attack Surface. It adds decision-level context, including prompts, model parameters, context window states, and guardrail interactions, needed to reconstruct non-deterministic operational failures. This Practice supports the development of audit-ready systems that support forensic investigations and regulatory compliance, contributing the data on which organizational accountability relies.

Decision-level records can lose their meaning when the system that produced them changes. After an update, be it deliberate or as the system learns over time, or once the system is retired, the model version, prompts, retrieval sources, configuration, and other state and information behind a past decision may no longer be available (Implementation 10.1.5: Define What Each Operational Record Is For), so what is kept alongside the records, and for how long, can be decided as part of Task 10.1: Define Decision-Level Logging and Metadata Retention Policies. For records that need to outlast the system itself, see also Practice 13: Establish Procedures for Retiring SI Systems and Withdrawing Their Authority.

Main Trustworthiness Contributions:

  • Transparent: Contributes a decision-level record, covering prompts, inputs, outputs, model versions, and guardrail interactions rather than only network events, so that what an SI system did can be reconstructed after the fact. This Practice does not cover deciding when such a reconstruction is required, or what to do where it cannot be done, which Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures covers.
  • Accountable: Contributes records protected against alteration and kept apart from the systems they describe, so that they can be relied on in regulatory oversight and liability decisions about automated actions. This Practice does not cover naming who answers for an incident, or who owns the SI capability outside one. For those, see Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures and Implementation 3.9.1: Establish Accountable Ownership.
  • Reliable: Contributes periodic and trend-based review of the accumulated record for drift, emerging bias, and silent failure, so that slow degradation can be caught before it becomes an incident. This Practice does not cover real-time detection of anomalous behavior, which Practice 3: Define Risks, Policies, and Oversight for Automated, Agentic, and SI Behavior covers.

Trustworthiness Side Effects to Consider:

  • Secure: The decision-level record this Practice builds holds prompts, system prompts, guardrail interactions, active thresholds, and version identifiers. Together they show how the SI system is instructed, what its safeguards block, and where its limits lie. An attacker who obtains the record could use it to craft inputs that stay just under a threshold, to learn which guardrails to get around, or to harvest sensitive information captured in prompts. Protecting the record is a matter for the organization’s existing information classification and handling practice, together with the access controls in Task 10.2: Establish Data Integrity and Tamper-Evidence Controls for Telemetry and Logging.

Trustworthiness Tensions to Balance:

  • Privacy-Enhanced and Secure versus Transparent and Accountable: Where an SI system works on machine telemetry and configuration, capturing its full inputs and outputs may provide insight into sensitive organizational processes. Where it works on sensitive personal information, such as clinical support decisions or smart meter consumption records, the raw text needed to reconstruct a decision is itself the protected data. Either kind of record can help an attacker, by showing how sensitive processes are run or by exposing personal information. One way to balance this is to define which fields are essential to reconstruction, mask the rest, and hold the unredacted record in secured storage separated from day-to-day operational view, as Implementation 10.1.3: Forensic Integrity and Privacy-Preserving Log Partitioning describes; others include limiting what is captured in the first place to the elements a privacy risk assessment identifies as needed.
  • Transparent versus Reliable and Safe: Where an SI system advises on decisions taken over minutes or hours, capturing a full decision-level record at each output may cause little delay. Where it sits on a real-time control path, capturing the same record synchronously can add latency or load that disturbs the timing of the control function, and where that function protects people or equipment or is otherwise time-critical, the delay is itself a hazard. One way to balance this is to give the control function priority and buffer or defer the record rather than block on it, as Implementation 10.1.4: Asynchronous Telemetry and Inference Logging for Constrained Edges does for constrained edge devices; others include capturing the record on a parallel path outside the control loop.

Related AI RMF Subcategories Include:

  • MEASURE 2.8 (on measuring accountability and transparency): This Practice covers the decision-level record from which transparency and accountability for automated actions can be examined after the fact; it does not cover examining and documenting those risks.
  • MANAGE 4.3 (on incident response): This Practice describes the preserved, tamper-evident runtime context on which tracking, responding to, and recovering from SI incidents depends; it does not cover communicating incidents to relevant SI actors or affected communities. For that, see for example Practice 8: Incorporate SI-Aware Incident Analysis and Response Procedures.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Where capturing a decision-level record could delay a real-time control function, is the record buffered, deferred or captured on a parallel path? (see the Transparent versus Reliable and Safe tension)
  • Who is responsible for the logging and review this Practice describes, and what staff time, storage, and tooling are set aside for it?
  • For SI systems supplied as a service, which decision-level records does the provider hold, and how does the organization obtain them when it needs them?

References:

  • Guidance OMB M-26-14, Ensuring Effective and Efficient Agency Logging and Network Visibility to Defend Against Evolving Cyber Threats, 22 May 2026, from the Office of Management and Budget. Rescinds M-21-31 and directs United States federal agencies to take a risk-based, prioritized approach to logging, organized around two objectives, continuous event monitoring and threat hunting, investigation, response and forensics, and covering operational technology and IoT that is part of an information system. Appendix B sets minimum retention (searchable for six months, retrievable for a year). The memorandum addresses network and system logging rather than SI decision-level records; it parallels the forensic purpose and the risk-based prioritization this Practice describes.
  • Regulation Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the Artificial Intelligence Act, 13 June 2024, from the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union. Article 12 requires high-risk SI systems to allow the automatic recording of events over their lifetime, including events relevant to identifying situations that may present a risk and to monitoring their operation. Articles 19(1) and 26(6) have providers and deployers keep those logs for at least six months. Annex III point 2 classes SI systems used as safety components in managing critical infrastructure as high-risk. It is outside this profile’s scope and parallels the decision-level record this Practice describes.
