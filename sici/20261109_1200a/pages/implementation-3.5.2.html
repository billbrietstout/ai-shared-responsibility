Implementation 3.5.2: Identify Indirect Shared Infrastructure and Collateral Damage Exposure

Finding what an SI system shares with otherwise unrelated systems helps an organization reduce the chance that a single failure disables several of them at once, including safeguards assumed to be redundant. Audit the operational environment to identify functionally independent systems that are vulnerable to collateral damage from an AI failure. Evaluation criteria should include cross-cutting environmental and infrastructure commonalities, such as:

  • Physical Proximity: Independent physical assets co-located in the same facility or enclosure, or that share the same power, cooling, or other physical resources.
  • Network Topography: Systems sharing the same network switches, backbones, or uplink paths.
  • Logical Substrates: Unrelated applications sharing the same hypervisor, underlying cloud tenant, base operating system image, or database instance.
  • Shared Services: Systems relying on the same identity provider, time source, remote access gateway, vendor update channel, model provider, inference endpoint or retrieval store.

This mapping should also consider whether a single event or action could affect multiple identified safeguards at once, rather than assuming redundant controls fail independently. Common-cause analysis is established practice in safety and reliability engineering. Organizations that use it can build on it here, applying it to the SI system and the services it relies on. This includes the case where the same model, vendor product or update channel is deployed across many sites or organizations, so that one defect, update or manipulation can affect them at once and remove a fallback that several of them share. Diversity in fallback arrangements can help limit this exposure; see Task 2.2: Establish Requirements for Redundancy in SI-Driven Functions. This asset assessment should, where possible, reach outward past the facility’s immediate perimeter to evaluate shared municipal substrates, such as the regional electrical grid, water networks, or telecommunication backbones, where a localized AI failure or resource-saturation event could cause regional collateral damage.

Where direct technical visibility or telemetry past the facility’s perimeter is unavailable, organizations should utilize qualified engineering approximations and best-available sector intelligence to estimate these external impacts. These estimations should be explicitly documented as bounded technical assumptions, so that the limits of the data stay visible and the assessment can proceed without complete external information. The task of mapping internal dependencies is common to Implementation 7.6.1: Internal Dependency Mapping (Orphan System Prevention). Maintaining this map can reduce the effort needed when this system is later decommissioned. This mapping is also the basis for identifying what depends on an AI system at retirement, under Implementation 13.3.3: Determine and Record What Happens to Everything that Depended on the System.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • How far past the facility perimeter does the assessment reach, and which external impacts are recorded as bounded assumptions?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-160v2r1, Developing Cyber-Resilient Systems: A Systems Security Engineering Approach, from NIST. Section D.5.2.6, Maintain Redundancy, states that the extent of redundancy is established through analysis of single points of failure and shared resources, and that trends to convergence undermine it. Its example is an organization using Voice over Internet Protocol, which cannot claim alternate paths for phone, email and instant messaging. Section D.5.1.1, Focus on Common Critical Assets, makes common infrastructures, shared services and shared data repositories high priorities for the application of cyber resiliency techniques because they are common to multiple missions. This supports the common-cause view this Implementation takes of functionally independent systems.
  • Guidance NIST AI 600-1, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile, July 2024, from NIST. Section 2 names algorithmic monocultures as an ecosystem-level risk, and its footnote describes how repeated use of the same model or algorithm can increase susceptibility to correlated failures, such as unexpected shocks, because multiple actors rely on the same algorithm. This bears on the same model deployed across many sites or organizations.
  • Research 2025 Long-Term Reliability Assessment, January 2026, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). Recommendation 2 notes that inverter-based resources respond to disturbances based on programmed logic and inverter controls, and that their tripping during grid faults has caused sudden loss of generation, over wide areas in some cases. The common thread NERC identifies is a shared lack of ride-through capability that lets a minor disturbance become a major one. Under Data Centers and Large Commercial and Industrial Loads, it also reports an event in which data centers and other power electronic loads transferred load to backup generation at the same time, a reduction of about 1,500 MW that caused frequency overshoot and high voltages on the grid. Written about the grid rather than AI systems, these are sector examples of many independent assets failing together because they share the same control behavior, and of a facility’s collective response reaching the regional grid.
  • Incident Report Incident Review: Considering Simultaneous Voltage-Sensitive Load Reductions, January 2025, from the North American Electric Reliability Corporation (NERC). On July 10, 2024, a transmission line fault led to the customer-initiated, simultaneous loss of about 1,500 MW of voltage-sensitive load, mostly data centers transferring to backup power. Much of it came from a protection scheme that counts voltage disturbances and moves the facility off the grid until it is manually reconnected. NERC notes that grid planning has historically considered large generation losses rather than load losses of this size. This is the primary account of the large-load event that the 2025 assessment summarizes, and it bears on the shared municipal substrates this Implementation asks to be evaluated.
