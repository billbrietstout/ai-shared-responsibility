Implementation 7.4.1: Define and Implement Requirements for Approvals and Logging of Updates and Changes to SI Systems

Approvals and logging are particularly important for changes to models, knowledge bases, and guardrails, especially for applications operating in a reduced oversight mode, or where automation bias is a known risk and thus where changes in behavior may not be immediately noticed. Record for each change what was altered, who approved it, and the evidence the approval rested on, so that a later potential investigation can reconstruct why the change was judged safe. Where a change alters model behavior rather than configuration alone, treat it as a trigger for re-evaluation under Implementation 2.3.4: Recurrent Risk Evaluations, the same treatment a disclosed vendor update receives for an externally sourced component (Implementation 6.2.2: Track Vendor Updates and Third-Party Entity Changes Through Change Management Process). An internal change reaching production unreviewed is the internally sourced equivalent of a silent vendor update, and would otherwise be caught, if at all, only by Task 3.4: Monitor for Anomalous SI Behavior. An update to an SI system that performs, influences, or constrains a protective or safety function should be treated as a change to that function’s settings or configuration, subject to equivalent approval and logging requirements, regardless of how the vendor or developer characterizes the change.

Where changes are deployed through an automated pipeline, the pipeline can be configured so that a change cannot be promoted to production until the required approvals are recorded. Where the organization already runs a management of change process for operational technology or process changes, changes to SI systems that act on those processes can be routed through it rather than through a separate process. The depth of review can scale with consequence. For example, a change to an SI system that acts on a physical process, or to a safety mechanism, may warrant safety review, test evidence from outside production, independent approval and a tested rollback plan, while a change to an advisory tool may need only owner approval and logging. Where few qualified people are available, for example in a small organization or a field office, the person who makes a change may also have to approve it. Where that happens, record it, and how the conflict is offset, such as by a later review by someone outside the change or by closer monitoring after deployment (Implementation 3.9.6: Keep a Record of Design Decisions and Accepted Risks). Where the organization chooses to establish one, an emergency path for changes that cannot wait, such as disabling or reverting a capability, can let the change proceed and then be reviewed and recorded through the normal process as soon as practical afterward. Disabling or reverting a capability carries its own considerations, such as losing the function it performed or bringing back faults an earlier version had (see Practice 4 and Practice 11). Until that review is complete, temporary measures such as closer monitoring or tighter guardrails can limit the risk of a change that has not yet been reviewed.

Decisions for the Organization:

  • Are changes to SI systems that act on a process routed through the existing management of change process?
  • How does the depth of review scale with consequence, and which changes qualify for the emergency path?

References:

  • Guidance NIST SP 800-53r5, Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, from NIST. Control CM-3, Configuration Change Control, determines which changes are configuration-controlled, reviews and approves or disapproves them with explicit consideration of impact analyses, documents change decisions, and retains records of changes, which is the record of what was altered, who approved it and why that this Implementation asks for, applied here to models, knowledge bases and guardrails. Enhancement CM-3(1), Automated Documentation, Notification, and Prohibition of Changes, uses automated mechanisms to prohibit changes until designated approvals are received, which is the pipeline gate this Implementation suggests.
  • Guidance NIST SP 800-128, Guide for Security-Focused Configuration Management of Information Systems, August 2011, with updates as of October 2019, from NIST. Section 3.3.2, on the configuration change control process, states that when time does not allow the established process to be followed, unscheduled changes are still managed and controlled, that procedures allow rapid but controlled change to fix software errors, and that unscheduled changes are reviewed as soon as practical afterward. This is the emergency path this Implementation describes.
  • Guidance Adapting Zero Trust Principles to Operational Technology, 29 April 2026, from CISA, the Department of War and the Department of Energy with partners. The Configuration and Change Management section asks that changes, including updates to zero trust policies, follow a formalized Management of Change process, typically with a multi-disciplinary review board that includes engineering peer review and safety review. This bears on routing changes to SI systems that act on operational technology through that existing process, as this Implementation suggests.
